## 1. The Dakota War of 1862 (S49; gate: wars against Native nations)

### 1.1 Causes

- **Treaties.** Traverse des Sioux (23 July 1851, Sisseton and Wahpeton) and Mendota (5 August 1851, Mdewakanton and Wahpekute) ceded the bands' Minnesota and Iowa lands for $1,665,000 and $1,410,000, most of it held in trust at 5% for fifty years and paid as annuities; the Senate struck the reservation article of the first and amended the second's. 10 Stat. 949, 954. **H** [A-STAT10]. The "Traders' Paper" of 1851 diverted part of the cash to traders, Sibley among them (snippet, **L**) [A-S-MNHS-TRADERS]. The 1858 treaties were not read at the primary level; Heard (1863) says the money for the 1858 cession reached the Dakota only four years later and mostly went to traders (**M** for what Heard says, **L** as fact) [A-HEARD pp. 42–43].
- **Hunger.** Cutworms destroyed the Sisseton corn in 1861 and a late snow delayed the spring hunts; about 1,500 people needed extra support and "some died from starvation" (Heard). **M** [A-HEARD pp. 42–43].
- **The late annuity, 1862.** About 6,000 people camped at the Upper Agency from 14 July waiting for payment; on 4 August they broke into the government warehouse in front of about 100 soldiers, and a council and an issue of food ended it. **M** [A-HEARD pp. 46–47]. When the money arrived is disputed: it "did not leave St. Paul until the 17th day of August" (a Northern Superintendency report, snippet) or reached St. Paul on 16 August and Fort Ridgely on the 17th (Wikipedia, pointer); the war began on the 18th. **L** [A-S-NSUPT; A-WIKI-DAKOTA]. Why it was late: the Interior Secretary's report blamed "the insurrection of the southern States" (snippet, **L**) [A-S-SMITH]. Lincoln told Congress on 1 December 1862 that "How this outbreak was induced is not definitely known, and suspicions, which may be unjust, need not be stated." **H** [A-NH8 pp. 107–108].
- **Credit refused, and Myrick.** From about 1 July the traders refused credit against the coming payment (Heard). **M** [A-HEARD pp. 48–49]. The earliest source for Andrew Myrick's remark is Little Crow's letter to Sibley of 7 September 1862: Myrick "told the Indians that they would eat grass or dirt" (Heard's print) or "grass or their own dung" (the Adjutant General's report as quoted by Wikipedia). The usual full sentence comes from a 1920 book; Gary Clayton Anderson ("Myrick's Insult", *Minnesota History*, 1983, not read) reads the context differently. **The sourcing is disputed**: a Dakota leader's second-hand report in a letter to the enemy commander. **M** for the letter, **L** for the quoted sentence [A-HEARD pp. 147–148; A-WIKI-DAKOTA].
- **The spark.** At Acton on 17 August four or five young men from Shakopee's band killed five settlers after a quarrel; on 18 August the band's leaders went to Little Crow, who agreed to lead ("I am with you", per Heard), and the first shots were fired at Myrick's store at the Lower Agency between six and seven in the morning. **M** [A-HEARD pp. 52–62].

### 1.2 The Dakota who opposed the war, and the dead

- Wabasha and Taopi wrote privately to Sibley on 10 September 1862 that they had been "kept back by threats that I should be killed if I did anything to help the whites" and offered to bring the captives in; Sibley mentions the note on 13 September. **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 631–632; A-HEARD pp. 149–150]. Heard names John Other Day, Paul Mazakutemani, Lorenzo Lawrence, Simon, Robert Hopkins and others as Dakota who saved settlers. **M** [A-HEARD pp. 150–151, 186–187]. Sibley's Wood Lake report: "serious dissension in the Indian camp, many having been opposed to the war but driven into the field by the more violent." **H** [A-OR-I-13 p. 279].
- **Settler and civilian deaths (no official count; every figure is kept):** "several hundred" (Ramsey to Stanton, 27 August); "More than 500" (Ramsey to Lincoln, 6 September); "not less than eight hundred" (Lincoln's annual message, 1 December, from the Interior Department; Whipple also says 800); "nearly or quite one thousand" (Minnesota's senator and two representatives to Lincoln) and "more than one thousand" (Senator Wilkinson, 5 December); 358 civilians (modern count cited by Wikipedia to Wingerd); 400–800 (press summary, snippet). The crisis figures come from interested officials. **H** for who said what; the true number is **L** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 599, 617; A-NH8 p. 108; A-SED7 p. 3; A-GLOBE p. 13; A-WIKI-DAKOTA; A-S-STRIB].
- **Soldiers and Dakota:** 77 soldiers, 36 armed civilians and about 150 Dakota killed in battle (Clodfelter via Wikipedia, pointer, **L**). At Wood Lake (23 September) Sibley reported 4 killed and 35–40 wounded on his side and about 30 Dakota dead, 14 found. **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 278–281].

### 1.3 The state and the Union respond

- 26–27 August: Governor Ramsey asked for a month's stay of the draft ("Half the population of the State are fugitives"); Lincoln, 27 August: "Attend to the Indians. If the draft cannot proceed of course it will not proceed. Necessity knows no law. The Government cannot extend the time." Ramsey to Stanton the same day: "As against the Sioux it must be a war of extermination." **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 597–599].
- 6 September: Ramsey to Lincoln, "This is not our war; it is a national war." The War Department's order of 6 September (General Orders No. 128, 7 September) made the Department of the Northwest (Wisconsin, Iowa, Minnesota, Nebraska, Dakota) under Pope, at St. Paul, leaving measures "to your judgment and discretion." Pope assumed command on 16 September. **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 617–618, 642].
- 28 September, Pope to Sibley: "It is my purpose utterly to exterminate the Sioux if I have the power to do so ... They are to be treated as maniacs or wild beasts, and by no means as people with whom treaties or compromises can be made." OR I, 13, p. 686. **H**, page re-checked by the lead [A-OR-I-13; L-OR13]. No evidence it went to Lincoln.
- Camp Release, 26 September: Sibley reported "91 pure whites" and over 100 people of mixed descent delivered by the camp that opposed the war; Wikipedia gives 269 (107 white, 162 mixed). **H** for Sibley's figure; conflict kept [A-OR-I-13 pp. 679–680; A-WIKI-DAKOTA].

### 1.4 The military commission

- Sibley appointed a commission of officers at Camp Release about 27–28 September and wrote that executions "will be forthwith" if men were found guilty, "although perhaps it will be a stretch of my authority. If so, necessity must be my justification." **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 680, 687–688]. Members (named in Lincoln's order): Colonel Crooks, Lieutenant Colonel Marshall, Captains Grant and Bailey, Lieutenant Olin; the missionary Stephen Riggs gathered the charges. **H** [A-SED7 p. 6; A-HEARD pp. 251–252].
- **Numbers.** Heard, the commission's recorder: "over four hundred" tried, 303 sentenced to death, 18 to prison, "nearly one quarter" acquitted; up to forty cases a day, "five minutes would dispose of a case." Lincoln (1 December) and Pope (8–10 November) both say 300 condemned. Wikipedia gives 392 tried and 303 or 307 condemned. **M** (participant source) [A-HEARD pp. 254–255, 267; A-NH8 p. 92; A-OR-I-13 p. 787; A-WIKI-MANKATO].
- Sibley, 15 October: "There are doubtless some innocent men in the number I have secured in fetters, but there is no time to examine so large a number." **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 740–741]. The modern legal critique (Chomsky, *Stanford Law Review*, 1990: no counsel, cases of minutes) was not read. **L**.

### 1.5 Lincoln's review: the count, settled from his own message

- 8–10 November: Pope wired the names of about 300 condemned; Lincoln, 10 November: "forward as soon as possible the full and complete record of their convictions; and if the record does not fully indicate the more guilty and influential of the culprits, please have a careful statement made on these points." **H** [A-OR-I-13 p. 787]. Ramsey (10 November) asked for "the execution of every Sioux Indian condemned"; Pope (11 November) wrote that if all were not executed "I think it nearly impossible to prevent the indiscriminate massacre of all the Indians." **H** [A-OR-I-13 pp. 787–788].
- 1 December: Lincoln asked Judge Advocate General Holt whether, if he executed only part, he must name them himself or could leave it to an officer on the ground. **H** [A-NH8 p. 92]. Two lawyers, George Whiting and Francis Ruggles, sifted the records and listed "those who were convicted of rape and murder" (5 December). **H** [A-SED7 pp. 7–9]. The Senate asked for the records on 5 December (Wilkinson's resolution). **H** [A-GLOBE p. 13].
- **Lincoln's message to the Senate, 11 December 1862 (Senate Ex. Doc. 7, 37th Congress, 3rd session):** he looked first for those "proved guilty of violating females. Contrary to my expectations, only two of this class were found. I then directed a further examination, and a classification of all who were proven to have participated in massacres, as distinguished from participation in battles. This class numbered forty, and included the two convicted of female violation. One of the number is strongly recommended, by the commission which tried them, for commutation to ten years' imprisonment. I have ordered the other thirty-nine to be executed on Friday, the 19th instant." The order, dated 6 December, was sent on Monday 8 December; it names 39. The one set aside was Godfrey (O-ta-kla), a man of African descent, whose testimony the commission credited. **H**, the passage re-checked by the lead [A-SED7 pp. 1–2, 6–9; A-NH8 pp. 133–142; L-SED7].
- 16 December: Lincoln moved the date to Friday 26 December at Sibley's suggestion. **H** [A-NH8 pp. 144–145]. About 23 December he reprieved one more, Tatemima (Round Wind, No. 15), on new doubt about his guilt (Sibley's telegram per Wikipedia; Thomas Williamson's alibi per the Star Tribune, snippet). **M** for who, **L** for why.
- **38 hanged together at Mankato, 26 December 1862.** **H** [A-HEARD pp. 272–295; A-NH8]. So the count is: about 303 condemned; 40 classed; 1 commuted on the commission's recommendation; 39 ordered; 1 reprieved; 38 hanged. Heard prints the date as "26th day of February, 1863", a misprint against Lincoln's telegram [I].
- **Mistaken identity.** No. 121, "Chaskay-don or Chaskay-etay", is in the order; Wikipedia (citing Anderson and the *New York Times*, 2010) says another man called Chaska, We-chank-wash-ta-don-pee (case No. 3, not in the order), was hanged in his place; Sarah Wakefield believed it deliberate. **M** that No. 3 was not ordered; **L** for the rest [A-SED7 pp. 6, 8; A-WIKI-CHASKA].
- **Later, outside 1862:** Shakopee and Medicine Bottle, taken in Canada, were hanged at Fort Snelling on 11 November 1865 (Wikipedia only, **L**) [A-WIKI-MANKATO]. Ramsey's diary for 23 November 1864 reportedly has Lincoln saying "I could not afford to hang men for votes" (Donald, via Wikipedia; snippet, **L**) [A-S-RAMSEY-DIARY].

### 1.6 Aftermath: the camp, the prison, the acts of 1863

- About 1,600–1,700 non-combatants were held at Fort Snelling from 13 November 1862; deaths that winter are given as 130–300 ("nearly 300" in the MNHS FAQ). Snippet only, **L**; the one primary letter read (Olin, 26 May 1863) says "a considerable number of deaths" without a figure. [A-CIA1863 p. 313; A-S-MNHS-FAQ]. About 120 of some 280 men held at Davenport died (MNHS FAQ, snippet, **L**).
- **Act of 16 February 1863** (12 Stat. 652): the Dakota bands "have forfeited all just claim to the said moneys and annuities"; their treaties abrogated as to future obligations, their Minnesota lands and annuities forfeited; up to $200,000 of the annuities turned to settlers' damages, at most $200 per family. **H** [A-STAT12].
- **Act of 21 February 1863** (12 Stat. 658): removal of the Ho-Chunk (Winnebago) from Minnesota, who had not made the war; 1,930 left by 27 May 1863. **H** [A-STAT12; A-CIA1863 pp. 312–313].
- **Act of 3 March 1863** (12 Stat. 819): the president "authorized and hereby directed" to remove the four Dakota bands beyond any state; 80 acres allowed to any Dakota "who exerted himself to save the lives of the whites." **H** [A-STAT12]. About 1,300 Dakota were taken to Crow Creek in May 1863 (pointer, **L**).

### 1.7 What reached Lincoln, in order (for S49)

1. 26–27 August: the draft stay (refused) and Ramsey's request for a department and Harney. 2. 6 September: "a national war"; Pope sent. 3. 13–14 October: Pope asks whether he needs authority to execute and proposes disarming the annuity Indians; Stanton answers that the matters are "under consideration" by the president, who wants the Indian Department's view first. 4. 21 October: Sibley suspends executions "until the pleasure of the President is known." 5. 8–11 November: 300 names; Lincoln asks for records; Ramsey and Pope demand all be hanged and warn of mob violence. 6. Autumn (undated memoir): Bishop Whipple, introduced by Halleck, reports corruption in the Indian system; Lincoln: "If we get through this war, and I live, this Indian system shall be reformed." **M** [A-WHIPPLE pp. 136–139]. 7. 1 December: the question to Holt. 8. Late November to 5 December: the Minnesota delegation protests a reported plan to pardon most ("We do not wish to see mob law inaugurated in Minnesota, as it certainly will be, if you force the people to it"), and the Senate calls for the records. 9. 6–26 December: order, message, postponement, reprieve, hanging. **H** for 1–5 and 7–9 [A-OR-I-13 pp. 597–599, 617–618, 733, 737, 757, 787–788; A-SED7 pp. 2–4; A-GLOBE p. 13; A-NH8].

- **Advisers on record:** for executing all, Pope, Ramsey, Sibley's officers and the Minnesota delegation; for clemency, Whipple, Eastern clergy and a Quaker committee (named in Wilkinson's speech). Interior Secretary Caleb Smith and Commissioner Dole left no recommendation in what was read; Dole told the Senate on 8 December that his office had nothing beyond his annual report. Lincoln's own options as the record shows them: execute all; pardon most; hang only those convicted of rape (two); hang those in massacres as distinct from battles (his choice, forty); or leave the choice to an officer (his question to Holt). **H** for the record, [I] for the list of options.

## 2. Indian Territory, 1861–62 (catalogue gap 22; gate: wars against Native nations, slavery)

### 2.1 The Union withdrawal and the annuities (1861)

- **Orders.** By order of the general-in-chief, Scott, Emory was told on 18 March 1861 to concentrate the garrisons of Forts Cobb, Arbuckle and Washita ("The interests of the United States are paramount to those of the friendly Indians on the reservation near Fort Cobb, but good faith requires that notice shall be given them"), and on 17 April to march "all the troops in the Indian country west of Arkansas" to Fort Leavenworth. Scott's memorandum of 27 March called Arbuckle "of no importance as a military point". Officers on the spot appealed against abandoning Arbuckle; Harney forwarded the appeals with his dissent (13 April). Emory left Washita about 18–19 April; Texans occupied Washita the next day and Arbuckle on 5 May; Emory reached Kansas with 750 fighting men. **No document shows Lincoln or Cameron approving the withdrawal.** **H** [B-OR1 pp. 648–667].
- **Dole's account** (annual report, 27 November 1861): the Indian Office had promised the chiefs non-interference with their institutions and asked the War Department for troops, which it was "unable to furnish"; defection followed because the forts were "basely surrendered" and the troops withdrawn, and because slaveholders feared the administration. **H** [B-CIA61 pp. 626–628].
- **Annuities.** Dole: "withheld, not, as has been falsely represented, with a view to their confiscation", but to keep them from rebel hands. Pike: withheld "to keep them loyal", and a check for part of the Choctaws' money stopped "that the fear of loss might prevent an alliance". **H** that both wrote this; the motive is disputed [B-CIA61 p. 628; B-DAVIS].

### 2.2 The Confederate treaties

- Albert Pike, Confederate commissioner (appointed March 1861), made nine treaties: **Creek, 10 July 1861** (North Fork; the council voted to join after Opothleyahola withdrew); **Choctaw and Chickasaw, 12 July**; **Seminole, 1 August**; **two at the Wichita Agency on 12 August**, with four Comanche bands of the prairies and with the "Reserve Indians" (Penateka Comanches, Wichitas, Caddos, Anadarkos, Tonkawas and others, about 2,500 people); **Great Osage, 2 October**; **Quapaw, 4 October**; **Seneca and Shawnee, 4 October**; **Cherokee, 7 October** (at Park Hill; other sources say Tahlequah, three miles off). **H** [B-DAVIS; B-CSA].
- **Terms** (Pike's summary; the treaty texts were not read): the Confederacy assumed the federal annuities and arrears (against its own instructions: "without such assumption no treaty could be made"); title in fee simple; a delegate each in the Confederate Congress for the Choctaw–Chickasaw, Creek–Seminole and Cherokee nations; Confederate district courts; possible statehood for the Choctaw–Chickasaw country; in return, a protectorate, Confederate intercourse laws and troops (two Cherokee, one Creek and one Choctaw regiment in service by October). Davis asked the Confederate Congress to strike the statehood and testimony clauses and to soften the delegate clauses. **H** for Pike's and Davis's texts [B-DAVIS]. The Confederate War Secretary's instruction was to tell the nations the North "would undoubtedly free their slaves and seize their lands." **H** [B-DAVIS].
- **Slavery in the settlement:** no slavery article appears in Pike's summary; the Confederate act of 8 April 1862 extended its fugitive-slave laws into the Indian country; the Cherokee meeting of 21 August 1861 resolved "we distinctly recognize that of property in negro slaves". **H** for each [B-CSA; B-OR13 pp. 499–500].
- **The Cherokee path:** Ross refused Arkansas's invitation (22 February 1861: treaties bound them to the United States, though their "institutions, locality, and natural sympathies are unequivocally with the slave-holding States"); proclaimed "unswerving neutrality" (17 May); refused McCulloch Confederate home guards (17 June); the national meeting at Tahlequah on 21 August approved neutrality and left alliance to the authorities, and Ross's council message of 9 October said "geographical position and domestic institutions allied us to the South". Stand Watie was a Confederate colonel by 1 September. **H** [B-OR13 pp. 489–503; B-OR3 pp. 596–597, 690–691].

### 2.3 The loyalist exodus and the refugees in Kansas

- Opothleyahola's Creeks and Seminoles, with Black Creeks and freedom-seekers, were attacked by Cooper's Confederate Indian and Texan force at **Round Mountain (19 November 1861)**, **Chusto-Talasah (9 December)** and **Chustenahlah (26 December)**; Cooper put the loyalists at "800 to 1,200 Creeks and Seminoles and 200 to 300 negroes" at the first fight and "certainly over 2,500" at the second, and noted a Cherokee regiment deserting to them. Confederate reports only. **H** for what Cooper wrote, **M** for events [B-OR8 pp. 5–14, 712].
- **Refugees:** "There are now 6,000 women and children in Southern Kansas without tents ... Many of them have frozen to death" (the Creek and Seminole chiefs to the president, 28 January 1862, forwarded by John Hay to McClellan); 6,000–8,000 (Dole); about 7,000 (the Interior Secretary); 7,600 in the Neosho camps in late March (Collamore); 5,487 on 1 October 1862 plus nearly 2,000 Cherokees newly arrived (Coffin). No source read gives the "9,000" sometimes quoted. **H** for each figure [B-OR8 pp. 534–535; B-CIA62 pp. 3, 26, 136–137, 155–157].
- **Deaths:** 240 Creeks dead within two months of arriving and over a hundred frostbitten limbs amputated (Dole, Collamore); "fully ten per cent." of those who reached the camps dead (Coffin, his estimate). **H** [B-CIA62 pp. 27, 136, 156–157]. Relief: the army fed them until Hunter said he could not past 15 February 1862; Dole bought on credit with the Interior Secretary's approval; about $193,000 by late 1862, paid from the annuities withheld from the southern tribes. **H** [B-CIA62 pp. 27–28, 137].

### 2.4 The Union response in 1862

- **The "southern expedition" (December 1861–February 1862):** Lane, nominated brigadier by Lincoln, won approval for an expedition with 8,000–10,000 Kansans and "4,000 Indians" under Hunter's control (Thomas, 24 January); Hunter protested that it was "sanctioned by the President under misrepresentations". Stanton, new in office, would not enlist Indians; Interior Secretary Smith to Dole, 11 February: "President cant attend to business now. Sickness in the family"; 14 February: "War Department will not organize them." **H** for the OR texts, **M** for the telegrams (quoted by Abel) [B-OR8 pp. 482, 525, 829–830; B-ABEL].
- **19 March 1862:** "It is the desire of the President, on the application of the Secretary of the Interior and the Commissioner of Indian Affairs", that Halleck detail two regiments to open the way home for the refugees, and "Five thousand friendly Indians will also be armed". Halleck: Indians "can be used only against Indians or in defense of their own territory". **H** [B-OR8 pp. 624–625, 665–666].
- **The Indian Expedition (Weer), July 1862:** three Indian Home Guard regiments with white troops; Locust Grove, 3 July (about 30 killed and 100 captured, by Weer's count); on 12 July Weer proposed inviting the Cherokees to abolish slavery with compensation and asking Lincoln to have Congress extend the compensated-emancipation resolution to the Indian nations; on 19 July Blunt ordered him to accept recruits "without reference to color". **H** [B-OR13 pp. 137–138, 487–489]. On 15 July a detachment took the officers of the Cherokee regiment at Ross's house prisoner and left Ross "a prisoner of war ... on his parole"; on 18 or 19 July Colonel Salomon arrested Weer and the white troops retreated, leaving the Indian regiments and nearly 2,000 more refugees. **H** [B-OR13 pp. 160–162, 475–476, 484–485, 511–512; B-CIA62 pp. 27–28].
- **Ross and Lincoln:** Blunt sent Ross to Washington (13 August: "no doubt as to the loyalty of the Ross family and three-fourths of the Cherokee people"). Ross wrote to Lincoln on 16 September; Lincoln's reply of 25 September is known only from catalogue snippets (no ruling on treaty status; the Cherokees "remaining practically loyal" to receive the protection "which can be given them consistently with the duty of the Government"). **L**. On 10 October Lincoln asked Curtis whether the Cherokee regiments could reoccupy the Cherokee country; Curtis doubted "ground so remote from supplies"; on 14 October Curtis passed on that "The President also desires Ross re-established". **H** [B-OR13 pp. 565–566, 723–724, 736; B-S-LINCROSS].
- **Lincoln's annual message, 1 December 1862:** the tribes south of Kansas "renounced their allegiance"; the loyal "were driven from the country"; Ross "alleges that they were constrained by superior force ... and that the United States neglected to furnish the protection which their treaty stipulations required." **H** [B-LINC62].

### 2.5 The law Congress gave the president

- **Act of 5 July 1862** (Indian appropriations, 12 Stat. 512, proviso at 528): appropriations for tribes "in a state of actual hostility", naming the Cherokees, Creeks, Choctaws, Chickasaws, Seminoles and Wichitas, "may and shall be suspended and postponed ... at and during the discretion and pleasure of the President", and spent instead on members "driven from their homes ... on account of their friendship to the government"; where the tribal organisation is hostile, the president may "by proclamation, ... declare all treaties with such tribe to be abrogated by such tribe, if, in his opinion, the same can be done consistently with good faith". **H** [B-STAT12]. **No abrogation proclamation was found**; the 1866 treaties speak of liability to forfeiture, not of a completed abrogation. **M** [B-STAT14].
- Advice: Superintendent Coffin (1 September 1862) wanted new treaties but wrote that "the government first wholly failed to keep its treaty stipulations ... by withdrawing all the troops from the forts"; Dole put the whole question to Congress. **H** [B-CIA62 pp. 28–29, 167–168].

### 2.6 Black people in Indian Territory

- **1860 census:** 7,369 enslaved people among the Choctaws (2,297), Cherokees (2,504), Creeks (1,651) and Chickasaws (917), about 12.5% of those nations; the Seminoles held none but intermarried with free Black people. **H** (two census printings) [B-CENSUS60P pp. 10–11; B-CENSUS60 pp. xv–xvi]. Other estimates (8,000–10,000 for the whole territory, OHS; about 4,000 Cherokee-held, Wikipedia) are snippets. **L**.
- Black men served with Opothleyahola (Cooper's 200–300). That African Creeks and Seminoles served in the First Indian Home Guard rests on a BlackPast snippet citing Zellar (*Chronicles of Oklahoma*). **L** [B-OR8 p. 6; B-S-BLACKPAST].

### 2.7 Decision points that reached Lincoln or his cabinet

1. March–April 1861: the withdrawal (Scott; no Lincoln document). 2. 1861: annuities withheld (Dole, Interior). 3. ## 3. Emancipation in the District of Columbia, compensation and colonization, 1862 (S33; a candidate DC situation; gate: slavery and emancipation)

### 3.1 The District of Columbia act, 16 April 1862

- **Citation and passage:** "An Act for the Release of certain Persons held to Service or Labor in the District of Columbia", ch. 54, 12 Stat. 376–378, approved 16 April 1862. Senate bill No. 108 (Henry Wilson, introduced 16 December 1861); Senate passage 3 April 1862, 29–14 (Globe 37–2, p. 1526; the nays were border-state and Democratic senators); House passage 11 April, 92–39 on the previous question and **92–38 on passage**, without amendment, Stevens managing (pp. 1648–1649). **H** [C-STAT-DC; C-GLOBE-1; C-GLOBE-2].
- **Terms:** everyone held "by reason of African descent" freed and slavery barred in the District (sec. 1); loyal claimants to petition within ninety days, "but not thereafter", swearing they had not aided the rebellion (sec. 2); three presidential commissioners to appraise, the total not to exceed **$300 for each person** (sec. 3); witnesses not excluded "on account of color" (sec. 5); appropriation "not exceeding one million of dollars" (sec. 7); kidnapping or re-enslaving the freed a felony, five to twenty years (sec. 8); **$100,000 for voluntary colonization** of free Black residents to Haiti, Liberia or another country the president chooses, at most **$100 per emigrant** (sec. 11). **H** [C-STAT-DC].
- **Lincoln's message on signing:** "I have never doubted the constitutional authority of Congress to abolish slavery in this District ... Hence there has never been in my mind any question on the subject except the one of expediency"; "I am gratified that the two principles of compensation and colonization are both recognized and practically applied in the act"; his one stated reservation was that the ninety-day bar had "no saving for minors, femmes covert, insane or absent persons". **H** [C-LAPSLEY5]. That he had wanted a referendum and a delay rests on a *New York Times* report cited by Wikipedia (**L**).
- **Opposition:** a memorial from Washington's aldermen and council against "unqualified abolition" at present, lest the city become "an asylum for free negroes" (Senate, 2 April); border-state and Democratic votes in both chambers. **H** [C-GLOBE-2].
- **Supplementary act, 12 July 1862** (ch. 155, 12 Stat. 538–539): guardians and agents may swear for minors and absentees; **if an owner fails to file the schedule, "the person or persons, whose services are claimed" may file it themselves**; people brought into the District with their owners' consent since 16 April are freed; no witness excluded by colour in any District court. **H** [C-STAT-SUPP].
- **Results:** commissioners Daniel Goodloe, Horatio King and (after Vinton's death) John Brodhead (**M**). About 966 petitions; about 930 approved; people freed with compensation 2,989 (National Archives and Senate pages, snippets), 2,981 (civilwardc.org, snippet), 2,991 (snippet), 3,185 (Wikipedia), "almost 3,000" (Britannica); no source for the brief's 3,100. Total paid: "nearly $900,000" (snippet) against "almost all of the $1 million" (Wikipedia); 2,989 × $300 = $896,700 [I]. The commissioners' final report was not read. **L** for all totals [C-S-DCFIG; C-S-DCFIG2; C-WP-DC; C-WP-EC]. The Globe shows a Black Washingtonian, "George White, colored", claiming $613.20 for people he had bought free with his own earnings. **H** [C-GLOBE-4 p. 885].

### 3.2 The compensated-emancipation offer of March 1862 (S33)

- **The proposal's text** (message of 6 March 1862): "Resolved, That the United States ought to co-operate with any State which may adopt gradual abolishment of slavery, giving to such State pecuniary aid, to be used by such State, in its discretion, to compensate for the inconveniences, public and private, produced by such change of system." The message said it "sets up no claim of a right by Federal authority to interfere with slavery within State limits", and that the war's cost would soon "purchase, at fair valuation, all the slaves in any named State". **H** [C-LAPSLEY5; C-NH5]. Lincoln costed it at $400 a head (to Raymond, 9 March; to McDougall, 14 March, in 6% bonds over twenty years). **M** [C-NH5].
- **Passage:** House 11 March, 89–31; Senate 2 April, 32–10; signed 10 April as Joint Resolution No. 26, 12 Stat. 617 ("inconvenience", singular, in the statute). **H** [C-GLOBE-2 pp. 1179, 1496; C-STAT-RES26].
- **The meeting of 10 March:** border-state members at the White House, invited through Montgomery Blair; John Crisfield's same-day memorandum, certified by Menzies, Crittenden and Mallory: Lincoln said the offer was "in good faith", voluntary, emancipation "exclusively under the control of the States"; he refused to let Crisfield publish his assurance that "as long as he remained there Maryland had nothing to fear" ("it would force me into a quarrel before the proper time"); he said slavery was wrong and existed "as well by the act of the North as of the South". **H** [C-LAPSLEY5].
- **The appeal of 12 July 1862:** "Break that lever before their faces, and they can shake you no more forever"; "I do not speak of emancipation at once, but of a decision at once to emancipate gradually"; of the Hunter order, "I repudiated the proclamation" but "the pressure in this direction is still upon me, and is increasing." **H** [C-LAPSLEY6]. **Replies, 14 July:** a majority of twenty (Kentucky, Missouri, Maryland and Carlile of Virginia) declined; a minority of seven (Noell, Casey, Clements, Fisher, Willey, W. G. Brown, J. B. Blair) promised to put it to their people; Henderson and Maynard wrote separately. **M** (Nicolay and Hay; the replies themselves not read) [C-NH6].
- **Cabinet advice on the proposal: none found.** The catalogue's staff line for S33 stays unsourced. Delaware's own compensated-emancipation bill, Lincoln's plan, had failed in February 1862 (**M**) [C-NH5].

### 3.3 Colonization in 1862

- **Money:** the District act's $100,000 (sec. 11); and **$500,000 in the supplemental appropriation act of 16 July 1862** (ch. 182, 12 Stat. 582), "to colonize those to be made free by the probable passage of a confiscation bill", repayable from confiscated property, at the president's discretion. The Second Confiscation Act's section 12 authorised colonization "in some tropical country" with the host's consent but **appropriated nothing** (the brief's attribution of the $500,000 to that act is corrected). Both appropriations were repealed on 2 July 1864 (13 Stat. 352). **H** [C-STAT-DC; C-STAT-APPROP; C-STAT-SCA; C-STAT-REPEAL].
- **Haiti and Liberia:** the act of 5 June 1862 (ch. 96, 12 Stat. 421) authorised diplomatic representatives to both. **H** [C-STAT-HAITI].
- **14 August 1862:** Lincoln received a committee of Black men introduced by the Reverend James Mitchell, commissioner of emigration, with Edward M. Thomas as chairman (the others are not named in the text read). From the newspaper report: "Your race are suffering, in my judgment, the greatest wrong inflicted on any people. But even when you cease to be slaves, you are yet far removed from being placed on an equality with the white race"; "without the institution of slavery and the colored race as a basis, the war could not have an existence. It is better for us both, therefore, to be separated"; he proposed Central America and asked for "a hundred tolerably intelligent men, with their wives and children ... Can I have fifty?" **H** [C-LAPSLEY6]. Frederick Douglass answered that Lincoln spoke like "an itinerant Colonization lecturer" (*Douglass' Monthly*, September 1862; snippet, **L**).
- **Chiriquí:** a preliminary contract with the grantee of lands on the Chiriquí lagoon; Senator Pomeroy's emigration party (snippets: accepted 28 August, about 500 recruits, sailing planned for 1 October); protests from Costa Rica, Nicaragua and Honduras and a Smithsonian report that the "coal" was lignite ended it. Ended **about 24 September** (snippets and Wikipedia) against the brief's "October". **M** for Nicolay and Hay's narrative, **L** for dates [C-NH6; C-S-CHIR; C-WP-LINC]. Lincoln's annual message: several Spanish American republics protested, so he had "declined to move any such colony to any state without first obtaining the consent of its government". **H** [C-LAPSLEY6].
- **Île à Vache:** a contract with Bernard Kock for 5,000 colonists at $50 a head, dated 31 December 1862 by Nicolay and Hay but "the 3rd day of December last" in Lincoln's own instrument of 16 April 1863 cancelling his signature; it was never sealed. About 450 sailed in April 1863; 378 of 431 survived to December; the survivors were brought back in March 1864 (outside the window). **H** for both texts [C-NH6; C-LAPSLEY6].
- **Annual message, 1 December 1862:** three proposed constitutional articles: federal bonds to any state abolishing slavery before 1 January 1900; "All slaves who shall have enjoyed actual freedom by the chances of the war at any time before the end of the rebellion shall be forever free"; and Congress may provide "for colonizing free colored persons with their own consent". "I cannot make it better known than it already is that I strongly favor colonization." **H** [C-LAPSLEY6].
- **Spending:** not found; in June 1864 Senator Wilson said "We have lost several thousand dollars on it", and two Senate requests for an account had gone unanswered. **H** for the remarks [C-GLOBE-5 pp. 3261–3262].

## 4. The pocket veto in 1862, and the acts of 17 July 1862 (S45; schema pressure P6)

### 4.1 The rule as it stood

- **The text** (Art. I, sec. 7, cl. 2): a bill not returned "within ten Days (Sundays excepted) after it shall have been presented to him, the Same shall be a Law, in like Manner as if he had signed it, unless the Congress by their Adjournment prevent its Return, in which Case it shall not be a Law." **H** [C-CONST].
- **Practice before 1862:** Madison's first pocket veto (naturalization bill, passed July 1812, explained in his message of 5 November 1812: presented "at an hour too near the close of the session to be returned with objections ... the bill failed to become a law"); Jackson's seven pocket vetoes, with his reasons sent to the next session. **H** for the messages [C-RICH1; C-RICH3; C-WP-VETO].
- **What 1862 did not yet have:** no Attorney General opinion on the pocket veto before 1863 was found. The questions later courts settled were open: whether an adjournment other than the final one prevents return (the *Pocket Veto Case*, 1929: yes, a recess between sessions can); whether the president may sign after Congress adjourns (*Edwards v. United States*, 1932: yes, within the ten days); whether one chamber's short recess prevents return (*Wright v. United States*, 1938: no). **H** for what each case decided; [I] that the points were unsettled in 1862 [C-WP-PVC; C-WP-EDW; C-WP-WRIGHT; C-S-POCKET].
- **Lincoln's practice:** on 17 July 1862 he signed the Second Confiscation Act and many other bills the same day Congress adjourned sine die; that he was at the Capitol that night is **not** documented (it is documented for 4 July 1864, when he sat in his Capitol room signing bills and let the Wade–Davis bill, presented "less than one hour before the sine die adjournment", go unsigned). **H** for 1864, **M** for 1862 [C-GLOBE-3; C-NH9; C-LAPSLEY7].
- **Lincoln's vetoes:** two regular vetoes in 1862 (23 June, small bank notes in the District; 2 July, additional medical officers, override failed 0–37) and pocket vetoes later (one in 1863, Wade–Davis and another in 1864, two in 1865), from Wikipedia's list, whose dates for the 1863 and 1864 pocket vetoes do not fit the calendar. **M** for the 1862 vetoes (partly confirmed in the Globe index), **L** for the list's dates [C-WP-VETO; C-GLOBE-2].
- [I] **For the schema (P6):** during a session, an unsigned bill becomes law after ten days, Sundays excepted; a bill reaching the president in the last ten days before a sine die adjournment dies if unsigned; whether he could sign it after adjournment was untested, and Lincoln's practice was to sign on the last day.

### 4.2 The Second Confiscation Act and the Militia Act (S45)

- **Second Confiscation Act** (ch. 195, 12 Stat. 589–592, 17 July 1862): treason punishable by death or by prison and fine, with the freeing of the offender's slaves (sec. 1); seizure of the property of Confederate officers and officials (sec. 5) and, after sixty days' warning, of other supporters (sec. 6); **sec. 9**: slaves of rebels escaping to Union lines, captured, deserted, or found in places Union forces occupy "shall be deemed captives of war, and shall be forever free of their servitude"; sec. 10: no fugitive returned unless the claimant swears loyalty, and no officer to decide claims; **sec. 11**: the president "is authorized to employ as many persons of African descent as he may deem necessary and proper for the suppression of this rebellion ... in such manner as he may judge best"; sec. 12: colonization, without money. **H** for secs. 9–14, **M** for the rest [C-STAT-SCA].
- **Militia Act** (ch. 201, 12 Stat. 597–600): militia service up to nine months; **sec. 12**: the president may receive "persons of African descent" for labour "or any military or naval service for which they may be found competent"; **sec. 13**: one who serves, if owned by a rebel, is freed with his mother, wife and children (if also owned by rebels); **sec. 15**: Black men employed under it "shall receive ten dollars per month and one ration, three dollars of which monthly pay may be in clothing". The white private's pay to compare it with was not sourced in this pass (UNVERIFIED). **H** [C-STAT-MIL].
- **The explanatory resolution and the veto message:** Joint Resolution No. 63 (12 Stat. 627) confined section 5 to acts after its passage, exempted state legislators and judges who had not sworn to the Confederate constitution, and barred forfeiture of real estate "beyond his natural life". Lincoln's message of 17 July: "Considering the bill ... and the joint resolution explanatory of said act as being substantially one, I have approved and signed both. Before I was informed of the passage of the resolution I had prepared the draft of a message stating objections ... a copy of which draft is herewith transmitted." The draft objected chiefly to forfeiture beyond the offender's life as unconstitutional ("no attainder of treason shall work corruption of blood or forfeiture except during the life of the person attainted"), asked a power of remission ("A man without hope is a most dangerous man"), and said of section 11: "I have no hesitation to go as far in the direction indicated". Congress adjourned sine die that day. **H** [C-STAT-RES63; C-LAPSLEY6; C-GLOBE-3].
- **Bates:** Lincoln's order of 13 November 1862 put the Attorney General in charge of seizures under sections 5–7. **H** [C-LAPSLEY6]. No recorded position of Bates on either act before signing was found, so the catalogue's S45 staff line stays unsourced; Orville Browning, not Bates, is the adviser the secondary literature records as thinking the act unconstitutional (snippet, **L**) [C-S-BATES].

## 5. Prisoners: exchange before and under the cartel (gate: prison camps; catalogue gap 10)

### 5.1 Before the cartel (1861 to July 1862)

- **The privateers and the hostages.** The crew of the privateer *Savannah*, taken in June 1861, was tried for piracy in New York; the jury was discharged without a verdict on 31 October 1861. William Smith of the *Jefferson Davis* and three of her crew were convicted of piracy at Philadelphia in October 1861. **H** [D-WARB; D-OR2-3 pp. 143–144]. Davis had warned Lincoln on 6 July 1861 that Union prisoners would get "the same treatment and the same fate" as the *Savannah*'s men. **H** [D-OR2-3 pp. 5–6]. On 9 November 1861 Benjamin ordered Winder to choose by lot one Union officer of the highest rank to be "held for execution in the same manner as may be adopted by the enemy for the execution of ... Smith", and thirteen more to be held as felons; the lot, drawn on 10 November, fell first on Colonel Michael Corcoran of the 69th New York. **H** [D-OR2-3 pp. 130–132, 738–739].
- **How it ended:** on 31 January 1862 Seward had the prisoners charged with piracy moved from the marshals to Fort Lafayette, and on 13 February Wool told Huger they were held "only in military custody and on the same footing as the other prisoners taken in arms"; the Confederates then returned the hostages to ordinary status (Cobb, 28 February), and in May about 85 privateersmen were exchanged against them. Stanton, 4 May: the privateers were moved "for the express and sole purpose of removing them from the action of the civil tribunals". **H** [D-OR2-3 pp. 229, 259–260, 339, 514–515].
- **Local exchanges.** Field commanders arranged their own: Pillow and Wallace (August 1861), Grant and Polk (from September), Frémont and Price (October; Price also proposed that the war be "conducted exclusively by and between the armies in the field"), Wool and Huger swapping individuals in late 1861. Halleck, 3 December 1861: "This exchange is a mere military convention. A prisoner exchanged under the laws of war is not thereby exempted from trial and punishment as a traitor." **H** [D-OR2-1 pp. 548–562; D-OR2-3 pp. 150–151, 156, 164].
- **Congress.** A joint resolution adopted by the House held that exchange "has already been practiced indirectly" and "does not involve a recognition of the rebels as a government", and asked the president "to inaugurate systematic measures for the exchange of prisoners". Dated 14 December 1861 in the OR OCR, 11 December in Wikipedia; whether the Senate concurred is not shown. **H** for the text, **L** for the date [D-OR2-3 p. 157; D-WP-DH].
- **Recognition.** No single Union document refusing a cartel was found. The documented concern: the February 1862 Ames–Fish mission called itself "purely an errand of mercy" lest it "inferentially aid a cherished object" of the rebellion; and Stanton's authority to Dix (12 July 1862) warned "against any recognition of the rebel Government". **H** for the texts; the reading that recognition was the reason is secondary (**M**) [D-OR2-3 pp. 251–252; D-OR2-4 p. 174].
- **Wool–Cobb (23 February–4 March 1862).** They agreed on all but the Confederate demand that prisoners be paroled within ten days and delivered to their own lines at the captor's expense; Wool, with about 13,000 Fort Donelson prisoners in the North, said it exceeded his powers and was then limited to exchanging up to the smaller side's number. Cobb's report: once Donelson "gave to the United States the largest number of prisoners ... the United States changed their policy." Benjamin, 4 March: "Make no exchanges with General Wool." **H** for both sides' accounts; the Union side's own reasons were not found [D-OR2-3 pp. 259–260, 301–304, 334–342, 812–813].
- **Numbers.** The Union held about 20,500 Confederate prisoners on 24 July 1862 (Hoffman: Camp Douglas 7,800, Camp Morton 4,000, Camp Butler 2,000 and others). **H** [D-OR2-4 p. 277]. The Confederate-held total for mid-1862 was not found (2,600–2,700 had reached Richmond by November 1861). UNVERIFIED.

### 5.2 The Dix–Hill cartel, 22 July 1862

- Authorised on the Union side by Stanton for the president (12 July: "The President directs me to say that he authorizes you to negotiate a general exchange of prisoners ... observing proper caution against any recognition of the rebel Government"); on the Confederate side by Lee, who named Cobb, then D. H. Hill. Signed by Dix and Hill at Haxall's Landing on the James on 22 July 1862. **H** [D-OR2-4 pp. 174, 232–233, 265–268].
- **Terms (nine articles):** all prisoners, privateers included, discharged; man for man and officer for officer on a scale of equivalents (commanding general or admiral 60 privates; major general 40; brigadier 20; colonel 15; lieutenant colonel 10; major 8; captain 6; lieutenant 4; non-commissioned officers 2); militia and civil rank not recognised; citizens held for disloyalty exchanged only for citizens; **parole within ten days** of capture, delivered at the captor's expense, the surplus barred from any military duty until exchanged; binding for the war "it matters not which party may have the surplus"; delivery at Aiken's Landing below Dutch Gap on the James, or Vicksburg; two agents each. **H** (full text read) [D-OR2-4 pp. 266–268].
- **Agents:** Robert Ould for the Confederacy; for the Union, Adjutant General Lorenzo Thomas in August–September 1862, then Lieutenant Colonel William Ludlow as acting agent from September. **H** [D-OR2-4 pp. 265, 334, 584; D-OR2-5 p. 127].
- **In operation:** Thomas received 3,021 men at Aiken's on 5 August; further deliveries through the autumn; paroled Union men waited at Camp Parole near Annapolis (8,226 on one return), where disorder followed. The Confederacy held Pope's captured officers outside the cartel from 1 August (General Orders No. 54, against Pope's orders on civilians) until 24 September. Ludlow claimed about 10,000 Union men released from parole in the December exchange. No grand total for 1862 was found. **H** for the items [D-OR2-4 pp. 350, 359, 584–585, 836–838, 851; D-OR2-5 pp. 127, 180–181].
- **Breakdown (outside the window, brief):** Davis's proclamation of 23 December 1862 and message of 12 January 1863 (section 7), the Union halt on exchanging officers (28–30 December 1862), the Confederate joint resolution of 1 May 1863, Lincoln's retaliation order of 30 July 1863, and the Vicksburg and Gettysburg paroles. Only the first two were read. **L** for the rest [D-WP-DH].

## 6. Confederate suspensions of habeas corpus in 1862 (gate: civil liberties; the Confederate seat)

- **Act of 27 February 1862:** "during the present invasion", the president may suspend the writ "in such cities, towns and military districts as shall, in his judgment, be in such danger of attack by the enemy as to require the declaration of martial law." **H** [D-CSL-1 pp. 1–2].
- **Davis's proclamations** (each suspending civil jurisdiction as well as the writ, with exceptions from mid-March for probate and county business, and banning the sale of liquor): **H** [D-RICH1 pp. 219–227]
  - 27 February: Norfolk and Portsmouth and ten miles around (Huger; instructions included imprisoning "all persons against whom there is well-grounded suspicion of disloyalty").
  - 1 March: Richmond and ten miles around, except the mayor's jurisdiction; Brigadier General John Winder to execute.
  - 8 March: Petersburg and ten miles around.
  - 13 March: by telegram, Governor Moore asked to proclaim martial law in Davis's name over Orleans, Jefferson, St. Bernard and Plaquemines parishes [D-OR2-2 p. 1421].
  - 14 March: Elizabeth City, York, Warwick, Gloucester and Mathews counties (Magruder).
  - 29 March: ten western Virginia counties (Heth).
  - 8 April: the Department of East Tennessee (Kirby Smith).
  - 1 May: South Carolina from the Santee to the South Edisto (Pemberton; this includes Charleston, which is not named).
  - 3 May: Lee, Wise, Buchanan, McDowell and Wyoming counties, Virginia (Humphrey Marshall).
- **Act of 19 April 1862:** confined suspension to arrests by Confederate authorities or for offences against the Confederacy, and limited the 27 February act to thirty days after the next session met (18 August), so it lapsed about 17 September [I, arithmetic]. Davis told the Senate on 8 October that "the writ is now nowhere suspended by action of the Executive" and that criminal jurisdiction of the courts had "in no instance" been interfered with. **H** [D-CSL-1 p. 40; D-RICH1 pp. 259–260].
- **Act of 13 October 1862:** the president may suspend "whenever in his judgment the public safety may require it", again only for arrests by Confederate authorities; officers must investigate arrests and discharge those improperly held "unless they can be speedily tried"; in force to thirty days after the next session met (January 1863, so about mid-February). No 1862 proclamation under it was found. **H** for the text [D-CSL-2 p. 84].
- **Generals on their own authority:** Thomas Hindman proclaimed martial law across his Arkansas district (his report: 30 June 1862; an encyclopedia snippet: Pulaski County from 10 June) with a provost marshal in every county; Secretary of War Randolph told the House on 5 September that Holmes had been told to rescind it if found. Albert Pike, 25 October: the powers "usurped by Generals Bragg, Van Dorn, and Hindman" were Caesar's; "There is no unwritten martial law." **H** for the texts [D-OR1-13 pp. 39, 44, 835–836, 899–901]. Bragg's martial law at Atlanta and Van Dorn's in Mississippi were not read (snippet, **L**).
- **Scale:** Neely, *Southern Rights* (1999), reportedly identified 4,108 civilians arrested by name and found no difference in arrest rates whether or not suspension was authorised (snippet, **L**) [D-S-NEELY].
- Context: the act of 15 February 1864 suspended the writ for arrests ordered by the president or Secretary of War for listed offences. **H** [D-CSL-1 p. 188].

## 7. Davis's proclamation of 23 December 1862 and the Union's answer (S60; gate: Black soldiers and their treatment, prison camps)

### 7.1 What came before it, inside 1862

- **Mumford** (section 8) was the stated cause. Randolph (29 June), Lee (6 July) and Davis (1 August, with a fifteen-day deadline, adding Hunter, Phelps and Fitch) asked the Union for an explanation; Halleck replied on 7 August that "no authentic information has been received". **H** [D-OR2-4 pp. 792, 806, 835–836].
- **Confederate General Orders No. 54 (1 August 1862)** put Pope and Steinwehr outside the usages of war for their orders on civilians and held Pope's captured officers. **H** [D-OR2-4 pp. 836–838].
- **General Orders No. 60 (21 August 1862):** Hunter and Phelps, for having "organized and armed negro slaves for military service against their masters", to be treated "as outlaws", and if any "commissioned officer employed in drilling, organizing or instructing slaves with a view to their armed service" is captured, "he shall not be regarded as a prisoner of war but held in close confinement for execution as a felon". OR II, 4, p. 857. **H** [D-OR2-4].
- **St. Catherines Island and Seddon's letter (30 November 1862).** In November a Confederate cavalry captain met six armed Black men in Federal uniform on St. Catherines Island, Georgia, killed two and captured four; Brigadier General Mercer asked that they be "made an example of". An endorsement signed "J. A. S., Secretary of War" reads: "With his [the President's] concurrence my decision is that the negro be executed as an example." Seddon to Beauregard, 30 November: "Slaves in flagrant rebellion are subject to death by the laws of every slave-holding State ... They cannot be recognized in any way as soldiers subject to the rules of war ... Summary execution must therefore be inflicted on those taken", the order to be given by the local commander. **OR Series II vol. 4, pp. 945–946, 954** (not vol. 5, as the Confederate-side note had it). The four men's fate is not recorded. **H** for the text [D-OR2-4].
- The Confederate War Department told its House on 22 September 1862 that it "has not been informed of the capture of any slaves by our armies." **H** [D-OR2-4 pp. 892–894]. No capture in 1862 of men of the 1st South Carolina, the Louisiana Native Guards or the 1st Kansas Colored (Island Mound, 29 October) was found. UNVERIFIED.

### 7.2 The proclamation

- Dated 23 December 1862, published as General Orders No. 111 on 24 December, countersigned by Benjamin. OR II, 5, pp. 795–797; Richardson, *Messages and Papers of the Confederacy* I, pp. 269–274. **H** (two texts agree) [D-OR2-5; D-RICH1].
- **Reasons given:** Mumford "actually and publicly executed in cold blood by hanging ... an unresisting and noncombatant captive"; no answer to repeated inquiries; Butler's New Orleans orders (the Woman Order, prisoners in ball and chain, sequestration under General Order No. 91); and "the African slaves have not only been excited to insurrection ... but numbers of them have actually been armed for a servile war".
- **Operative text:** Butler "a felon deserving of capital punishment ... an outlaw and common enemy of mankind", to be hanged on capture; no Union commissioned officer to be paroled before exchange "until the said Butler shall have met with due punishment". Then: "1. That all commissioned officers in the command of said Benjamin F. Butler be declared not entitled to be considered as soldiers engaged in honorable warfare, but as robbers and criminals deserving death, and that they and each of them be, whenever captured, reserved for execution. 2. That the private soldiers and noncommissioned officers in the army of said Butler be considered as only the instruments ... and not as free agents; that they therefore be treated, when captured, as prisoners of war with kindness and humanity and be sent home on the usual parole ... 3. That all negro slaves captured in arms be at once delivered over to the executive authorities of the respective States to which they belong, to be dealt with according to the laws of said States. 4. That the like orders be executed in all cases with respect to all commissioned officers of the United States when found serving in company with armed slaves in insurrection against the authorities of the different States of this Confederacy." **H** [D-OR2-5 pp. 795–797].

### 7.3 The Union response, 28 December 1862 to January 1863

- 28 December: Ludlow asked Stanton whether to stop forwarding Confederate officers for exchange; Stanton: "You will not make any exchange of commissioned officers until further instructions". 30 December: Halleck to eight department commanders, "No officers, prisoners of war, will be released on parole till further orders." Telegrams, not numbered general orders. **H** [D-OR2-5 pp. 127–131].
- January 1863 (outside the window): Rosecrans's General Orders No. 1 (7 January) answered "barbarous measures" in kind; Davis's message of 12 January proposed handing all captured Union officers in the states named in the Emancipation Proclamation to the states for trial for "exciting servile insurrection"; Ould (17 January) held that officers taken before 12 January would still be exchanged; the House asked Halleck on 30 January whether any rebel officers had been paroled since the proclamation (none since 30 December). **H** [D-OR2-5 pp. 162–163, 186–193, 226–228; D-RICH1 pp. 289–291].
- **Lincoln's own response before 30 July 1863: none found** in the sources read (the Collected Works could not be searched). Stanton and Halleck acted. **L** (a negative result).
- Press: the *New York Times* printed the text on 29 December 1862 (snippet, **L**) [D-S-NYT].

## 8. Mumford and Butler's orders in New Orleans, 1862 (S40, S60; gate: civilians, civil liberties, Black soldiers)

### 8.1 Mumford

- **The flag.** Farragut's order to hoist the flag on the Mint is dated 26 April 1862, and the *Pensacola*'s log has the colours up at 8 a.m. that day. **H** [E-ORN18 pp. 154–155, 237–238, 759]. When William B. Mumford pulled it down is disputed: Sunday 27 April during the morning service (Parton, 1864, and *Butler's Book*), against the naval records' 26 April for the raising, which a *Pensacola* officer runs together with the tearing-down; a fleet diarist's 25 April is impossible. All agree it was after the fleet anchored (25 April) and before Butler's troops landed (1 May). **L** for the day [E-Parton pp. 273–275; E-ButlerBook pp. 370, 439; E-ORN18 pp. 748, 770–771]. The *Picayune* of the next morning named "W. B. Mumford, who cut it loose from the flag-staff amid the shower of grape". **H** [E-Parton p. 275].
- **Sentence.** Special Orders No. 70 (5 June 1862; Parton and *Butler's Book* print "No. 10"): Mumford, "having been convicted before the military commission of treason and an overt act thereof, tearing down the United States flag from a public building of the United States, for the purpose of inciting other evil-minded persons to further resistance ... after said flag was placed there by Commodore Farragut", to be executed on Saturday 7 June between 8 a.m. and noon. **H** [E-OR-I15 p. 469]. The trial date (30 May), formal charge and members of the commission come only from Wikipedia. **L** [E-WP-Mumford].
- **Hanged on 7 June 1862 at the Mint**, about 10–11 a.m. **H** for the day and place [E-OR-I15 pp. 465–466; E-ButlerBook pp. 441–443; E-Parton p. 351]. Butler to Stanton, 10 June: the lowering of the flag "might, nay, ought, by every military rule, to have brought a bombardment upon the city." **H**. His wife and children and Dr. William Mercer, president of the Bank of Louisiana, asked for clemency and were refused; the same week Butler commuted the death sentences of six paroled men (the "Monroe Guard") to hard labour, which Parton (a Butler partisan) says made a second reprieve impossible. **H** for the appeals and the commutation [E-OR-I15 pp. 465–469; E-Parton pp. 347–351].
- **Southern reaction.** Governor Moore's proclamation "MUMFORD THE MARTYR" ("The city was not occupied by the United States troops nor had they reached there ... nor shall it pass unatoned"); Moore asked Davis to hang a captured Union lieutenant in retaliation (12 June). The Confederate inquiries and Davis's proclamation followed (section 7). **H** [E-OR-I15 pp. 508–509, 753–754, 906–908].

### 8.2 Butler's orders, with numbers and dates

All in OR Series I vol. 15. **H** for text and date unless marked.

- **General Order No. 28, 15 May 1862** (p. 426): "As the officers and soldiers of the United States have been subject to repeated insults from the women (calling themselves ladies) of New Orleans ... it is ordered that hereafter when any female shall by word, gesture, or movement insult or show contempt for any officer or soldier of the United States she shall be regarded and held liable to be treated as a woman of the town plying her avocation." Butler to Stanton, 16 May: "an absolute necessity". Never revoked; Butler later wrote that no arrest was made under it. Palmerston called it "infamous" in the Commons (Parton's quotation, undated; Hansard lists questions on 13 June 1862, snippet, **L** for the date) [E-Parton p. 341; E-SN-Hansard].
- **No. 25 (9 May):** relief for the city's destitute. **No. 29 (16 May):** Confederate notes banned from 27 May. **Newspapers:** the *Crescent* and *Bee* suppressed (13 May), the *Delta* taken over, *L'Estafette du Sud* (24 May), the *Picayune* stopped until its editors swore allegiance (31 July) (pp. 421–438, 532).
- **No. 41 (10 June):** the oath of allegiance for officials and for anyone asking a favour, passport or payment; a neutrality oath for foreigners claiming privileges; acts after 15 June by non-swearers void ("This oath will not be ... forced upon any"). Halleck, for the War Department, 28 August: "oaths are not to be prescribed by us to aliens". (pp. 483–484, 557–558).
- **No. 55 (4 August):** assessments on the subscribers to the city's $1,250,000 Confederate defence loan and on the cotton brokers who had urged planters not to ship cotton, "a fund for the purpose of providing employment and food for the deserving poor"; property seized or the party imprisoned if unpaid (pp. 538–540). The total assessed was not extracted.
- **No. 63 (22 August):** free Black men of the Native Guards, a militia under Confederate state commissions since 1861, invited to enlist as United States volunteers "subject to the approval of the President" (pp. 556–557). Butler to Halleck (27 August): "I have kept clear of the vexed question of arming the slaves". Before this Butler had told John W. Phelps (2 August) that "the President of the United States alone has the authority to employ Africans in arms" and to stop forming Black companies; Phelps, refusing to be "the mere slave-driver which you propose", resigned (pp. 534–537, 542–543, 555–556).
- **No. 71 (13 September):** neutral foreigners to register. **No. 73 (18 September):** property transfers by those who had not returned to allegiance void. **No. 76 (24 September):** everyone 18 or older not returned to allegiance to register by 1 October as "claiming to be an enemy of the United States", with a list of their property. **No. 91 (9 November):** all property in the District of La Fourche sequestered, which Davis's proclamation singled out (pp. 570–576, 592–593).
- **The consuls.** About 10–12 May Butler seized $800,000 in Mexican coin from the liquor store of Amédée Couturié, consul of the Netherlands, claimed by Hope & Co. of Amsterdam; he required the French consul to hold $716,196. **H** [E-OR-III2 pp. 116–119, 501–503]. Seward sent Reverdy Johnson to investigate (appointed 10 June; in New Orleans by 15 July); on 20 August Seward told the Dutch minister that Johnson's reports "have been approved by the President", the coin would be restored, and Couturié "has been very ill-used". **H** [E-OR-III2 pp. 115–116, 503–505; E-OR-I15 p. 471].

### 8.3 Seward's position and the recall (for S40's staff line)

- **Seward, 3 June 1862**, to Stanton: not "prejudging, much less censuring, Major-General Butler, whose general course of administration seems to me to have been eminently judicious and energetic", but asking "the utmost delicacy ... in transactions with consuls and with foreigners". **H** [E-OR-III2 p. 142]. **Seward, 20 August**, to the Dutch minister: "dissatisfaction with Major-General Butler's precipitancy and harshness in the transactions concerned were among the causes for transferring the administration of public affairs at New Orleans to General Shepley" (Butler's replacement as military governor, not his recall from the department). **H** [E-OR-III2 pp. 504–505]. Lincoln to Reverdy Johnson, 26 July, on Louisiana Unionists' complaints against Phelps: "I distrust the wisdom if not the sincerity of friends who would hold my hands while my enemies stab me." **H** [E-ButlerCorr2 pp. 135–136].
- **The recall.** General Orders No. 184 (8 November 1862) assigned Banks "By direction of the President" to the Department of the Gulf; Halleck told Banks on 9 November to "relieve Major-General Butler"; Banks assumed command on 16 December. **H** [E-OR-I15 pp. 590, 610, 624]. **Why is not documented at the time.** Butler (1892) says Lincoln would not tell him, Seward said Halleck knew, and Halleck said "It was done solely under the direction of the Secretary of State"; Butler concluded Napoleon III asked for it. France had protested in August. Lincoln wrote on 23 January 1863 that "we cannot longer dispense with General Butler's services." **L** for the cause; **H** for the dates and Lincoln's letter [E-ButlerBook pp. 533–549; E-OR-III2 p. 567; E-ButlerCorr2 p. 587].

## 9. The Maryland arrests of September 1861 (S23; gate: civil liberties), and the scale of arrests in 1861–62 (S48)

### 9.1 Who ordered them

- **Cameron to Banks, 11 September 1861:** "The passage of any act of secession by the legislature of Maryland must be prevented. If necessary, all or any part of the members must be arrested. Exercise your own judgment as to the time and manner, but do the work effectively." OR I, 5, p. 193; II, 1, pp. 678–679. **H** [E-OR-I5; E-OR-II1].
- The same day McClellan sent Cameron a letter for Dix ordering the arrest "at once" of six Baltimore men (members-elect Scott, Wallis and Warfield, the editors Frank Key Howard and Thomas W. Hall, and Congressman Henry May), with "E. J. Allen", Allan Pinkerton's alias, in charge; "I am informed that the Legislature meets the 14th." **H** [E-OR-II1 pp. 678–679]. McClellan's letter to Banks of 11 or 12 September, preserved only in Scharf (1879) and Brown (1887), says that "After full consultation with the President, Secretary of State, War, etc., it has been decided to effect the operation proposed for the 17th"; the OR notes the instructions "cannot be found", but its index dates the order "after conference with the President and Secretary of War". **M** that Lincoln was consulted [E-Scharf pp. 442–443; E-Brown p. 102; E-OR-I5 p. 194 n.; E-OR-II1 p. 563]. A claim that Lincoln and Seward went to Banks's headquarters on 12 September is a newspaper snippet. **L** [E-SN-WT].
- **Seward's office ran it**: Pinkerton reported to Seward, Seward and Dix were to settle the "modus operandi", Banks wired Seward during the operation, and the State Department handled discharges until 15 February 1862. **H** [E-OR-I5 pp. 194–196; E-OR-II1 pp. 667–675, 682].

### 9.2 Why: what the government believed

- The documents show the belief, not the evidence for it: Cameron's order; a Frederick editor's letter to Seward (12 September) claiming "it was decided in a caucus of the majority to pass an ordinance of secession at their next meeting at all hazards", while admitting "Perhaps, sir, these beliefs are unfounded apprehensions"; and the State Department's record calling the members "the band of conspirators who were known to be plotting to pass an act of secession", whose only exhibit is a printed petition for recognition of the Confederacy found in Howard's papers. **H** for the texts [E-OR-II1 pp. 671, 676–680].
- **Against:** the legislature had resolved against secession in spring 1861 (Wallis's committee report; a 53–13 House vote of 29 April per Wikipedia, **L**); William Schley wrote on 4 November that none of the arrested had done "anything whatever beyond speaking or writing in favor of secession"; Mayor Brown (1887) called the fear "so absurd that it is difficult to believe that he seriously entertained it" and thought the arrests "intended to strike with terror the opponents of the war". Partisan sources on both sides. **H** for what each wrote [E-OR-II1 pp. 610–612; E-Brown p. 103]. Governor Hicks to Banks, 20 September: "We see the good fruit already produced by the arrests ... I concur in all you have done." **H** [E-OR-I5 p. 197].
- Historians: Neely is summarised as treating the arrests as part of what kept Maryland in the Union, and McPherson as calling them "excessive and unjustified by any reasonable military necessity" (both snippets, **L**). Towers and Marvel: not found.

### 9.3 Scale: every count kept

- Baltimore, night of 12–13 September: Mayor George W. Brown; ten members-elect (Wallis, Warfield, Pitts, Scott, Sangston, Winans, J. Hanson Thomas, Harrison, Quinlan, Denison); Congressman Henry May; the editors Howard and Hall; Andrew Lynch: fifteen names on Dix's list of 13 September. **H** [E-OR-I5 pp. 194–196].
- Frederick, 17–18 September: only four members came on the opening day; Banks reported "after rigid examination but nine secession members were found in the city. These were arrested, with the clerk of the senate." **H** [E-OR-I5 pp. 194–195; E-OR-II1 pp. 682–685].
- **Legislators arrested in all:** about 27 named members and members-elect in the State Department's record (26 without one August arrest, 28 with J. Lawrence Jones; the agent's own count, **M**); "some thirty" (Brown, 1887); "fully one third" of the Assembly (Wikipedia); 31 (NPS, snippet); 27 (Emerging Civil War, snippet). **No source for the catalogue's "31–33" as a documentary count, and none at all for 33.** [I] 26–28 is the best-supported number [E-OR-II1 pp. 667–675; E-Brown p. 102].
- Where held and released: Fort McHenry, Fort Monroe, Fort Lafayette, Fort Warren. Many were released on oath or parole between September 1861 and January 1862; about fourteen still refused on 15 February 1862; the Dix–Pierrepont commission offered parole in March–May 1862; the last, including Brown, Howard, Hall, Wallis, Scott and Kane, were released unconditionally on 27 November 1862. **H** for the entries, **M** for the count of fourteen [E-OR-II1 pp. 619–620, 667–675, 744–748; E-Brown pp. 103, 108–109].
- **Corrections to the 1861 note [SW 3.2, 6.6]:** Brown was arrested on the night of 12–13 September, not 11–12; Baltimore's police commissioners were arrested on 1 July 1861, not only suspended. **H** [E-OR-II1 p. 620; E-Brown p. 98].

### 9.4 The scale of civilian arrests, 1861–62 (for S48)

- **Executive Order No. 1 (14 February 1862):** political prisoners in military custody released on parole, with amnesty for past disloyalty, spies excepted; "Extraordinary arrests will hereafter be made under the direction of the military authorities alone." Prisoners passed from State to War on 15 February. **Executive Order No. 2 (27 February):** Dix and Edwards Pierrepont to examine the remaining cases "ex parte and in a summary manner". **H** [E-OR-II2 pp. 221–223, 249; E-OR-II1 pp. 619–620]. How many the commission released: not found.
- **Counts:** 13,535 citizens arrested and confined in military prisons from February 1862 to the war's end, from the Commissary General of Prisoners' records (Rhodes, via Randall, who adds that the numbers "have been commonly exaggerated"). **H** for the figure's origin [E-Randall p. 152 n. 25]. Neely's counts reach us only as snippets that disagree: 864 or 866 under the State Department (April 1861–February 1862); "at least 14,000" overall; "well over 4,000" (scope unclear). **L** [E-SN-LincolnLore; E-SN-Podcast; E-SN-Neely2; E-SN-TCU]. The union-politics note's 10,000–15,000 [UP 4.3] sits inside these ranges.

## 10. Guerrilla war in Missouri, 1861–62 (catalogue gap 21; gate: guerrilla war, civilians, civil liberties)

### 10.1 Frémont's proclamation: the shooting clause was an order, the emancipation clause a request

- 30 August 1861: Frémont declared martial law across Missouri; "All persons who shall be taken with arms in their hands within these lines shall be tried by court-martial, and if found guilty will be shot"; those who destroyed railroads, bridges or telegraphs "shall suffer the extreme penalty of the law"; the enslaved people of those in arms "are hereby declared freemen." **H** [F-OR-I3 pp. 466–467].
- 2 September, Lincoln by special messenger: "Should you shoot a man, according to the proclamation, the Confederates would very certainly shoot our best men in their hands in retaliation; and so, man for man, indefinitely. It is, therefore, my order that you allow no man to be shot under the proclamation without first having my approbation or consent." On emancipation he only asked: "Allow me, therefore, to ask that you will, as of your own motion, modify that paragraph". **H** (OR and Lapsley agree) [F-OR-I3 pp. 469–470; F-Lapsley-5]. Frémont, 8 September, asked to be ordered openly on emancipation and to be allowed "to carry out upon the spot" the shooting clause; Lincoln's open order of 11 September modified only the emancipation clause and left the 2 September order on shootings standing. **H** [F-OR-I3 pp. 477–478, 485–486]. To Browning (22 September) he called the shooting clause "within military law" but "impolitic". **H** [F-Lapsley-5]. **The catalogue's S22 should carry the shooting order as a second element of Lincoln's 2 September letter.**

### 10.2 Martial law, habeas corpus and the commissions (November 1861 to spring 1862)

- 20 November 1861: Halleck, new to the department, found "No written authority ... to declare and enforce martial law" and asked for it; on 30 November: "if the President is not willing to intrust me with it he should relieve me from the command." **H** [F-OR-I8 pp. 395, 817]. **2 December 1861, Lincoln to Halleck** (countersigned by Seward): "you are hereby authorized and empowered to suspend the writ of habeas corpus within the limits of the military division under your command, and to exercise martial law as you find it necessary in your discretion". **H** [F-OR-I8 p. 401; F-Lapsley-5]. Halleck's General Orders No. 34 (26 December) enforced martial law in St. Louis and "in and about all railroads in this State". **H** [F-OR-I8 p. 468].
- **Bridge-burners.** General Orders No. 32 (22 December 1861): anyone caught burning bridges "will be immediately shot", the accused tried by military commission and, if guilty, "he also will suffer death"; the slaves of secessionists nearby, and the secessionists themselves, impressed to repair damage; towns and counties to pay for repairs. **H** [F-OR-I8 pp. 463–464]. Halleck to McClellan, 26 December: "Examples of severe punishment are the only remedies"; to Prentiss, 30 December: "Handle these bridge-burners without gloves." **H** [F-OR-I8 pp. 462–463, 474].
- **Commission procedure.** Halleck's General Orders No. 1 (about 1 January 1862): commissions only by the general-in-chief or department commander, at least three members, for "violation of the laws of war", not for offences triable by loyal civil courts; General Orders No. 13 (30 March 1862) let brigade and higher commanders convene them, but death, confiscation or imprisonment over thirty days needed the department commander's confirmation. Stanton "heartily approved" the procedure for all departments. **H** [F-OR-II1 pp. 247–250, 274, ~276].
- **Sentences.** OR Series II vol. 1 prints about twenty Missouri commission trials of winter 1861–62, most ending "to be shot to death". Halleck's General Orders No. 44 (20 February 1862) "provisionally mitigated" eight of them to confinement at Alton: "If rebel spies again destroy railroads and telegraph lines ... the original sentences against these men will be carried into execution." No execution of a tried bridge-burner was found in those pages. **H** for the orders, **M** that none was shot [F-OR-II1 pp. 265, 374–460]. The model came from Thomas Ewing's advice of 5 January (hang two or three, condemn and reprieve the rest as hostages for good behaviour). **H** [F-OR-II1 pp. 251–252].
- **The one case that reached Lincoln:** Ebenezer Magoffin, condemned for breaking parole. On 25 March 1862 "The President orders that execution of the sentence against E. Magoffin be suspended and that the record be transmitted to this office for his consideration", at the request of Kentucky's Governor Crittenden and the Magoffin family; on 9 April Lincoln asked that his confinement at Alton be eased if it endangered his health. He later escaped. **H** [F-OR-II1 pp. 272–276, 353].
- **Assessments.** General Orders No. 24 (12 December 1861) levied $10,000 on St. Louis secessionists for refugees from the south-west, "merely a collection to be made from friends of the enemy for charitable purposes"; No. 44 (20 February 1862) stopped further assessments on anyone who took the oath. **H** [F-OR-I8 pp. 431–432, 561].
- **Halleck on guerrillas, 13 March 1862** (General Orders No. 2): those who join guerrilla bands licensed by Price "will not, if captured, be treated as ordinary prisoners of war, but will be hung as robbers and murderers." **H** [F-OR-I8 p. 612].

### 10.3 The militia agreement (November 1861) and the Enrolled Missouri Militia (July 1862)

- 5–6 November 1861: Lincoln approved Governor Gamble's plan for a Missouri State Militia to serve only in the state, paid, armed and supplied by the United States, with one change: the governor must commission the federal department commander as the militia's major general. **H** [F-OR-I8 pp. 455–456]. By 15 April 1862 it had 13,800 men in the field (Schofield's report, self-interested). **H** for what he reported [F-OR-I13 p. 8].
- 29 May 1862, Schofield's General Orders No. 18: guerrillas "caught in arms, engaged in their unlawful warfare ... will be shot down upon the spot", with surrender on oath and bond offered. 22 July, General Orders No. 19, by the governor's authority: every able-bodied man to report "for the purpose of exterminating the guerrillas". Schofield counted 50,900 enrolled by late July, about 30,000 armed, and admitted the order made "every rebel in the State who could possess himself of a weapon ... spring to arms and join the nearest guerrilla band" while "thousands fled from the State to avoid the enrollment." **H** for what he wrote; one source, not independently testable [F-OR-I13 pp. 10–12, 402–403, 505–506]. No sign that either order went to Lincoln first.
- 29 November 1862, Lincoln to Bates: the Enrolled Militia is "not strictly either 'State troops' or 'United States troops.' It is of mixed character." **H** [F-Lapsley-6].

### 10.4 Summer and autumn 1862: the campaign, the executions, Palmyra

- 21 April 1862: the Confederate Partisan Ranger Act authorised partisan bands with soldiers' pay and payment for captured arms. **H** [F-OR-IV1 pp. 1094–1095]. Union officers answered that guerrillas "are but robbers, horse thieves and assassins" not entitled to prisoner-of-war treatment (Totten to Hindman, 17 September). **H** [F-OR-II4 pp. 524–525].
- Kirksville, 6 August: after the fight McNeil had fifteen captured men shot because they had broken earlier paroles. **H** [F-OR-I13 p. 215]. Independence fell on 11 August (garrison 312 per Schofield, 344 per NPS via Wikipedia); Lone Jack, 16 August. Merrill ordered public executions at Mexico (5 September) and Huntsville (late September) "that no further execution in your vicinity may be necessary." **H** [F-OR-I13 pp. 15, 611–612, 659–660].
- **Palmyra, 18 October 1862:** a notice of 8 October on McNeil's order warned that unless Andrew Allsman, an elderly Union informant taken in Porter's raid, was returned within ten days, ten of Porter's men in custody "will be shot"; ten were shot on the 18th. **H** for the notice; the shooting is confirmed by a Confederate officers' resolution [F-OR-I13 pp. 719, 909]. The Confederate commander Holmes protested to Curtis (7 December); Curtis answered on 27 December that there were no "Confederate citizens of Missouri", only Federal citizens, and sent the reply to Halleck hoping it would "meet the approval of the General-in-Chief." **H** [F-OR-II5 pp. 146–147]. **No Lincoln response to Palmyra was found**; a claim that it was discussed in cabinet comes from a local-history snippet (**L**) [F-WS-Palmyra].
- Scale: Schofield counted more than 100 engagements from 1 April to 20 September 1862, Union losses 77 killed, 156 wounded, 347 taken, and enemy losses 506 killed, about 1,800 wounded and 560 taken; effective federal and militia strength in June was 17,360, and Missouri had about 40,000 men in federal service in July. One commander's report. **L–M** [F-OR-I13 pp. 9–15].
- Kansas raiders: Halleck, 18 January 1862, on Jennison's men burning forty-two houses: "They are no better than a band of robbers ... Their conduct within the last six months has caused a change of 20,000 votes in this State." Lane's own report of Osceola (24 September 1861) says the town "was burned to ashes". **H** for what each wrote [F-OR-I8 pp. 507–508; F-OR-I3 p. 196].
- Francis Lieber's *Guerrilla Parties* (August 1862), written at Halleck's request, separated the partisan, "part and parcel of the army", from self-constituted bands, and held that "armed bands that rise in a district fairly occupied by military force ... are universally considered, if captured, brigands, and not prisoners of war." He declined to apply it to the civil war, leaving that to "the executive power". **H** [F-Lieber pp. 7–22].

### 10.5 What reached Lincoln (decision points for a situation)

1. **30 August–11 September 1861**: Frémont's proclamation (S22): order on shootings, request then open order on emancipation. 2. **5–6 November 1861**: the Gamble militia plan, approved with a change. 3. **20 November–2 December 1861**: Halleck's request for martial law; authorised for St. Louis on 21 November (Lapsley only, **M**) and for the department, with habeas suspension, on 2 December. 4. **25 March–9 April 1862**: the Magoffin death sentence, suspended for review at Kentucky's request. 5. **4 February 1862**: Hunter made senior to Lane (the only Lincoln act on the Kansas raiders found). 6. **7 December 1862–5 January 1863**: Lincoln suspended Schofield's $500,000 assessment on St. Louis secessionists (10 December), asked whether civil authority could replace military rule (17 December; Curtis: "The peace of this State rests on military power"), asked Gamble whether the Enrolled Militia alone could hold the north (18 December), refused to leave the north to it without Gamble's and Curtis's joint judgment (27 December), suspended the banishment of the Reverend McPheeters (27 December), and on 5 January told Curtis: "nothing but the sternest necessity can justify the making and maintaining of a system so liable to such abuses ... I could at once safely do (or you could safely do without me) whatever you and he agree upon." **H** [F-OR-I22-1 pp. 801–803, 826–840, 877–878, 888; F-OR-II5 pp. 99–100; F-Lapsley-6].
- Advice on record at the December decisions: against the assessments, the Reverend W. G. Eliot, Governor Gamble and "all the members of Congress here from Missouri but one"; for keeping military rule, Curtis and the provost marshal general F. A. Dick, who reported banishing "leading dangerous men" and complained of "the influence rebel sympathizers have at Washington." **H** [F-OR-I22-1; F-OR-II5].
- **Not decided by Lincoln on the record**: Halleck's bridge-burner and guerrilla orders, Schofield's orders 18 and 19, Kirksville, Merrill's executions and Palmyra were all department or district decisions; no document shows them put to him. A south-west Missouri commission's death sentences of September–November 1862 were lost at department headquarters ("No record of them whatever can be found here"). **H** for the documents, **M** for the absence [F-OR-II5 pp. 104–106]. [I] His levers in this war were who commanded, the grant of martial law and habeas authority, the review of death sentences when someone brought one to him, the militia arrangement, and suspending particular orders after petitioners reached him.

## 11. Sand Creek (29 November 1864): the Joint Committee's report only (gate: wars against Native nations)

Outside the window; recorded only as far as Congress's report, as the brief asks.

- **The inquiry:** the House resolution of 10 January 1865 directed the Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War to report "all the facts connected with the late attack of the third regiment of Colorado volunteers, under Colonel Chivington, on a village of the Cheyenne tribe of Indians, near Fort Lyon". The report, *Massacre of Cheyenne Indians* (38th Congress, 2nd session), was agreed on 4 May 1865 and filed on 22 May, signed by Wade. With it the committee told the president that Governor Evans "should be immediately removed from office, and that Colonel Chivington and Major Anthony should be at once arrested and brought before a military commission for trial". **H** [A-JCCW-CHEY; A-JCCW-V1].
- **Findings:** Black Kettle's and Left Hand's bands "were and had been friendly to the whites"; they had been told by Evans and the army to report to Fort Lyon and then to camp on Sand Creek; Black Kettle flew an American flag and a white flag; "men, women, and children were indiscriminately slaughtered"; Chivington "surprised and murdered, in cold blood, the unsuspecting men, women, and children on Sand creek, who had every reason to believe they were under the protection of the United States authorities"; "a foul and dastardly massacre which would have disgraced the veriest savage among those who were the victims of his cruelty"; "No attempt seems to have been made by the officers to restrain the savage cruelty of the men." Evans's testimony showed "such prevarication and shuffling as has been shown by no witness they have examined during the four years". **H** [A-JCCW-CHEY pp. ii–v].
- **The dead (every figure kept):** the committee, "more than one hundred dead bodies, three-fourths of them of women and children"; Chivington, "about five hundred" (first report), "between five and six hundred" (later report), and in testimony "five hundred or six hundred", having seen "but one woman" dead; Major Anthony "not over 125", his scouts counting 69 bodies, about two-thirds women and children; the agent Colley 100–150; the trader John Smith about 70. Wikipedia (pointer) gives the army commission's 137 and "around 150" as the common estimate. **H** for what each witness said; the true number is **L** [A-JCCW-CHEY; A-WIKI-SANDCREEK].

## 12. The catalogue's other needs-source items

### 12.1 The five unknown historical defaults

- **S04, the evacuation stories (March 1861): nothing recorded.** Nicolay and Hay say only that the rumour "had been openly and half-officially printed in the newspapers nearly a whole week" by about 15 March and that "The President had as yet announced no decision"; the cabinet voted on 15 March, "five to evacuate, two to attempt to supply". No denial, statement to senators, letter to Trumbull or reply to Blair was found. Burlingame's chapter (reported in snippets only) describes the Republican senators' caucus of 13 March and a resignation letter Blair drafted, but not Lincoln's reaction. **H** that Nicolay and Hay record none; **M** that none exists [G-NH3 pp. 407–408; G-BURL22 snippet]. [I] The defensible default is `say-nothing`, marked "no response recorded".
- **S32, the Grant rumour (March 1862): an inquiry through the War Department.** Halleck to McClellan, 3 March: Grant "left his command without my authority and went to Nashville"; McClellan, with Stanton's approval, replied the same evening, "Do not hesitate to arrest him at once if the good of the service requires it." Halleck, 4 March: "A rumor has just reached me that since the taking of Fort Donelson General Grant has resumed his former bad habits ... I do not deem it advisable to arrest him at present, but have placed General Smith in command of the expedition"; he told Grant to "remain yourself at Fort Henry". **Adjutant General Thomas to Halleck, 10 March: "By direction of the President the Secretary of War desires you to ascertain and report whether General Grant left his command at any time without proper authority, and, if so, for how long; whether he has made to you proper reports and returns of his force; whether he has committed any acts which were unauthorized or not in accordance with military subordination or propriety, and, if so, what."** Halleck restored Grant on 13 March and answered on 15 March that Grant had acted "from good intentions", recommending "that no further notice be taken of it." OR I, 7, pp. 679–684; I, 10, pt. 2, pp. 5, 30–33. **H**, the 10 March text re-checked by the lead on OR I, 7, p. 683 [G-OR7; G-OR10-2; L-OR7]. The default is `ask-halleck`. The catalogue's wording needs two fixes: the "bad habits" wire is of 4 March, and Halleck did not arrest Grant.
- **S42, the Harrison's Landing letter (8 July 1862): silence.** McClellan: "He read it in my presence, but made no comments upon it, merely saying, when he had finished it, that he was obliged to me for it, or words to that effect. I do not think that he alluded further to it during his visit, or at any time after that." Nicolay and Hay: he "simply passed it by in good-natured silence". No written answer; no showing to the cabinet in Welles's July entries or Nicolay and Hay. The order of 11 July made Halleck general-in-chief. **H** for the silence (two sources, though Nicolay and Hay partly rely on McClellan); **M** for the cabinet absence [G-MCC p. 487; G-NH5 pp. 447–455]. The default is `file`; Welles (quoted by McClellan) links Halleck's appointment partly to the letter, which is Welles's inference [I].
- **S50, Sturgis stops the trains (23 August 1862): Halleck settled it; Lincoln took no part.** On the night of 23 August Sturgis held four trains outside Alexandria and ordered Haupt arrested; Halleck's office wired Haupt that "No military officer has any right to interfere with it ... You will inform General Sturgis that your orders are supreme" (OR) or "if he attempts to interfere, I will arrest him" (Haupt's later recollection); Sturgis gave way ("take your d----d railroad!"). Haupt reported more than 24 hours' delay and later claimed it kept "at least 10,000 men out of the battle"; he declined to bring charges because Sturgis admitted the fault. **H** [G-HAUPT pp. 82–89; G-OR12-3 p. 638]. For the catalogue: the default sits with the general-in-chief (`back-railroad` issued by Halleck), and the president's option set may be empty in history [I].
- **S52, the Altoona governors (26 September 1862): received, answered briefly.** Twelve governors signed the address (OR III, 2, pp. 582–584: Curtin, Andrew, Yates, Washburn, Salomon, Kirkwood, Morton by proxy, Sprague, Pierpont, Tod, Berry and Austin Blair) pledging support, asking for a reserve of at least 100,000 one-year volunteers and hailing the 22 September proclamation. They called on Lincoln on 26 September (Welles: "an unauthorized gathering of State Executives ... I dislike these irregular and extraordinary movements"); Andrew read the address; Lincoln made "a short and pleasant reply, somewhat conservative", then, pressed by Kirkwood on McClellan, said he believed in his loyalty, listed his faults, and asked "who shall it be?" (Egle's account from participants, **M**). Lincoln laid the address before the cabinet on 30 September, its publication delayed in hope Maryland's governor would sign. **H** for the address, the date and the cabinet entry [G-OR3-2; G-WELLES1 p. 153 and 30 September; G-EGLE pp. 305–323]. The default is `hear-them`. Lincoln later told Boutwell the meeting had no part in the proclamation. **M** [G-NH6 p. 164].

### 12.2 Other lines

- **S07:** Nicolay and Hay print Lincoln's 1 April 1861 reply as a letter ("Since parting with you I have been considering your paper ... if this must be done, I must do it"), and say the affair "never reached the knowledge of any other member of the Cabinet". Whether it was sent or read aloud to Seward is unresolved (one snippet says read aloud). **H** for the text, **L** for delivery [G-NH3 pp. 448–449; G-HD snippet].
- **S12:** at the conference of 21 April 1861 Scott named three routes (through Baltimore, around it from Harrisburg, by Annapolis); "The last two routes were therefore agreed upon"; he "earnestly deprecated" fighting through Baltimore. On 22 April he called the Harrisburg route "of no value to us here, without a force of perhaps ten thousand men". So Scott's staff line is: around Baltimore, by Annapolis. **M** (Nicolay and Hay, citing OR I, 2, pp. 582–587, not opened) [G-NH4 pp. 128–131].
- **S18:** Bates's opinion on suspension, 10 Op. Att'y Gen. 74, dated 5 July 1861 and sent to the House on 13 July: the president's duty to protect the public safety includes power to arrest and to suspend the writ, with impeachment as the check (snippets only; the volume was not found). **L** [G-OUP; G-HEIN]. **S15:** no 1861 Bates opinion on the Restored Government was found; on West Virginia's admission (December 1862) Bates called the Restored Government "a government of necessity" and the admission "nothing less than attempted secession, hardly veiled under the flimsy forms of law"; the cabinet split three to three (Seward, Chase and Stanton for; Welles, Blair and Bates against). **H** [G-NH6 pp. 299–310].
- **S26:** Lincoln drafted, and did not use, a dispatch offering "such friendly arbitration as is usual among nations"; Nicolay and Hay say it "was not read" at the cabinet of 25–26 December, where Bates argued necessity and the cabinet agreed unanimously to Seward's release note. **H** for the draft, the inference is Nicolay and Hay's [G-NH5 pp. 34–38]. Arbitration can be an option with a sourced author: the president himself.
- **S28:** Cameron's report had been printed and mailed to postmasters to be released when the message was read; Lincoln found it unseen; "These were hastily recalled by telegraph, and Secretary Cameron printed a new edition, modified according to the President's direction", replacing the arming passage with one on not returning slaves on abandoned plantations. **H** for Nicolay and Hay's account [G-NH5 pp. 125–127]. Exact dates not found.
- **S34:** Welles's later account of 9 March 1862 has Stanton saying McClellan's plan "must be abandoned, and Burnside would inevitably be captured", and Welles answering that the *Virginia*'s draught kept her out of the Sounds; **no recall order was found** in what was read (Stanton's telegrams to governors in OR I, 9 and 11 were not searched). **M** [G-WELLES1 pp. 60–65].
- **S44:** Halleck to Stanton, 28 June 1862: "It is impossible to send forces to Vicksburg at present"; 15 July: "I cannot at present give Commodore Farragut any aid against Vicksburg." **H** [G-OR17-2 pp. 43, 97–98].
- **S55:** Welles's diary, 1 October 1862: Porter "has, however, stirring and positive qualities, is fertile in resources, has great energy, excessive and sometimes not over-scrupulous ambition ... a young and active officer is required"; Davis lacked "the vim, dash, — recklessness perhaps is the better word"; the president favoured Dahlgren and "has not great admiration or respect for Porter". **H** [G-WELLES1 pp. 157–158].
- **S40 (Seward's staff line)** and **S23 (Seward's role)** are in sections 8 and 9.

## 15. Corrections to the catalogue and to earlier notes

- **S23 / [SW 3.2, 6.6]:** Brown was arrested on the night of 12–13 September 1861; Baltimore's police commissioners were arrested on 1 July 1861, not only suspended; "31–33 legislators" is not a documentary count (26–28 is), and no source gives 33.
- **S32:** the "bad habits" wire is dated 4 March 1862; Halleck explicitly did not arrest Grant; Lincoln's act was the inquiry of 10 March.
- **S22:** Lincoln's 2 September 1861 letter contained an order (no shootings without his approval) as well as the request on emancipation; the 11 September order modified only the emancipation clause.
- **S60 / [CS 4.4]:** Seddon's letter of 30 November 1862 is in OR Series II vol. 4 (pp. 945–946, 954), addressed to Beauregard about the four men captured on St. Catherines Island.
- **S40:** Butler lost the military governorship to Shepley in June 1862, partly, Seward wrote, for his "precipitancy and harshness"; Banks was assigned on 8 November and took command on 16 December.
- **S45 (the brief, not the catalogue):** the $500,000 for colonization is in the appropriation act of 16 July 1862, not the Second Confiscation Act.
- **[SW 6.5]:** Pike made nine treaties, not four; Watie was a Confederate colonel by 1 September 1861 (a July commission is not confirmed); the Texans took Arbuckle on 5 May 1861.
- **Prisoners (the brief):** Lorenzo Thomas, not Ludlow, was the first Union agent under the cartel.

## Findings outside my scope

- **The catalogue's schema case P14** gains real examples: S04's default is "no response recorded" and S50's default belongs to the general-in-chief, not the president, so the schema should allow a default of "none recorded" and a default held by another post.
- **S50 and S44** may not be presidential situations at all in history: both were settled by Halleck without Lincoln. Whether they stay as president's situations (with Halleck's seat answering) is a design call for lane 0.7.
- **The 5 July 1862 act** gave the president a lever the catalogue lacks: suspending annuities and abrogating treaties by proclamation, never used. A candidate situation for gap 22, for the gate to place.
- **Guerrilla war has presidential levers on record** (martial-law and habeas grants, death-sentence reviews, the militia agreement, the December 1862 suspensions), so 0029's "regional pressure, not a decision" may be too narrow; the decision points in section 10.5 are a starting list.
