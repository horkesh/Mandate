## 1. The changing law, with the clauses kept separate

**Earlier war-aims context, with a limit.** Senate Miscellaneous Document 7 reproduces the resolution **Andrew Johnson submitted on 24 July 1861**, ordered to lie on the table and be printed. It says the war was not prosecuted for conquest, subjugation, or overthrowing or interfering with the states' rights and established institutions, and names constitutional supremacy and preservation of the Union as its objects. The text does **not expressly use the word slavery**. This printing establishes the submitted Senate wording, not the House's wording or either chamber's final adoption vote. Those require their respective journals or the *Congressional Globe*. No legal conclusion that Congress guaranteed the permanent survival of slavery is derived here. [J24, p. 1.]

## 2. Frémont's proclamation was more than its emancipation clause

Frémont's **30 August 1861 proclamation** imposes martial law throughout Missouri, identifies an army-occupation line, threatens court-martial and shooting for people taken with arms within it, declares confiscation and freedom of the enslaved people of the described opponents, and threatens punishment for attacks on communications and other conduct. It also says ordinary civil tribunals are not suspended where they can operate peaceably. These different clauses should not be collapsed into one emancipation order. [O3, pp. 466–467.]

Lincoln's **2 September letter** has two different acts. First, he **orders** that no person be shot under the proclamation without his prior approval or consent, explaining his fear of reciprocal killing of prisoners. Second, he **asks** Frémont to modify the confiscation and emancipation paragraph on his own motion to conform to the 6 August act, citing the danger of alienating Union supporters, particularly in Kentucky. Those are Lincoln's stated expectations, not measured public-opinion effects. [O3, pp. 469–470.]

On **11 September**, replying to Frémont's preference for an open order, Lincoln orders the disputed confiscation and emancipation clause construed not to exceed the statute. This is not evidence that the entire proclamation was revoked, or that his first letter merely requested restraint over shooting. [O3, pp. 485–486.]

## 3. Hunter's emancipation order and Lincoln's reserved authority

Lincoln's **19 May 1862 proclamation** reproduces Hunter's **9 May General Orders No. 11**, declaring enslaved people in Georgia, Florida and South Carolina free after the department had been put under martial law. Lincoln says no commander had been authorized to issue such a declaration and declares it void **so far as it declares freedom**. He reserves to himself the question whether commander-in-chief emancipation is competent and indispensable. He distinguishes that question from army and camp police regulations. [12 Stat. 1264–1265.]

The same proclamation renews the appeal for gradual compensated abolition under the April resolution. It is not a promise that the states will accept, nor a statement that a commander's emancipation decree and the organization of a regiment are the same administrative act. The House correspondence over Hunter's regiment is separate evidence in the [Black service note](2026-11-20-s7-freedpeople-and-black-service.md). [12 Stat. 1265; H133, pp. 1–2; H143, pp. 1–3.]

## 4. September: the announced future act and immediate instructions

The **22 September 1862 preliminary proclamation** first states restoration of constitutional relations as the war's object. It proposes renewed aid for voluntary immediate or gradual abolition in states not then in rebellion, and continued colonization with the people's and receiving governments' consent. It then announces that on **1 January 1863** people held enslaved in states or designated parts whose people are then in rebellion will be free, with executive recognition and protection. It describes the evidence by which representation in Congress could establish non-rebellion absent contrary evidence. [12 Stat. 1267.]

Its immediate instructions require military and naval personnel to obey and enforce the March Article of War and July sections 9–10. It also announces a future recommendation to compensate loyal citizens for losses by U.S. acts, including loss of enslaved people. The words are a promised recommendation, not an appropriation paid on 22 September. [12 Stat. 1267–1268.]

**Date discipline.** The announcement is not the **1 January 1863 final proclamation**. The latter begins on printed p. 1268 in the same scan. No list of January exceptions or later recruitment language is imported into September here. [12 Stat. 1267–1268.]

### Cabinet evidence and its limitations

Welles's published diary, under **22 September**, records a special cabinet meeting. He says Lincoln had decided the policy and invited criticism of the document; Seward suggested amendments. Welles records Blair's opposition on grounds of border-state and electoral danger. These are attributed accounts of anticipated consequences, not evidence that all border states or all northern voters responded as Blair predicted. [W, pp. 142–144.]

**Seward's advice to wait for military success is sourced as retrospective testimony.** In Carpenter's *Six Months at the White House* (1866), Lincoln recounts the earlier cabinet discussion and Seward's advice to postpone publication until it could be supported by military success. Carpenter reports Lincoln as saying that he put the draft aside and revised it while waiting. But the account explicitly says Lincoln did **not remember the exact date**, and its account of the final meeting's day differs from Welles's dated entry. It cannot alone establish an exact 22 July meeting or a verbatim speech by Seward on that date. [C, pp. 20–23; W, pp. 142–144.]

[I] Use this as a sourced recollection of the recommendation, with its provenance visible, while a precise July chronology remains a question for contemporary cabinet notes. Do not silently upgrade an 1866 recollection to a contemporaneous transcript.

## 5. Colonization: consent clauses, money and Black criticism

The District act's section 11 appropriates **$100,000**, with a per-emigrant expenditure ceiling, for people who desire to emigrate. A **different appropriation, 16 July**, provides **$500,000** for carrying out District emancipation and colonizing people expected to be freed under confiscation legislation, with repayment from confiscated property and discretion given to the President. The Second Confiscation Act's section 12 supplies further authority with willingness and host-government consent conditions; it is not the $500,000 appropriation. [12 Stat. 378, 582, 592.]

These clauses prove neither that everyone was willing nor that all the appropriated money was spent. No expenditure total, emigrant total or precise ending date of the Chiriquí scheme is supplied in this note. Later schemes remain outside the research window unless separately sourced and dated.

The unsigned **“The President and His Speeches”** editorial in *Douglass' Monthly*, **September 1862**, attacks Lincoln's address to Black men and its colonization argument. It rejects blaming the presence of Black people for the war, locates the cause in enslavement and the determination to maintain it, and argues that racial prejudice is produced by slavery rather than an immutable bar to living together. It also criticizes government delay in carrying out emancipation legislation and arming Black men. [DSEP, pp. 707–708.]

This is a Black abolitionist newspaper's intervention, not a poll of all Black people and not a factual finding that every accusation in the editorial is proven. Nor should its paraphrases be substituted for the actual text of Lincoln's August address. The complete primary printing supplies substantially more than the earlier note's single search-summary phrase about a colonization lecturer. [DSEP, pp. 707–708.]

## 6. Confederate legislation and the purposes it states

The **11 October 1862 exemption act**, chapter 45, includes many civil, professional and industrial exemptions. Its plantation clause expressly invokes policing. It provides an exemption for the described agent, enslaver or overseer where state law requires a white person to remain, with a separate provision in states without such a law for plantations with twenty enslaved people. It also provides an additional policing exemption for groups of smaller plantations within five miles, under stated conditions. The clauses exclude cases with an available white male adult not liable to service. [CS, pp. 77–79, especially p. 79.]

It is therefore imprecise to paraphrase the whole statute as simply “every enslaver of twenty people goes free of conscription.” The text establishes a policy of sustaining slavery's coercive policing. It does **not by itself establish a numerical level of resentment, demonstrate a uniform non-slaveholder reaction, or prove that the preliminary proclamation was its sole cause**. Those causal and reception claims need petitions, debates, letters or other evidence in addition to the statute. [CS, p. 79.]

The **13 October act**, chapter 62, requires military or naval personnel who capture or take possession of enslaved people to report them, provides depots and public notices, and provides restoration to claimants upon proof. It authorizes guarded work while people are held and requires records of removals. This is evidence of a Confederate re-enslavement policy concerning people taken from Union control; it should not be confused with the later March 1863 impressment act. Actual enforcement and individual cases require further records. [CS, pp. 89–90.]
