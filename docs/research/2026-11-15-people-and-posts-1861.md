## 1. Verification of the March 1861 cabinet and its citations

### 1a. The Senate Executive Journal

The volume exists in the Internet Archive as a Google scan, identifier `journal-of-the-executive-proceedings-of-the-senate-of-the-united-states-of-...` (its index page reads "[Volume XI.]"). The earlier lane could not find it: the other Google scans under similar titles are volumes of other years (vol. VII, 1845-48; vol. XII, Dec 1861-Jul 1862; and others). The sitting is headed "TUESDAY, MARCH 5, 1861" under "[At a special session convened by the Proclamation of the President of the United States, of the 11th day of February, 1861.]" (p. 289).

| Office | Nominee | Message | Consent | Vote |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Secretary of State | William H. Seward, of New York | p. 289 | p. 290 | by unanimous consent, no roll call |
| Secretary of the Treasury | Salmon P. Chase, of Ohio | p. 289 | p. 290 | the same resolution |
| Secretary of War | Simon Cameron, of Pennsylvania ("Pensylvania") | p. 289 | p. 290 | the same resolution |
| Secretary of the Navy | Gideon Welles, of Connecticut | p. 289 | p. 290 | the same resolution |
| Secretary of the Interior | Caleb B. Smith, of Indiana | p. 289 | p. 290 | the same resolution |
| Postmaster General | Montgomery Blair, of the District of Columbia | p. 289 | p. 290 | roll call on Mason's motion: 41 yeas, 4 nays |
| Attorney General | Edward Bates, of Missouri | p. 290 | p. 290 | roll call: 40 yeas, 5 nays |

- The messages are each dated "Washington, 5th March, 1861" and were "received from the President of the United States, by Mr. Nicolay, his secretary" (p. 289). The Senate "by unanimous consent, proceeded to consider the said nominations" and resolved to advise and consent to the first five "agreeably to their nominations respectively" (p. 290).
- Blair's nays: Bragg, Clingman, Mason, Mitchel. Bates's: those four and Powell. Counts are mine, from the OCR name lists (Blair's yeas run 41 names; Bates's 40).
- **The pack's date, 5 March 1861, is the consent date and is now primary-sourced** for all seven. The "7 March" for Welles in Callahan p. 1 and the snippet for Treasury are oath or commission dates, [I]; the Journal does not give them.
- The same sitting orders the Secretary to give the President a list of the nominations left unacted at the close of the last session (p. 290). The day's next sitting (6 March, p. 291) opens with Lincoln's nomination of Norman B. Judd to Berlin, so the Senate was taking foreign-ministers' nominations from the second day. [MF q.10]'s "about 19 March" for Adams and Dayton is a lower bound, not the date; I did not read their consent pages.
- **Barbour at Harpers Ferry** (an unplanned find, see 1c): vol. XI p. 34 (13 Jan 1859) prints the nomination of "Alfred M. Barbour, of Virginia, to be superintendent of the United States armory at Harper's Ferry, Virginia, to date from January 1, 1859, as proposed by the Secretary of War", and p. 39 (19 Jan 1859) his confirmation.
- **Interior under Buchanan.** I found no nomination to the Interior office in the Journal for January-March 1861 (searched the OCR for "Secretary of the Interior"). Absence is not proof that the office was vacant (the chief clerk may have acted); the pack row keeps "needs-source" for that sentence only.
- Licence: a US Senate document, public domain (government work). The scan is a third party's upload of a Google copy; I use the printed pages' content, not the scan.

### 1b. The *New York Times* of 6 March 1861 (`NYT-1861-03-06`)

Read in the archive.org OCR of issue 2950. Under "OUR WASHINGTON DISPATCHES. Tuesday, March 5." the heading is "THE CABINET NOMINATIONS CONFIRMED". It lists all seven names and says the appointments "were sent to the [Senate] and immediately confirmed to-day", and that "The Cabinet nominations were all confirmed without objection except in the case of Messrs. Bates and Blair, against whom some Southern Senators voted." A second report in the same issue (the Senate proceedings) says the appointments "were immediately sent in, and almost unanimously confirmed". **Verdict: the citation is verified and says what the pack claims**, and the Journal's roll calls (Southern nays only on those two) agree. One slip in the same issue: a late dispatch says "Mr. Mallory has been confirmed as Secretary of the Navy"; that is the Confederate Congress's Mallory, not a sign of a Union one.

### 1d. The two remaining checks

- **McClellan's telegram of 28 June 1862.** OR I-11-1 p. 61 (civilwar.com page transcription; the scan was not seen) prints it in full: "Savage Station, June 28, 1862-12.20 a.m.", "Honorable E. M. Stanton", ending "If I save this army now, I tell you plainly that I owe no thanks to you or to any other persons in Washington. You have done your best to sacrifice this army." So the Official Records do print the sentence, and the unresolved item in McClellan's people row is answered: the tags `shifts_blame` and `disdains_superiors` stand. What is *not* settled is whether the telegraph censor struck it before Stanton saw it ([IF] 2.4: Bates ch. VII p. 109 says Sanford cut it; another page dates a Sanford cut to July 1862). The OR prints McClellan's original; the censorship is a separate claim.
- **Fox in ORN I,4.** At p. 247 (read in the OCR): Fox left Washington on 19 March, passed Richmond and Wilmington and "reached Charleston the 21st"; sought Captain Hartstene, was introduced to Governor Pickens, shown to Beauregard; "We reached Fort Sumter after dark and remained about two hours"; Anderson agreed he "might report that the 15th of April at noon would be the period beyond which he could not hold the fort unless supplies were furnished"; "I made no arrangements with Major Anderson for reenforcing or supplying the fort, nor did I inform him of my plan." This matches [MF] question 4 and its verification table (rows 2-4); nothing in it changes.

## 3. Filter posts and intelligence chiefs on the Union side, from March 1861

0058's `role: filter` is a *post kind* property ("holds or passes what comes in for its principal"), and `intelligenceChief` is a person on one post. 0032 defines a filter by what its holders "can hold, trim or delay". The questions: who screened what reached the president and the chief commanders in March 1861, and when.

**Sourced.**
- **The President's private secretary, Nicolay, for callers.** The Executive Journal records him on 5 March 1861 as "his secretary", bringing the President's messages to the Senate (p. 289). The *Times*' Washington dispatch of 7 March (printed 8 March) reports that Lincoln "has adopted a systematic routine, and all callers have first to see Nicolay, the Secretary, who now bears the brunt of the labor." Burlingame (secondary; citing Stoddard, Howells and the *Times* of 8 March) says Nicolay screened each caller, was called "the bulldog in the ante-room", and in late March got business hours cut to 10 a.m.-3 p.m., then shorter (vol. 2 ch. 21; [MF] q.14). This is a filter in 0058's sense on what reached Lincoln from visitors, which included governors and congressmen (0032's "informal sources"). Date: from the inauguration at the latest.
- **How the army's reports reached Washington.** Department commanders addressed the Adjutant General's office at "Hdqrs. of the Army", still styled New York City (OR I-1 p. 599, Loring to Thomas, 23 March 1861; the pack's `us_general_in_chief` row already says so). The Secretary of War then reported to Lincoln (Holt did so on 5 March for Anderson's letters of 28 Feb and 2 March, as [MF] q.2 reads it from Epperson, which I could not re-read). Lincoln also took Scott's judgement on each plan (Fox's memorandum: Scott "agreed ... that an entrance from the sea was impossible", ORN I,4 pp. 246-247).
- **No War Department telegraph filter in March 1861.** 0040 and [IF] 2.1 place the filtering telegraph office in Stanton's War Department from early 1862 (Eckert to Washington in 1862; Sanford's censorship from February 1862). I did not re-verify those dates (Bates's book did not download, and [IF] read it with the White House museum page and Wikipedia). I found nothing in the sources I did read that puts a telegraph office or manager between the army and Lincoln in March 1861; that is an absence, not a dated document. [I]
- **No Union intelligence chief on 5 March 1861.** Fishel (CIA, *Military Intelligence 1861-63*, Part II): "there was no superior intelligence and counterintelligence agency at Washington"; the two bureaus there were Pinkerton's, "attached not to the War Department but to the Army of the Potomac", at the capital "for the eight months in 1861-62 when the army was headquartered there", and one headed by Lafayette C. Baker (the passage on its origin runs past what I read). The Union's service was "improvised pretty much according to the taste of the individual commander". Fishel's pieces concern the army's campaigns from Manassas, so they put neither bureau at the start of March 1861 by implication only. This is a sourced *absence*, not a document dated 5 March.

**Inferred ([I]), not applied to the pack.**
- Scott's headquarters, with the Adjutant General's office, as a filter: the *structure* is sourced above, but no source I read shows it holding, trimming or delaying a report in March 1861. What the record shows instead is non-compliance (Scott "ignored" the 9 March directive; [MF] design implication 8), which the compliance model handles. So I did not set `role: filter` on the `general_in_chief` kind. If the lead wants it, the kind has one Union post and no Confederate one.
- Nicolay sorting mail and Hay's role: Hay's own essay on the White House would be the source; not found as text.
- Seward as a filter on the diplomatic and commissioners' channel: documented in Burlingame ch. 22 and [MF] findings, but it is a cabinet man's use of his office; `cabinet` as a kind cannot be a filter without making all seven filters.
- The Navy Department's reports reach Welles first ([IF] section 5); the pack's row names Charles W. Welsh as chief clerk under Toucey (`NavyReg1861`), and no source read shows what he or Welles's clerks screened.

**Not known.** Who, if anyone, screened reports to Scott's headquarters in New York and Washington (the Army's chief clerk or Scott's military secretary; I did not look the office up); whether Pinkerton or any detective served the Union government before the war began.

**What the pack now says (all marked as such in the rows).**
- A new post kind `private_secretary` with `role: filter`; a new post `us_president_secretary` (department `us_executive`, parent `us_president`, region `washington`), held by `nicolay_john_g` (the tenure start left empty: the Journal puts him in office on 5 March).
- No kind has `role: intelligence`; every `intelligenceChief` stays null; Scott's row records why.
- **Lead's ruling (2026-11-15):** the secretary post stays, with `role: filter` and Nicolay as holder. It does not conflict with 0040, whose empty March filter is the telegraph office (0032's seat); the secretary is a different channel, the President's door. The ruling reads that channel as callers and mail. The sources I read cover callers only; that he also screened mail is not sourced (see Open, item 5).
