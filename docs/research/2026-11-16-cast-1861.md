## How to read this note

- **Evidence first, mapping second.** The appendices (A to E, one per research helper) hold the cited evidence, person by person, in the vocabulary-free form the brief asked for. The tables here are the mapping onto the vocabulary lane 1.1 settled and the lead approved. If the vocabulary changes, re-map from the appendices; no one needs to re-research.
- **The settled vocabulary.**
  - **values**: an unordered set from `union, party, duty, order, conciliation, reputation, career, caution, abolition, glory`, each value cited.
  - **temperament**: two centres, `boldness` and `candour`, each a direction (`high, above, average, below, low`) with a citation.
  - **regard**: one centre, toward his own faction's principal (Lincoln or Davis) at the 5 March 1861 start, as a direction with a citation.
  - **regard between people**: kept in the CSV column `regard_others` as `person_id:direction:CITE;...`, for lane 1.5.
  - **Empty** means no evidence found, never "average".
- **What the directions mean** (my reading of lane 1.1's scale, for lane 1.11 to confirm): `high` and `low` are marked and documented in the window; `above` and `below` are clear but moderate or single-episode; `average` means the evidence is mixed or neutral. Regard: `high` = devoted or warm, `above` = respectful and co-operative, `average` = correct and formal, `below` = wary or resentful, `low` = hostile or contemptuous.
- **Boldness is not the existing `nerve` column.** Nerve is a hidden stat. Boldness here is the readiness to take or advance risk, judged from what the person did and wrote in the window. Both are cited; where they differ the CSV says so.
- **Candour** is how frankly he tells his superior bad news or his true view. A person can be candid in private and guarded in public; the CSV records the superior-facing behaviour.
- **Regard is at the 5 March start.** Where the only evidence is later (after April), the regard cell is empty and the later evidence is in the appendix, not coded. Regard toward the principal is empty for Lincoln (player's seat) and for Davis (Davis is the principal).

## Judgement calls the mapping rests on

1. **Seward: regard `below`, candour `below`.** The 5 March start has Seward asking to withdraw from State on 2 March (N&H 3 p370-371) and, per Noah Brooks's second-hand account, thinking himself "the greater man". On candour he let the Confederate commissioners believe Sumter would be evacuated (Campbell's account, needs-source against OR I-1 pp. 280 to 295; Tulane commentary). His 1 April memo is blunt to Lincoln, but the Confederate channel decides the cell. The regard flips to respect after April, which the start-date rule does not code. **Flag for the lead:** this is the cell most likely to look wrong in a run of the pushback engine.
2. **Chase `high` boldness, `average` regard.** One cabinet voice (with Blair) for relief on 15 and 29 March, against compromise since November. His own regard for Lincoln in the window is not found; the `average` rests on N&H and a secondary summary. Flagged needs-source.
3. **Cameron `below` boldness.** The existing row rates nerve `above` on a December 1861 report (NH 5 p125-126). In the window he deferred to Army officers on Sumter (16 March) and was absent on 29 March. Both facts are cited; the disagreement is flagged below.
4. **Welles `average` boldness.** On 15 March he warned of "untold disaster" and refused to advise provoking hostilities; on 29 March he argued for armed relief. The existing row's `nerve above` holds from 29 March only.
5. **Scott `average` boldness.** Cautious on Sumter, firm on defending the capital, and he pushed Patterson hard in July. `conciliation` comes from his 3 March memo to Seward and his 28 March memo to Cameron (hold the eight remaining slave states).
6. **McClellan `below` boldness.** Large aggressive plans on paper (27 April plan for 80,000 men), but at Rich Mountain on 11 July he did not make the attack the plan required (Poland, Essential Civil War Curriculum, a scholarly secondary). Regard toward Lincoln is empty: he was a civilian on 5 March and the first evidence is his 27 July letter to his wife, which is after the window and quoted from a modern edition.
7. **Patterson `low` boldness** (his own reports of 12 June, 9 July and 16 to 18 July) and **Lyon `high`** (Camp Jackson) are the most solid temperament cells in the Union military set.
8. **Harney, Ellis and Pierpont have existing rows with every stat `unknown`.** The window evidence above supports directions for the temperament cells, and those are in the CSV. The stat cells they already hold are not touched.
9. **Lee's `conciliation` and `union` are not coded.** His 20 April letters show duty and loyalty to his native State; the only "opposed to secession" evidence is a self-report of 1868 in a family compilation (Lee Jr.), a sole weak source. Both are recorded as unmapped motives below.
10. **Magoffin `conciliation`** is the neutrality policy, which Nicolay and Hay (own-side, hostile) call a cover for rebel sympathies. It is kept with that warning.

### For the sensitive-history gate

All of the following are described plainly in the appendices with their sources, and left for the lead and the gate to place:
- **Davis, 29 April 1861:** his message to the Provisional Congress describes the enslaved population as raised from "brutal savages" to "docile, intelligent, and civilized agricultural laborers" "under the supervision of a superior race", and calls their labour indispensable (Richardson I p68). This is the speaker's own public statement, cited as primary.
- **Stephens, 21 March 1861 (Savannah):** the new government's "corner-stone" rests on the inequality of the races and slavery as the Black man's "natural and normal condition". The text is a reporter's sketch of the speech, not verbatim.
- **Toombs, 7 January 1861:** territorial rights for slaveholders and "no negro equality", ending "equality or war".
- **Lee:** the 27 December 1856 letter calls slavery a moral and political evil yet speaks of the "painful discipline" necessary "for their instruction as a race" and leaves the result to Providence; he managed the Custis estate of 197 enslaved people, whose will required freeing them within five years, from 1858 (NPS). Described, not placed.
- **Harney, McClellan, Butler:** non-interference and fugitive-slave handling, recorded as policy and military necessity, not as a belief.
- **Toombs's "suicide, murder" remark** at the 9 April cabinet has **no first-hand source**; it is not used.

## Findings that change what 1A should expect

- **Seward and the Confederate channel** is a dated candour case worth a situation: the evidence is in Appendix A and needs OR I-1 pp. 280 to 295 (Campbell's account) before use.
- **The 15 and 29 March cabinet opinions** give a dated, cited vote for seven men on one question (15 March: Chase and Blair for relief; Seward, Cameron, Welles, Smith and Bates against. 29 March: Chase, Blair and Welles for relief; Seward and Smith against; Bates "evacuate or relieve" without choosing; Cameron absent). That is the best single source of `caution`/`union` for the cabinet and could seed a test.
- **Welles's front chapters are not diary.** Any existing row that cites `WELLES-D 1 p1 to p60` for a 1861 judgement is citing a later memoir.
- **Lee, Johnston and Beauregard at Manassas:** the window evidence for Beauregard's and Davis's regard is thin and mostly after July. Davis's October 1861 endorsement of Beauregard's report is official and primary but outside the window; the regard cells are empty for that reason.
- **Fox's candour** about Seward ("timid", "paralizes every movement from abject fear", 2 May 1861) is a private letter, own words, primary.

Role in window: General-in-Chief of the US Army (Lieutenant General by brevet), Washington; plans the defence of the capital and the 4 Mar inauguration, advises on Sumter/Pickens, drafts the Anaconda-style strategy (3 May), directs Patterson and McDowell, retires 1 Nov 1861 (letter asking retirement 9 Aug 1861 is after the window).
Existing row: yes; scott_winfield

VALUES

He frames the first (conciliation) as the way to hold the other slave states. — Scott to Seward, 3 Mar 1861, Dickinson House Divided transcription https://hd.housedivided.dickinson.edu/node/35246 (summary read; transcribed from Lincoln Papers) — [OWN-LETTER]
- V2 [conciliation] 28 Mar memo to Cameron: evacuating Sumter and Pickens would "instantly soothe and give confidence to the eight remaining slaveholding States"; Forts Jefferson and Taylor to be kept.
