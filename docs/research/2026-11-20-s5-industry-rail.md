# S5: industrial capacity, railroads and supply constraints

Research, 2026-11-20; docs only, not accepted design, data or gate approval. See [S5 sources](2026-11-20-s5-sources.md). Printed pages are cited throughout. **[I]** marks calculations, interpretations and proposed model treatment.

## Baselines are productive systems, not immediately available stores

Kennedy's **preliminary** 1860 census report estimates national manufactures, including fisheries and mining, at **$1.9 billion** for the year ending 1 June 1860. It identifies small mechanical production below the reporting threshold of $500 as omitted. This is an annual value of output, partly estimated, not a balance of stock in government depots. [I] The entries support a marked asymmetry in the location of industrial plant, but not an all-purpose numerical ratio of Union to Confederate combat power. Definitions, control, transport, inputs, skilled work, contracts and delivery intervene. Pennsylvania pig iron alone exceeds the two listed Virginia/Tennessee entries by roughly twenty to one; that describes these returns, not a complete census of all Confederate iron or all usable armament.

Schwab's language about factories being absent or textile production scarcely existing at the war's start is broader than the primary census evidence can safely sustain. [SCH pp. 269, 271; C60 pp. 61-66] [I] Use specific establishment/output evidence and distinguish new government armories from existing industry. This note does not import his blanket industrial descriptions into pack data.

## Union naval expansion: purchase and transfer were not new manufacture

Welles's table of additions since **4 March 1861**, excluding losses, lists **180 vessels acquired by purchase, 50 by transfer and 123 by construction**, together **353**. The construction column includes **13 second-class screw sloops, 27 screw gunboats, 39 side-wheel gunboats, 12 armored wooden vessels and 32 armored iron vessels**, totalling **120,290 tons**. These rows were read visually because OCR changes 50 to 60 and 353 to 363. [N62 p. 27; scan checked]

The report explicitly says its prospective fleet composition applies **when vessels under construction are completed**. [N62 p. 28] [I] Consequently the construction additions are not 123 delivered, commissioned warships on the report date. Likewise purchased merchant hulls, departmental transfers and new construction describe different means of expansion; they cannot all be charged as new shipyard output. The acquisitions table is a cumulative administrative measure, not an annual production rate or a direct measure of blockade readiness. Reconcile losses and the pre-existing fleet before comparing additions with the separate 427-vessel total; do not add the tables together.

## Confederate adaptation: capacity, output and imported components

Schwab's account of the September 1862 ordnance report gives **more than 14,000 small arms manufactured**, with reported monthly public-armory capacity above **2,000** and private capacity above **1,500**. He immediately cautions that it should not be assumed these capacities were achieved. His study emphasizes captured machinery, Tredegar and import purchases as different sources. [SCH p. 269]

[I] Three measures must remain separate: installed or claimed monthly capacity; arms actually manufactured; and serviceable arms delivered to a force. The small-arms figure is not a monthly delivery target of 3,500. It is also not all arms available: imports, captured weapons, repairs and existing stocks are distinct categories.

For powder inputs, Schwab reports nearly **400 men working in sixteen caves by 1 August 1862**, with **200,000 lb of saltpetre produced by October** and foreign imports exceeding domestic production in the account he summarizes. He separately reports **one million pounds of powder from Augusta in the year ending July 1863**. Saltpetre and powder are not the same commodity, and the later production window cannot become a November 1862 stock. [SCH pp. 270-271]

Memminger gives another concrete industrial bottleneck: the difficulty of engraving and supplying paper for Treasury notes. His March report describes dependence on a single available note engraver, substitution of lithography and development of suitable paper, with banks advancing notes while government production lagged. This is an interested official's account of an actual administrative obstacle, not a general claim that printing currency had no cost or delay. [M62 p. 2]

[I] Proposed munitions explanations should therefore identify an unavailable input, damaged plant, procurement constraint or delivery delay rather than a generic penalty called industrial weakness. Treasury credit can finance an order; it cannot supply a missing machine or create an immediate cargo arrival. Do not create a managed stock of enslaved labour in order to explain production; existing protected-population rules remain binding.

## Railroads: preserve the denominator before comparing systems

The preliminary census report contains an unresolved internal inconsistency. Its narrative gives **30,598.77 miles** in operation at the end of the decade and **$1,134,452,909** in cost. They do not establish train capacity, track gauge, through-running arrangements, gradients, repair rates or which bridge limits a supply route.

The census narrative identifies the eastern trunk connections to the interior and discusses the roles of the New York, Pennsylvania and Baltimore & Ohio routes, alongside the Western & Atlantic and Memphis & Charleston connections. [C60 pp. 103-104] [I] This is a guide to which lines deserve research; it is not sufficiently precise evidence to redraw the game's graph. A named-line capacity needs its own schedules, rolling-stock inventory and operating conditions.

## Wartime authority and company incentives

The Union act of **31 January 1862** authorized the President, when public safety required, to take possession of railroad and telegraph lines and their equipment, repair or extend them, and put their personnel under military control. It also provided commissioners to assess compensation and placed military transport under the Secretary of War and his agents. The text authorizes action; it does not prove that all private railroads were nationalized or operated directly by the army. [RR62 p. 334, §§1, 3-4]

[I] This fits orders through the responsible holder. Presidential priorities, War Department supervision, private-company operation, military repair and compensation are different actions. The authority should not be represented as the player moving a train or a division directly.

Schwab describes Confederate railway deterioration, unavailable replacement rolling stock and bridges, and reduced freight capability. His example of only a few roads operating more than two trains daily is **1863**, not a measured 1862 national service level. He also reports rising nominal revenues/dividends despite deterioration. [SCH pp. 273-274] [I] Financial profit in depreciating paper does not establish increasing physical carrying capacity.

His account gives **$2.5 million** in Confederate congressional aid to railroads during 1862, beginning with **$1 million in February** for the Richmond & Danville extension, with other grants in Georgia and Louisiana. Opposition questioned military necessity and private benefit. [SCH p. 275] [I] An appropriation is not completion of track, availability of rail iron or new freight service on the date of the vote.

The South Carolina Railroad example reports cotton carriage falling from over **800,000 bales in 1860** to **120,000 in 1861**, with **28,000 a year averaged over 1862-64**. These are commodity-traffic measures alongside changed military usage, not proof that train capacity fell in those same proportions. [SCH p. 274]

## Adjacent food and salt constraints

Salt was needed from sources whose access could be interrupted. Schwab describes coastal production exposed to Union operations, inland works, state contracts and competing state claims on Saltville. He reports output above **7,000 bushels daily before the end of 1862**, without giving a November-only observation. [SCH pp. 267-268] [I] Do not grant that rate from the beginning of the year or turn production into automatic equal distribution among all states.

The finance note's November indices show salt much more expensive relative to 1860 than flour, while domestic cotton lagged gold. [SCH Appendix I, foldout opposite p. 312] [I] This is consistent with distinct procurement and delivery constraints and cannot be captured by assuming all regional output is equally exchangeable everywhere.

Schwab acknowledges that wartime agricultural statistics are thin. He reports arguments over planting cotton versus grain, and later difficulties inducing shipments to Richmond under impressment prices. He goes further and attributes scarcity primarily to distribution and policy. [SCH pp. 276-278] [I] Preserve the mechanism as a research question without adopting the stronger claim that regional harvest failure never mattered. Actual army and civilian supply depends on place, season, transport, access, coercion and households' choices, none of which is measured by a single national crop stock here.

### Quantities still needed before calibration

[I] Highest-value next evidence is monthly serviceable locomotives/cars, train weights and frequency on the particular supply lines, repair shop and iron availability, bridge outages and restoration dates, named-depot receipts, contract prices and actual arms deliveries. No measured tons-per-day, universal gauge penalty, conversion factor from dollars to weapons, or November inventory of military rail equipment is established by this note. Missing figures should remain absent until sourced; the census is a baseline, not a substitute for wartime operating returns.
