# The secession winter and the first four months of Lincoln's presidency, Dec 1860 – Jul 1861 (Mandate scenario research)

Status: first pass complete (2026-11-14), for decision 0028 (the campaign starts at the inauguration, 4 March 1861). Built from six parallel source sweeps and then merged. **Nothing here contradicts 0028's premise**: the war begins whatever the player does. Two findings sharpen it, see "Design implications" (the Confederate decision to fire was taken before Fox's fleet arrived, and the call for troops, not the bombardment alone, took the upper South out).

Conventions: citation keys in [brackets] resolve in the Sources table at the end. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result summary, page not read. **[I]** = our interpretation, never a source's claim. **UNVERIFIED** = asked for and no source found. Where sources disagree both values are given. Several sources are secondary (Wikipedia, blogs, the Tulane Sumter site, which transcribes the Official Records and Lincoln's Collected Works); the Collected Works host (quod.lib.umich.edu) returned HTTP 403 to every fetch, so Lincoln texts are cited at second hand through sites that cite Basler. Hidden-stat numbers are not in this note; the permille shares below are [I] proposals for the designer.

## 2. Lincoln's real choices

### 2.1 Sumter: evacuate, provision, or reinforce (5 March – 6 April)

What the men who advised him actually said. The 15–16 March written question was "Assuming it to be possible to now provision Fort Sumter, under all the circumstances is it wise to attempt it?" [TUL-15]. Answers:

| Member | 15–16 March | 29 March (written, after the noon meeting; Cameron absent) |
|---|---|---|
| Seward (State) | No. Force would "provoke combat" and start civil war; the fort is "practically useless". [TUL-Seward] | "against the expedition in every view"; Anderson to "retire from Sumter forthwith". [TUL-S29] |
| Chase (Treasury) | Yes, "with serious difficulty"; civil war "highly improbable". [TUL-Chase] | Clearly for provisioning Sumter and holding Pickens; if resisted, reinforce. [TUL-S29] |
| Cameron (War) | No; "the sooner it be done the better", relying on Scott, Totten and Anderson. [TUL-Cameron] | absent |
| Welles (Navy) | No; would not "advise a course that would provoke hostilities". [TUL-Welles] | Concurs in sending an armed force with provisions and a peaceful notice. [TUL-S29] |
| Smith (Interior) | No; if war must come, better "by the resistance of the authorities" of South Carolina. [TUL-Smith] | Evacuation "a necessity", present it as forced. [TUL-S29] |
| Bates (Attorney General) | No (page undated); willing to evacuate; reinforce Pickens and Key West. [TUL-Bates] | Reinforce Pickens "at all hazards"; Sumter's garrison must be withdrawn or rescued. [TUL-S29] |
| Blair (Postmaster General) | Yes; evacuation would "convince the rebels that the administration lacks firmness"; Fox can relieve "with little risk". [TUL-Blair] | Relieve Sumter "without reference to Pickens"; no confidence in Scott's judgment. [TUL-S29] |

Tally conflict: McPherson counts five against and Blair alone for [AH-Disastrous]; Tulane's transcription of Chase is a yes, which gives five to two [TUL-Chase]. Tulane says only two members explicitly objected to relieving Sumter on 29 March [TUL-Soln4].

The military advice. Scott (12 March): Sumter held by under 100 men, about 3,500 besiegers, provisions for 26–48 days depending on the ration; relief needs a fleet not collectable in under four months, 5,000 regulars and 20,000 volunteers, six to eight months; the time for warship reinforcement "passed away nearly a month ago"; Anderson, Captain Foster and Totten called relief "impracticable"; evacuate [EPP-ScottMar12]. His 28 March memo extended that to Pickens, and the cabinet read it as political advice, which weakened it [EPP-ScottAbandon], [AH-Showdown].

The options and what each risked (sources, then our reading):

| Option | Who backed it | Risk, per sources | Historians' judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evacuate Sumter (hold Pickens) | Seward, Smith, Cameron, Bates, Scott, Totten; Welles on 15 March | Would "soothe" the eight slave states still in the Union (Scott) but demoralise the party, concede Confederate legitimacy, and risk foreign recognition. [BORI-Sumter], [AH-Showdown] | Republican hawks warned it would leave Lincoln "as dead politically as John Brown is physically" (Blight lecture). [OYC-12] |
| Provision only, with notice | Blair, Chase; Welles by 29 March; Lincoln | Looks like aggression to the border states; might push more slave states out; divides the North into war and peace parties. [BORI-Sumter] | See 2.2. |
| Reinforce by force | Nobody seriously after Scott's 12 March letter | 25,000 troops and six months or more (Scott, 9 March). [AH-Showdown] | n/a |
| Do nothing | Implicit | Garrison out of bread in weeks; Anderson wrote on 1 April that rations would last "about a week longer". [TUL-Apr4] | n/a |

**Speculation, marked as such.** No historian read here says what evacuation would have produced. [I] Hurlbut's report that Charleston would demand Pickens next suggests evacuation would not have ended the crisis [TUL-Hurlbut]; the Confederacy also held that its aim was the fort, not a settlement. This is our inference only.

### 2.2 Did Lincoln mean the Confederates to fire first?

Positions (sources, not our view): Ramsdell (1937) argued Lincoln could and did maneuver the Confederates into firing [TUL-LinWar]. Potter (1942) held Lincoln leaned toward abandoning Sumter until late and that 6 April, when he learned Pickens had not been reinforced, was the first irrevocable step; Current and Stampp date the firmer decision to 29 March and describe a "strategy of defense" in which any first shot would be Southern but was not goaded [TUL-Soln4Comm], [TUL-LinRealist]. McPherson calls it "a stroke of brilliance" and quotes "Heads I win; tails you lose" [AH-Disastrous]. Fuller says Lincoln "hoped" the Confederates would fire first [BORI-Sumter]. Davis and Stephens held after the war that the fleet was the provocation [TUL-LinWar]. Lincoln's own words: the 1 May letter to Fox says both "anticipated that the cause of the country would be advanced by making the attempt" [EPP-Fox0501]; the 4 July message says the assailants "began the conflict of arms" and the government had said only provisions would go [MC-July4]; Browning's diary of 3 July reports Lincoln saying "They attacked Sumter—it fell, and thus, did more service than it otherwise could", which Ramsdell reads as proof, Potter doubts, and Nevins treats as probably accurate from memory [TUL-LinRealistComm]. Not found: Thomas, Hoogenboom or Detzer on this question.

Facts that bind the design: Davis's cabinet decided 5-1 to attack on 9 April and the orders went out 10 April, before the fleet could arrive [CWM-Apr9], [TUL-Apr10]; the fleet's ships were scattered by the gale and the *Powhatan* was at Pensacola [TUL-Apr12-3], [TUL-Apr6].

### 2.3 Fort Pickens (11 March – 17 April)

Lincoln ordered reinforcement on 11–12 March; Captain Adams did not land the troops because he read the order as breaking the truce of 28 January (orders arrived 31 March) [BF-Pensacola]. On 1 April Lincoln signed fresh orders; on 6 April he learned nothing had landed. The *Powhatan* mix-up: Seward's telegram to restore her to the Sumter fleet reached Foote at 3:00 pm, after she sailed at 2:45; Porter, holding a Lincoln-signed order, refused to turn back [TUL-Apr6]. Sources split on blame: some put it on Seward, others on Lincoln's decision to run the Pickens plan around the War and Navy secretaries [ECW-Misgov], [TUL-Apr6]. Troops landed 12 April (night of 11 April per Battlefields); more on 16–17 April [TUL-Apr12-4], [BF-Pensacola]. Why Pickens was not the trigger: no source I read states it; [I] the Confederate decision at Charleston preceded the landing and Sumter was the target. Lincoln said later that the failure at Pickens made the Sumter expedition the strongest case for using it [TUL-Soln4Comm].

### 2.4 The informal channel (Seward, Campbell, the Confederate commissioners)

Commissioners Forsyth and Crawford reached Washington 5 March. Seward's memorandum refusing recognition is dated 15 March but was delivered only on 8 April [EPP-SewMemo]. Through Justice Campbell, Seward led them to expect evacuation "soon"; on 1 April Seward said he thought the president did not intend to supply Sumter; about 7 April he sent word "Faith as to Sumter fully kept. Wait and see." Campbell read an evacuation pledge, Seward meant a pledge of notice [CWM-Faith] (dates after 15 March rest on a snippet and blogs). Potter: Seward's views matched Lincoln's until late and his talks bought four weeks. Current and Stampp: Seward acted on his own initiative [TUL-SewAdv]. Seward's 1 April "Some Thoughts for the President's Consideration" proposed no domestic policy beyond abandoning Sumter, demanded explanations from Spain and France, and implied the president should let him direct policy; Lincoln's draft reply said he must hold the property [TUL-Apr1].

### 2.5 After Sumter

- **The call (15 April).** Legal basis the Militia Act of 1795 (disperse proclamation, three-month limit; a 1799 act capped calls at 75,000) [WIKI-75k]. The proclamation convened Congress for 4 July, an interval of 80 days in which the president acted alone (3 May call for 42,034 three-year volunteers, 22,714 regulars, 18,000 seamen, "submitted to Congress"); Congress legalised these acts by the Act of 6 August (snippet) [UCSB-P83], [GOVINFO-326]. No source read describes the cabinet debate over the size or form of the call: UNVERIFIED.
- **Blockade or closing ports.** Proclaimed 19 April, extended 27 April. Conflict: one source says Welles preferred a de facto blockade and Lincoln went with Seward; another says Bates favoured "closing" the ports because a blockade implies belligerent status; no source I fetched settled each man's position [HIST-Blockade], [FLC-1984] (snippet), [ARCH-Prologue2001]. Britain recognised belligerency on 13 May [HIST-Blockade]. The Supreme Court upheld it 5-4 in the Prize Cases (1863, outside window).
- **Maryland.** Lincoln accepted the Baltimore-bypass (20 April), authorised habeas suspension (27 April), let Butler occupy Baltimore (13 May), and defended the arrests in his 4 July message ("all the laws, but one"); Taney's ruling was ignored [BOOKRAGS-Apr20], [WIKS-Habeas], [WIKI-Merryman], [QUOD-July4].
- **Kentucky.** Restraint: no troops, trade allowed, 5,000 muskets ("Lincoln guns") shipped quietly, Joshua Speed as private channel (section 3).
- **Missouri.** Lincoln approved Lyon's enrolment of St. Louis loyalists on 30 April (snippet), and Harney was removed on 30 May after Price–Harney (section 3).
- **Slavery policy.** Cameron approved Butler's holding of fugitives (30 May) without a public policy; Lincoln joked privately about "Butler's fugitive slave law"; other officers kept returning fugitives [CWN-Cameron530], [FONER-Guardian]. Black volunteers were refused (section 6).
- **Bull Run.** McDowell warned that enlistments (90-day) would expire; two units went home on the morning of the battle [WIKI-Bull]. Whether Lincoln or the press forced the advance: UNVERIFIED in these sources.

### 3.2 Maryland

- **Factions.** A Southern-rights plurality in Baltimore and the lower Western Shore, a Bell-Unionist (conditional) mass, and almost no Republican base [WIKI-MD60].
- **People.** Governor Thomas H. Hicks (American Party) refused to call the legislature in winter and spoke of a "neutral position" [MSA-Hicks2], [WIKI-Hicks]. Baltimore Mayor George W. Brown and Police Marshal George P. Kane [WIKI-Baltimore]. Severn Teackle Wallis chaired House Federal Relations [WIKI-Wallis]. Former governor Enoch Louis Lowe sided with Brown and Kane. Hicks, Brown and Kane planned the bridge burning on the night of 19 April; each later blamed another [MSA-Hicks4].
- **What moved it.** The Pratt Street riot (4–5 soldiers and 9–12 civilians dead depending on source; NPS pages give 11 and 16) and the bridge burning cut Washington off; Hicks asked for no troops to cross [NPS-Pratt], [NPS-Riot], [MSA-Hicks2].
- **Union actions that helped.** Routing troops through Annapolis (7th New York arrived 25 April); the occupation of Baltimore on 13 May; Hicks's own move of the legislature to Frederick; the 29 April 53-13 vote [WIKI-Baltimore], [HD-MDVote].
- **That backfired or cost.** The habeas suspension and the Merryman arrest (25 May; Taney's writ; Lincoln defied it); Kane's arrest and the replacement of the police board (27 June–1 July); on 11–12 September the arrest of Mayor Brown and 31–33 legislators, which ended the legislature's session. Brown was held 14 months [WIKI-Merryman], [WIKI-Kane], [CHESOB-Sep], [NPS-PolPris].
- **Outcome.** Maryland did not secede. 6 November 1861: Unionist Augustus Bradford 57,472 (68.8%) to Democrat B. C. Howard 26,045, under troop supervision and a loyalty oath [WIKI-MD61].

### 3.3 Delaware

Legislature turned away Dickinson on 3 January; governor William Burton defended slavery and proposed a convention yet spoke for the Union; after Sumter he named Henry du Pont militia commander. Sussex County was the Southern-leaning part [MT-Burton], [WIKI-DEcw]. Slavery was marginal; Wilmington's rail links to Philadelphia left no room [I]. In November Lincoln sent Representative-elect George Fisher with a compensated-emancipation offer that failed [MT-Burton].

### 3.4 Kentucky

- **Factions and people.** Governor Beriah Magoffin (Southern sympathiser; refused the troop call but let recruits slip south); Senator John J. Crittenden (Compromise author, backed neutrality; his sons George (Confederate) and Thomas (Union) split); Garrett Davis, a Unionist who understood Lincoln to promise no occupation without a Kentucky show of force; Joshua Speed, Lincoln's unofficial agent; Simon B. Buckner, State Guard head, Confederate-leaning; Robert Anderson, given command of a Department of Kentucky on 28 May (ECWC) and named by the legislature on 18 September (House Divided) [ECWC-KY], [CWM-KYDE], [KYM-Guns], [ALC-KY], [HD-KY37856].
- **What moved it.** Neutrality (16 and 20 May); the Military Board created 21 May, which took arming power from Magoffin and starved the State Guard; the 20 June congressional elections (Unionists 9 of 10 seats, ballots about half the 1860 total, Union share over 70% from a snippet) and the 5 August legislative elections (Union 75–76 of 100 House seats, 27-11 Senate); Polk's occupation of Columbus ended neutrality in September [ECWC-KY], [WKMS-Neutral], [WKMS-Aug], [CWM-KYNorth].
- **Union actions that helped.** Restraint; Lincoln's 5,000 muskets (first shipment 14 May per ECWC; Cincinnati 5 May per Kentucky Monthly; later reportedly about 23,000); secrecy that respected neutrality [KY-Markers], [KYM-Guns], [ECWC-KY].
- **That backfired or risked.** Lt. William Nelson's Camp Dick Robinson (Nelson detached 1 July; recruits arrived 6 August; about 3,200 men and 7,000 arms by end of August) drew Magoffin's 19 August protest; Lincoln's 24 August reply (26 August in Wikipedia) declined to remove it. Frémont's Missouri order (30 August) alarmed Kentucky Unionists: Lincoln wrote Browning on 22 September that "to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game" [CAMPDR], [HD-KY37379], [AHC-Lose].
- **Corrections to inputs.** Cameron, not Lincoln, made the 15 April request (quota 3,123 men). No separate "Lincoln April refusal" document exists; Lincoln's April act was assuring Davis. Thomas Crittenden as Home Guard commander is unconfirmed: Delph and then James Speed led the Louisville Home Guard [CWGOV-KY], [WIKI-LHG].

### 3.5 Missouri

- **Factions.** German-American Republicans and unconditional Unionists (Frank Blair Jr.), conditional Unionists including Sterling Price, and secessionists under Governor Claiborne Jackson, with the State Militia under Brigadier General Daniel Frost. Lyon commanded the St. Louis arsenal; William Harney commanded the department until 30 May [WIKI-CampJackson], [MOENC-Frost], [ECW-CJ].
- **Seats.** The February convention (no avowed secessionists; Price its chairman) carried legitimacy, not the legislature. Governor Jackson was a secessionist in office from January; the convention vacated his office in July [MO-Timeline].
- **What moved it.** The arsenal removal (25–26 April); Camp Jackson, 10 May (669 captured per three sources, about 689 per Emerging Civil War; Lyon's force 6,000–8,000; 28 civilians, 2 Union and 3 militia soldiers killed, about 75 wounded), and the riots that followed; moderates such as Price moved to the State Guard. Price–Harney (21 May) gave the federals St. Louis and the State Guard the rest; Lincoln repudiated it within nine days by removing Harney. Planters House 11 June ("This means war" per Wikipedia), Boonville 17 June, Carthage 5 July, Gamble government 31 July [OZARKS-CJ], [ECW-CJ], [WIKI-PriceHarney], [WIKI-MOCW], [MO-Timeline].
- **Helped.** Securing the arsenal and arming St. Louis loyalists (Lincoln approved the enrolment, snippet; Lyon reported 3,300 offered, 3,082 armed, snippet) [NH-AL2109].
- **Backfired.** The Camp Jackson riot pushed conditional Unionists toward Price [ECW-CJ].
- **Guerrilla war by July 1861.** Proper guerrilla war is 1862–64; but sabotage and executions began in July: Monroe Station (10 July), ambushes near Wentzville (15–16 July), executions at Martinsburg (18 July) and Danville (21 July) [ECW-Grant].

### 3.6 Western Virginia and the rest of Virginia

- **Virginia convention.** 152 delegates elected 4 February; secession rejected 90-45 on 4 April (EV; I found no source for 88-45); the 16 April referendum alternative failed 64-77; 17 April, 88-55 (EV, Wikipedia, e-WV 2344) or 85-55 (e-WV 2341). Thirty-two trans-Allegheny delegates voted no. Lincoln's 15 April call asked Virginia for 2,340 militia, and Governor Letcher refused. [I] The call, not Sumter alone, flipped the convention between 4 and 17 April [EV-Conv], [EWV-2341], [EWV-2344], [WIKI-VACW].
- **Referendum, 23 May.** Totals conflict: 125,950-20,373 with many western returns discarded (EV); 128,884-32,134 (Wikipedia); 96,750-32,134 (snippet). Western estimate: 34,677 against, 19,121 for; two-thirds of "no" votes came from the northwest [EV-WV], [WIKI-WVCW].
- **Wheeling.** First convention 13–15 May (about 430 delegates; Carlile's "New Virginia" proposal rejected). Second 11–25 June (about 100 delegates from 34 counties per e-WV): declaration 13 June, reorganisation ordinance 19 June, Francis H. Pierpont governor 20 June, legislature 1 July. Lincoln and Congress recognised it as the legal government of Virginia; its legislature sent Carlile and Willey to the Senate [WIKI-Wheeling], [EWV-2344], [EWV-2345], [WIKI-Restored].
- **Military.** McClellan crossed the Ohio on 26 May with a proclamation to western Virginians; Philippi 3 June (about 775 recruits routed with "one volley"); Rich Mountain 11 July (Rosecrans's flank force beat Pegram, who surrendered about 553 on 13 July); Garnett killed 13 July. The October statehood vote was 18,408 to 781 (outside window) [CWN-McCProc], [WIKI-WVCamp], [NPS-RM], [BF-RM], [WIKI-WVCW].
- **Rest of Virginia.** Harpers Ferry (seized 17 April, armory burned 18 April, abandoned by Confederates 14 June), Gosport (20 April, 1,085 guns left, *Merrimack* burned), Lee's resignation (20 April, command of Virginia's forces 22–23 April), Alexandria occupied 24 May [NPS-HF], [BF-Gosport], [NPS-Lee], [NPS-Ellsworth].

### 3.7 The upper South that left

Arkansas 6 May (69-1), North Carolina 20 May, Tennessee 8 June (68,282 to 59,449 against a convention on 9 February; about 104,913 to 47,238 for secession on 8 June; Middle Tennessee went from 51% against to 88% for; East Tennessee against) [CWC-Secesh] (snippet). All left after the 15 April call, and their governors' refusals were explicit about "coercion" [WIKI-75k]. Tennessee's declaration text: UNVERIFIED whether it names slavery [CIVILDISC].

### 4.6 The first campaigns

Scott's 3 May letter to McClellan proposed a complete blockade, a Mississippi movement with a chain of posts and the capture of Forts Jackson and St. Philip, 12–20 gunboats and 40 transports for about 60,000 men, aiming to "envelop" the seceded states; it said 25,000 more regulars and 60,000 volunteers for three years [OR-LI1-369]. The "Anaconda" label came later from the press (snippet) [WIKI-Anaconda]. This corrects the existing Union dossier's "author of slow strangulation Anaconda" (its verify flag).

First Bull Run, 21 July: Union 35,732 present, about 18,000 engaged; Patterson's 14,000–18,000 did not engage; Confederates 32,000–34,000, about 18,000 engaged. Casualties: Union 2,708 (481, 1,011, 1,216) in one table and 2,896 (460, 1,124, 1,312) in the article's text; Confederate 1,982 [WIKI-Bull].

## 5. Northern opinion

**The winter.** Greeley's *Tribune* wrote on 9 November that the Cotton States could go "in peace" if they judged they could do better out of the Union, and that the right to secede "may be a revolutionary one, but it exists nevertheless"; similar pieces ran into January and then the paper turned hard-line. The phrase "erring sisters" is not in that article; do not attach it to November 1860 [WIKI-Greeley], [LBH-Tribune]. Raymond's *Times* backed the Union by war if needed; Bennett's *Herald* blamed Republicans [MLNY-Editors]. Lincoln's private line to Kellogg on 11 December: "Entertain no proposition for a compromise in regard to the extension of slavery" and "The tug has to come, & better now than later" (the line is in the Kellogg letter, not the Trumbull letter of 10 December) [EPP-Kellogg]. New York business panicked in November 1860 (Governor Morgan to Lincoln 20 November, about $3.5 million in gold sent south in ten days); a Pine Street meeting mid-December argued for conciliation; Mayor Fernando Wood floated a free city on 7 January 1861 (6 January in some accounts); in late January conservative Republicans claimed fewer than 10,000 city voters opposed a Crittenden-style partition against 33,000 Republican votes in November [MLNY-Politics], [MLNY-Wood]. The Crittenden plan died in the Senate on 16 January (25-23 in one snippet; the Tulane page gives no tally) and finally on 4 March (19-20 or 20-19, sources disagree); the Peace Convention (from 4 February, 21 of 34 states) produced a plan the Senate rejected 28-7 [TUL-DJan16], [MLF-Trans]. **How much of the North would have let the South go: no poll or reliable estimate found.** Historians' summaries: ultra-abolitionists and some others for letting go; most Republicans for the Union by force if need be; nearly all Democrats and many Republicans for compromise (a snippet from an old general history); McClintock finds opinion split with "a wishy-washy middle" converging on preserving the Union [EVERAND-McC] (preview), [CWCOM-Andrews] (snippet).

**After 12–14 April.** Union Square, 20 April: Dix presided; Dickinson, Draper, Cooper and Wood spoke; the crowd about 100,000 (an "extra" claimed 250,000, which George Templeton Strong thought exaggerated); the Stock Exchange closed; the Chamber of Commerce raised more than $20,000 in about ten minutes [MLNY-Politics], [COLUMBIA-Sumter]. Bennett's *Herald* turned pro-Union partly because a mob threatened it and sold a record 135,000 the next day [MLNY-Editors]. Buchanan wrote to Dix on 19 April that the administration "had no alternative but to accept the war initiated by South Carolina" and "The North will sustain the administration almost to a man" [CWN-Buchanan]. Douglas addressed the Illinois legislature on 25 April and told Chicago on 1 May there were "no neutrals... only patriots or traitors"; he died on 3 June (snippet) [SEN-Douglas], [ECW-Douglas]. Quotas were oversubscribed: New York's legislature authorised 30,000 volunteers and $3m and about 47,000 enlisted by July; Ohio offered about 30,000 against 13 regiments; Indiana about twice its quota; Union County, Illinois (population 11,181) gave about 3,000 volunteers [MLNY-Politics], [OHIOCW], [ECW-Douglas].

**Lincoln's framing.** First inaugural: "The government will not assail you. You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors" [MC-Inaug]. 6 April notice: provisions only [TUL-Soln5]. 4 July message: the assault was "in no sense a matter of self-defense"; the assailants "began the conflict of arms"; the war is "essentially a people's contest" for a government that should "elevate the condition of men" [MC-July4]. The historiography is in 2.2.

**Dissent after Sumter.** Vallandigham wrote on 20 April that the South could not be coerced back and answered the 4 July message in the House on 10 July (snippet) [CWREF-Vall]. Baltimore. Press: on 16 August a New York federal grand jury presented four papers (the *Daily News*, *Journal of Commerce*, *Day Book* and *Freeman's Journal*; other sources say five or six) and the Post Office stopped carrying them [MLNY-Press], [MTSU-FA]. Wood turned against the administration within months and lost the December mayoral race to Opdyke [MLNY-Wood]. The Crittenden–Johnson resolution passed the House on 22 July (121-2 and 119-2) and the Senate on 25 July (30-5) [WIKI-CJ], [HD-CJ]. No post-Sumter Northern state election before Ohio in October was found; Connecticut's 1 April vote (Buckingham 43,012, Loomis 40,926) came before Sumter [CT-Elect].

**Foreign opinion as seen from the North.** Britain recognised belligerency on 13 May, Spain on 17 June, Brazil on 1 August; Adams reached Liverpool on 13 May [HIST-Blockade], [ACWHC-May13] (snippet).

**If Lincoln had evacuated (speculation).** The evidence in section 2.1 suggests Republican hawks would have turned on him; evacuation rumours in March drew mixed reaction, some calling it "unfortunate but unavoidable", with Buchanan Democrats and Southern-trade businessmen pleased (snippet) [TUL-Mar11-12-1]. [I] The coalition would likely have split and Davis would have had a free victory. If the North had fired first: no source; [I] the Democratic peace and border-state bloc would have had a ready story.

## 6. Sensitive history (the gate: 0021, 0026)

### 6.1 Slavery as the stated cause of secession

- South Carolina (24 Dec 1860): grievances almost all slavery [AVA-SC]. Mississippi (Jan 1861) opens by saying its position is "thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery" and closes on property "worth four billions" [AVA-MS]. Georgia (29 Jan): "African slavery", the Republican Party's anti-slavery "mission", about $3 billion of property [AVA-GA]. Texas (2 Feb): "negro slavery" and a claim that governments rest on the white race, the most explicit of the four [AVA-TX].
- Stephens, Savannah, 21 March: slavery is the "immediate cause" of the rupture and white supremacy the new government's foundation; the Founders' equality "an error" (read through a summary; Teaching American History returned 403) [BF-Cornerstone].
- The Confederate Constitution (11 March): three-fifths of "slaves" (Art. I s.2(3)); a ban on imports from outside the slave states of the Union (s.9(1)); no law impairing "the right of property in negro slaves" (s.9(4)); transit and fugitive clauses (Art. IV s.2); territories must protect "the institution of negro slavery" (s.3(3)) [AVA-CSA].
- Virginia and the upper South: the convention lost 90-45 on 4 April, passed 88-55 on 17 April after the 15 April call; slavery ran through the debates (Stuart: secession meant emancipation; Wise: slavery under threat); the ordinance says federal power was "perverted" to the "oppression of the Southern slaveholding States" [EV-Conv], [EV-Ordinance]. Governors refused the call as "coercion". [I] Fair wording: Virginia left over the call to arms against fellow slaveholding states, after the Deep South had left over slavery. The "states' rights" gloss is exactly what 0021 forbids as a cause.

### 6.3 Enslaved people acting, spring and summer 1861

- **Fort Monroe.** On the night of 23 May Frank Baker, James Townsend and Shepard Mallory crossed Hampton Roads by boat from the Sewell's Point works; Butler interviewed them on the 24th, refused their owner's agent's demand under the 1850 Act [BF-FtMonroe], [UNCP-Three]. Butler to Scott, 27 May: twelve more had escaped from Sewell's Point that morning, and depriving the Confederacy of this labour was a military necessity; he asked what to do with families [FSSP-Butler]. Cameron to Butler, 30 May: approved; do not surrender persons to "alleged masters"; employ them and keep account; final status reserved [CWN-Cameron530]. Butler to Cameron, 30 July: about 900 people (300 able-bodied men, 30 old or infirm men, 175 women, 225 children under 10, 170 aged 10–18), "many more" coming [CWM-Butler900]. Cameron to Butler, 8 August (outside window): apply the Confiscation Act to fugitives of disloyal owners, record names, do not entice [CWN-Cameron808].
- **The First Confiscation Act** (6 August, outside window; 12 Stat. 319, s.4) makes a claimant who lets a person "held to labor" serve against the US forfeit "his claim to such labor". It does not declare anyone free; an NPS page's "therefore free" overstates it [FSSP-ConAct], [NPS-Contraband] (snippet).
- **No public policy.** Lincoln privately backed Butler; other officers continued to return fugitives [FONER-Guardian]. McClellan's 26 May proclamation to western Virginians promised no interference with slavery and to "crush any attempt at insurrection" [CWN-McCProc]. On 22–23 April Butler offered Massachusetts troops to Maryland's authorities against a threatened slave insurrection; Hicks declined (snippet) [FSSP-Hicks]. Alexandria: troops freed people in a slave pen after 24 May and refugees arrived through 1861 (snippet) [PBS-Contra]. Confederate payrolls show enslaved carpenters and labourers at Fort Sumter in 1861 and enslaved men building the Charleston batteries (snippet) [NPS-Payrolls]. No source found for fugitive returns at Cairo, Wheeling or by Patterson before July: UNVERIFIED. Washington had 3,185 enslaved and 11,131 free Black residents; Camp Barker and DC emancipation belong to 1862 [CENSUS-WIKI], [CBM-Barker] (snippet).
- **Lincoln, 4 March.** "No purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere" with slavery in states; read the fugitive clause as meant for fugitive slaves; no objection to a Corwin-type amendment made "express and irrevocable" [AVA-Inaug]. Corwin passed Congress 2 March (Senate 24-12 or 24-8; House 133-65); some states ratified (Ohio and Maryland per a snippet) [MLF-Trans], [PHIL-Corwin] (snippet).

### 6.4 Black Northerners and enlistment

Offers in April: a Boston mass meeting at Twelfth Baptist Church; two regiments organised in Philadelphia; a Providence company told to stop drilling; in Washington Jacob Dodson offered 300 free Black men (letter 23 April per a snippet); Ohio's governor and the Massachusetts legislature refused [EREN-IFreed], [OR-III1-107] (snippet). Cameron's reply wording not found; the offer was refused (snippet). The 1792 Militia Act limited service to "free able-bodied white male" citizens, though the 1861 exclusion rested more on policy than statute; the 1862 Militia Act changed it [MIL-1792], [WORNALL]. Douglass's September 1861 editorial: fighting the rebels "with only one hand" (title to be checked in Foner vol. 3) [FDPP-9130] (snippet). Lincoln worried that enlisting Black men would push Kentucky and Missouri out; in December 1861 he cut Cameron's arming passage; nothing shows a Lincoln decision on the 1861 offers [EREN-IFreed], [VIRGINIA-USCT].

### 6.5 Native nations

Union posts in Indian Territory were abandoned: Emory left Fort Washita in mid-April, Arbuckle was evacuated 3 May, Cobb about 9 May. Pike's treaties: Creek 10 July, Choctaw–Chickasaw 12 July, Seminole 1 August (Cherokee 7 October, outside the window, signed by John Ross after much negotiation). Ross favoured neutrality; Stand Watie was commissioned colonel in July (snippets). Opothleyahola led Upper Creek loyalists, with Black Creeks and enslaved people, toward Kansas; Round Mountain 19 November, Chusto-Talasah 9 December, Chustenahlah 26 December (all outside the window). The Dakota War is 1862 (0026) and was not researched here [OHS-Arbuckle], [OHS-Pike], [OHS-Watie], [OHS-Opo].

### 6.6 Civil liberties

27 April: Lincoln's order to Scott to suspend habeas corpus on or near the Philadelphia–Washington line [WIKS-Habeas]. Merryman: arrested 25 May; writ 26 May; General Cadwalader refused; the marshal was turned away 28 May (Taney's oral ruling the same day); Taney's written opinion filed 1 June; Merryman indicted 10 July (the first brief's 28 May was the oral ruling); bail 13 July (12 July in one source) [WIKI-Merryman], [NPS-Writ]. 4 July message: "are all the laws, but one" to go unexecuted [QUOD-July4]. Baltimore: Kane arrested 27 June on Banks's order (1 July in Kane's own letter); the police board's authority was suspended and members arrested on 1 July (snippet); Mayor Brown and 31–33 legislators arrested 11–12 September, Brown held at Fort McHenry, then Fort Monroe, then Fort Warren, released 27 November 1862 [BPM-Kane], [NPS-PolPris], [CHESOB-Sep], [WIKI-Brown]. Press: the August grand-jury presentment (section 5); mob attacks found are the Baltimore *Wecker* (19 April) and a crowd at a Philadelphia paper in mid-April; Concord NH is 8 August; no source for a general April wave of mob attacks, so do not state one [MLNY-Press], [MTSU-FA].

### 6.7 Violence against civilians, 1861

Camp Jackson: at least 28 civilians killed, riots for two days, then martial law; blame fell on Union troops [MO-Timeline], [ECW-CJ]. Pratt Street: 12 civilians and 4–5 soldiers per McPherson, the NPS giving 11 dead on one page and 16 on another. Missouri sabotage and executions in July (3.5). Sustained guerrilla war and Lane's Osceola raid are September and later [BF-Bush].

## Design implications

### Premise check against 0028

1. **The war does start whatever the player does.** Davis's cabinet decided on 9 April to attack Sumter, before the relief fleet could arrive [CWM-Apr9], [TUL-Apr10]. Without a relief attempt, [I] the garrison would have run out of bread within days of 12–15 April (Anderson: a week from 1 April, or noon 15 April on his own terms), so the Confederate choice is to fire or let them go; this is speculation, not sourced.
2. **"Who is seen to fire first" is a weaker lever than 0028's wording implies.** The player's real choices are (a) evacuate or send a relief expedition, (b) whether to give notice (the 6 April message made the attack the Confederates' choice), and (c) timing. The firing itself is the Confederate seat's decision, historically already taken. Model it as the scenario's labelled railroad, with the player's framing choices changing its political terms, not its occurrence.
3. **"Which border states secede" needs a distinction.** The four states that left (Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, Tennessee) left over the 15 April call for 75,000, not over Sumter alone: Virginia rejected secession 90-45 on 4 April and approved it 88-55 on 17 April; the refusals by their governors cite coercion [EV-Conv], [WIKI-75k]. The four that stayed (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri) are Mandate's "border states" with divided control. So the player's lever on the upper South is the shape of the call (size, form, timing, whether Congress is called sooner), and it is a separate situation from Sumter.

### Mechanics the research suggests

- **Staff recommendation differs from the historical default** in S1 (and S2 for Scott): a ready demonstration of 0023's two fields.
- **Seat-held levers** reappear: Kentucky's Military Board, Missouri's convention, Maryland's legislature, Maryland's police board, Virginia's convention.
- **The call (S5) is the main border-state lever**, not Sumter. Quotas for DE, MD, KY and MO (4 each for the three large ones, 1 for Delaware) were refused by two governors on the day.
- **Latency and the telegraph.** Baltimore cut the wire 19–20 April; the Chesapeake route to Annapolis was the line; Washington was isolated until 25 April [WIKI-Baltimore], [MSA-Hicks4].
- **A "who holds the arsenal" mechanic**: Harpers Ferry (burned), St. Louis (Lyon), Springfield (UNVERIFIED), Gosport (burned).

## Findings outside my scope

- **A Confederate-side situation set is needed** (Davis's cabinet vote of 9 April, Beauregard's orders, the upper South's secession conventions) before the scenario can show "who fires first" from the other seat; 0026 already requires a record before a playable Confederacy. The Union pack alone can only receive it as an event.
- **The navy through people** (0028): Welles, Fox, Stringham, Du Pont's Strategy Board and the 373 of 1,554 officers who resigned are the raw material for the Navy Department posts. A short naval research lane (Welles–Fox–Stringham, the purchase programme, the board) would fill the gap; the Navy Department is barely covered here.
- **The War Department seat** (Cameron: understaffed, patronage, July contract demands refused, Thomas A. Scott's railway role) is the first "stalling holder" the economy design needs; the corruption story belongs in a research note of its own.
- **The existing Union dossier** (`2026-11-14-union-1861-62.md`) has two items this note corrects: Scott's "Anaconda" was a 3 May letter to McClellan and the label came later; and its section 4 region table starts Maryland at "US (secessionist sympathy)", while this note proposes splitting Baltimore.
- **Verification debt:** the primary texts (Lincoln letters, OR tables) need a lane with working access to quod.lib.umich.edu and the Official Records scans before the pack cites them. The quota table in the OR (Series III vol. 1) is the first priority.
- **Indian Territory** (Union posts abandoned April–May; Pike's treaties July–August) has no Union lever in 1861 and is outside the first slice (0026), but a Phase 1 situation for the Indian Territory fracture and Opothleyahola would sit right after this window.
- **An executive-only window.** Congress was out from 4 March to 4 July. The 15 April and 3 May calls, the blockade and the habeas order were all presidential acts later ratified (Act of 6 August, snippet). The scenario needs a "Congress not sitting" state that changes which actions are legal and what Lincoln must answer for on 4 July; it is not modelled by any current design section.
- **Source working files.** The six sweeps' working notes are in the session scratchpad only (not in the repo, not committed); the note carries everything used.
