## How to read the fields

The eleven 0121 terms are the CSV's `issuer`, `holder`, `actions`,
`subordinates`, `regions`, `movement_modes`, `ceiling`, `guard`, `expiry`,
`reporting_duties` and `onward_grant`. These contain source-language summaries,
not proposed kernel verbs or resolved map IDs. Additional columns distinguish
the paper's date, receipt evidence, record status, page and limits.

An empty field means **the instrument read does not supply that field**, or
the original was not read. It means neither unlimited power nor an affirmative
historical prohibition. Under 0121 item 4, an executable mandate denies what
it does not name; the lead must review a separate, explicitly bounded pack
declaration. A blank historical field cannot silently become a permissive
default. An appointed command, a specific operational permission, a staff
transmission and permission to grant onward are different evidence.

`receipt_date` is accompanied by its evidence. Some dates are only upper bounds
from a dated acknowledgment or publication. They are not universal delivery
dates, witnessed arrival hours or proof of assent. In particular, a request
to forward a letter is not the subordinate's receipt. The five R rows leave
the missing original's substantive fields empty even where a later writer
describes what he understood. No unidentified earlier paper has been assigned
an invented authored identity.

## Union: Scott, Patterson and Wallace

### Scott's office and Lincoln's written directions

**U01.** The presidential General Orders of 5 July 1841 recite Scott's
appointment, with the Senate's advice and consent, as Major-General of the
Army of the United States and direct him to assume command and enter his
duties. That command text was read in Richardson, *Messages and Papers of the
Presidents*, IV, **p.73**. The underlying commission was not read (R01), nor
was a complete sequence of intervening command instruments. The 1841 text
contains no enumerated 1861 operational powers, regions, numerical ceiling,
guard, expiry, reporting duties or express permission to grant onward.

**U02.** The 1857 Regulations' approval leaf is unnumbered, dated 1 January,
and says the President commands observance. The read rank provisions say a
commission alone does not put an officer on duty and require special
assignment for brevet command of regularly constituted commands, including
departments and the army (**pp.2-3, paragraphs 7-14**). They are evidence
against filling Scott's mandate from rank alone. They are not his individual
assignment. The orders rules allow letters, identify the commander behind a
staff signature, require intermediate transmission and bypass reporting, and
require copies of orders to the Adjutant-General (**pp.57-60, paragraphs
422-447**). They support recognizing Townsend and Porter as transmitters when
the paper says so, rather than making them independent granting principals.

**U03-U06.** Lincoln's 25 April letter actually addresses Scott as General in
Chief. It rejects the proposed arrest or dispersion of the Maryland
legislature, directs watching its action, and conditionally permits extreme
countermeasures if it arms people against the United States (Richardson VI,
**pp.17-18**). Scott's 26 April transmission says he has received presidential
instructions, reproduces the operative passage, and turns it over in his
absence to Butler or the officer commanding at Annapolis, with moderation
and firmness (OR I-II **pp.601-602**). That establishes possession **by**
26 April and an actual downstream transmission, not Butler's receipt or a
general written presidential permission to subdelegate military operations.

The 27 April direction expressly permits Scott personally, or through the
officer commanding at a point of resistance, to suspend habeas corpus when
public safety requires it along a military line between Philadelphia and
Washington. The 20 June direction permits him, or an officer he designates,
to suspend the writ as it relates to Major Chase (Richardson VI **pp.18-19**).
These are affirmative written delegations with expressly described downstream
exercise. Their subject is specific civil-security power; they supply no
military sortie menu or blanket grant over all operations. S117 makes no
judgment of their legality and proposes none of these powers as gameplay.

**U27.** The War Department's 20 June letter to John Sherman counsels reliance
on the General-in-Chief in campaign plans, movements and commands. The printed
copy ends without a signature (OR I-II **pp.710-711**). It is useful
institutional context, but cannot be promoted to a signed Lincoln-to-Scott
root grant or a witnessed dispatch.

**Finding [interpretation]:** the read material establishes command-office
and specific presidential-direction evidence. It does **not** supply a
complete, received, enumerated 0121 military root. No general 1861 written
delegation to Scott with all those fields was located in this bounded reading;
that is not a claim that none ever existed. The lead must decide the bounded
root declaration supported by this packet, with gaps left explicit.

### Patterson's department and operational limits

**U07-U09.** Scott's Headquarters Army GO3 of 19 April assigns Patterson the
enlarged Department of Washington and directs posting mustered Pennsylvania
volunteers along the Wilmington-Washington communications route (OR I-II
**p.579**). War Department GO12 of 27 April creates the Department of
Pennsylvania, assigns Patterson, and defines it as Pennsylvania, Delaware and
Maryland outside the Washington and Annapolis departments. It gives no troop
roster or numerical ceiling. Virginia is not included in its territorial
definition (OR I-II **p.607**).

There is a real boundary discrepancy. GO12 defines the Annapolis exclusion as
**twenty miles** on each side of the Annapolis-Washington railroad to
Bladensburg. Patterson's GO23 of 29 April invokes the Secretary of War but
announces **twelve miles**, and describes Maryland east of Bladensburg (OR I-II
**pp.607,611**). Neither spelling is silently repaired. His announcement is
an administrative witness by 29 April, not proof of an exact receipt time or
a new presidential authorization. No resolving amendment was read.

**U10-U13, U16-U17.** Subsequent papers provide operational scope distinct
from the department boundary:

- Scott's 24 May direction covers the remaining Pennsylvania and New Jersey
  quotas toward Frederick, Hagerstown and Cumberland, to threaten Harpers
  Ferry and support Union sentiment in western Virginia; Cumberland must
  have a self-relying garrison if occupied. Patterson acknowledges the
  same-day telegrams (OR I-II **p.652**).
- Patterson's 29 May directions through Porter to Keim organize successive
  columns, specify marching and rail movements and three days' provisions,
  and withhold forward movement before Patterson arrives unless imperative
  circumstances require it, to be telegraphed (OR I-II **pp.658-659**).
  This shows subordinate task direction; it does not expressly let Keim create
  further standing mandates.
- Scott on 1 June permits neglecting Cumberland meanwhile if Harpers Ferry
  is the first object, requests free reporting and crossing readiness, and
  offers one battery. Patterson's resulting Martinsburg/Winchester plan is
  a proposal, not the contents of Scott's telegram (OR I-II **pp.657-658**).
- Townsend's 4 June direction by Scott prohibits forward movement until the
  specified battery and five Third Infantry companies join. Patterson on
  7 June recognizes a no-forward-movement restraint; it is a related
  acknowledgment, not a uniquely itemized receipt (OR I-II **pp.665,668-669**).
- Scott's 8 June letter calls the expedition well projected but requires a
  clear prospect of success and avoidance of reverse, check or drawn battle,
  plus practicable communication with Stone (OR I-II **pp.670-671**). It
  does not expressly cancel the 4 June prerequisite. No separate receipt
  date was found.
- The 13 June letter says not to hasten and to keep Scott informed for
  coordination. Its 17/18 June crossing supposition is no calendar grant.
  Patterson acknowledges a 13th note on the 14th, without uniquely separating
  the two 13th papers (OR I-II **pp.680,683**).

**U18-U23.** Authority over troops changes again. Scott's 16 June 11 a.m.
letter conditionally recalls regulars and Rhode Islanders if there is no
pursuit; his separate same-day letter challenges the Winchester detachment,
requires sending the troops as disengaged, and says to keep within the stated
limits until convinced otherwise. The urgent 17 June demand follows
(OR I-II **pp.691-696**). Patterson's 16 June letter acknowledges the recall
and asks to retain troops; his 18 June account describes receipt of the limits
and his withdrawal direction. A subordinate's request to retain troops is not
itself a permission (OR I-II **pp.693,701**).

The 18 June 9.15 p.m. qualification permits keeping siege guns and Perkins's
battery and discusses retaining regulars pending assessment of Johnston's
reported movement. Patterson explicitly receives that message at **12.15 p.m.
on 19 June** (OR I-II **pp.703,707**). It cannot authorize an earlier action
retroactively. Scott's 19 June 9.45 p.m. letter permits keeping **two** Thomas
cavalry companies and sends the other two with Thomas; their selection and
receipt date are not shown (OR I-II **p.708**). This is a specific resource
limit, not a ceiling on Patterson's whole army.

Scott's 20 June letter orders examination of Maryland Heights and requests a
plan for coordinated Leesburg-Alexandria operations, contemplating absorption
of Stone and cover for the Heights detachment. Patterson acknowledges it at
midnight and submits his proposal. The proposal's 2,100 men and twenty days'
stores are not a read grant from Scott (OR I-II **pp.709,711-712**). The
catalogue does not turn a requested plan into authorization to execute it.

### Wallace's standing authority and later directions

**U14.** The actual 6 June paper is Porter writing explicitly by Patterson's
instructions to Wallace, Eleventh Indiana, at Cumberland (OR I-II **p.668**).
Its military terms are to halt, secure the two named bridges, gather reliable
information, and, on learning of offensive armed gatherings that are not too
strong for a force he can safely detach, capture or rout them, by surprise if
possible. Reporting is full and continuing as opportunities permit. No
numerical detachment ceiling, numerical guard, transport mode, fixed duration
or permission to grant onward is stated. The bridge and station duties remain
part of the same letter.

It also directs political encouragement, local subsistence inquiries and
arrests of described persons. Those clauses are recorded as historical
content, not executable authority under 0121. They are not silently omitted
from the research or imported into the kernel. No target-specific superior
order to raid Romney was read.

Wallace's 11 June letter acknowledges dispatches delivered that morning by
Closson. Patterson's same-day report identifies Wallace as acting under his
instructions of the 6th (OR I-II **pp.676,105**). Together these support a
qualified association with the standing authority; the acknowledgment is not
an itemized list proving receipt of that uniquely identified enclosure.
Wallace's statement that he has over 800 effective men is a reported whole
command strength, not the letter's detachment ceiling (OR I-II **p.676**).

**U15, U24-U26.** Thomas's separate 7 June letter forbids responding to appeals
to cross into Virginia and permits overpowering nearby Maryland inroads,
with return to camp in every case (OR I-II **p.670**). Its express return
clause must not be attributed to Wallace's differently worded letter.
Wallace's bracket-dated 16 June instruction permits judging when to move and
maintaining a bold front (OR I-II **p.694**). The 21 June recall, forwarded
through McCall, leaves his route to his judgment; the 24 June reiteration
requires the shortest route unless strong unanticipated reasons oppose it
(OR I-II **pp.712,717**). Neither gives his receipt or actual arrival. These
are separate papers with potentially changing scope after receipt.

## Confederate instruments and the government replies

**C01-C04.** Letcher's 23 April order gives Lee command in chief of all
Virginia military and naval forces and State defenses; Lee's same-day GO1
expressly obeys it (OR I-II **pp.775-776**). Walker's 10 May telegram, published
in Lee's GO13 as received that day, separately gives control of Confederate
forces in Virginia and assignment of their duties **until further orders**
(OR I-II **p.827**). That is an express duty-assignment power, not an express
power to authorize recipients to grant further standing mandates. No
President-to-Walker grant was read.

Letcher's proclamation is dated **6 June**, published in GO25 on **8 June**.
It transfers specified Virginia forces and directs the Provisional Army to
obey lawful orders of the President and those acting under his authority.
Lee's appended direction transfers command and points military, naval and
staff officers to the respective Confederate departments (OR I-II
**pp.911-912**). Publication does not establish receipt at every command.
The State and national grants, and this later transfer, remain separate;
S117 does not backdate national authority to Lee's April State appointment.

**C05-C06; R02.** Johnston's 24 May assumption order invokes orders of the
Secretary of War and names troops at and near Harpers Ferry, with McLean
and Whiting directing quartermaster and engineer operations. Johnston asks
Jackson to distribute it. Jackson acknowledges receipt of the morning note
but refuses publication or transfer pending Letcher's or Lee's instructions
(OR I-II **pp.871-872**). The original Secretary of War assignment was not
recovered: its date and terms remain blank, rather than reconstructed from
Johnston's order. Johnston's October report dates assumption to **23 May**,
whereas the assumption paper is dated the **24th** (OR I-II **pp.470,871**).
Both witnesses stand; no invented receipt or automatic acceptance resolves
them.

**C07-C09.** Lee's 30 May letter suggests a joint attack with Bonham if a
rear threat, inability to hold and Johnston's judgment warrant it; it refers
to Preston's verbal explanations, which were not read (OR I-II **p.894**).
The main 1 June letter directs concentration, withdrawal and stepwise
resistance if the position cannot be defended against attack, and discretion
against a threat to the rear (OR I-II **p.897**). On 7 June Lee conveys the
President's priority of retaining Harpers Ferry and the Valley, but allows
retirement if the approaching force cannot be successfully opposed, leaving
discretion and judgment for safety (OR I-II **p.910**). No separate written
Davis enclosure or holder receipt dates were read. Johnston is not thereby
given command of Bonham, Garnett or McDonald.

**C10.** Cooper's 13 June letter explicitly leaves judgment of necessities
and means to Johnston. It conditionally authorizes destruction and retirement
toward Winchester when the enemy's turning movement threatens the command;
it then discusses attacking when supported or slow retirement toward the
Manassas road and defensive passes when not. It also explains the danger of
uncovering the Valley by junction with Beauregard (OR I-II **pp.923-925**).
Neither that warning nor the discretion is an unconditional junction order.
Johnston's 15 June acknowledgment names the letter of the 13th and calls it
just received (OR I-II **pp.929-930**).

**C11; R03-R04.** Cooper's 18 June reply reaffirms action as circumstances
require within the general purpose of resisting invasion and repelling the
invader, and requires frequent full reports on position and contemplated
movement. Its continuation requires vigilance and reconnaissance and prompt
reliable enemy information (OR I-II **pp.934-935**). This reads **p.935 beyond
S99's stated stopping point**, rather than extending S99's receipt by
assumption. It refers to a letter of **15 June** whose text was not read.
The 13 June letter's reference to earlier instructions is also not a uniquely
identified instrument.

The chronology remains qualified: Johnston's 17 June letter says instructions
were received on the 15th, without uniquely identifying the 13th or the
separately referenced 15th paper. His October report places a discretionary
dispatch's courier on the 16th. Cooper on the 18th explicitly acknowledges
receipt **at government** of Johnston's 17th letter; that is not Johnston's
receipt of Cooper's reply (OR I-II **pp.472,929-930,934**). No exact new
holder receipt is fabricated to remove these discrepancies.

## Proposed treatment for the lead [interpretation]

1. Review a bounded root from the office/assignment and operational evidence;
   keep the root declaration visibly separate from the historical catalogue.
   Reject rank alone, an unsigned third-party copy, a civil-security
   delegation or the existence of an issued child order as a substitute for
   all missing root fields. This packet does not accept a root for the lead.
2. Keep numerical ceilings, guard requirements, candidate limits, map scope
   and task duration as separately justified build proposals where the source
   supplies none. Reject converting Wallace's reported strength or a later
   plan's garrison into a sourced numerical mandate. Keep every existing
   acceptance criterion and source hold open until its own evidence meets it.
3. Preserve both boundary/date witnesses and the unrecovered originals. Reject
   silently choosing the convenient geography, matching unlike papers by date,
   treating a sent order as received, or treating receipt as assent. No new
   coverage, 1A, strength, safety or performance result follows from S117.
