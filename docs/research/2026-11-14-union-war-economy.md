# Research: the Union war economy, 1861–65 (money, men, munitions, rail, and who held each lever)

Date: 2026-11-14. Status: research note (not canon). Written by a research lane for the lead, for Phase 1's light economy (0023: money, men, munitions, rail capacity, each with a holder who can stall; 0033: new regiments and refilling old ones are separate levers). Union side only, with most detail for 1861–62.

**Conventions.**
- Every claim ends with a key in [brackets] that resolves to a URL in the Sources list.
- **(pdf-read)** = this lane downloaded the original PDF from FRASER (St. Louis Fed) and read its text layer. **(fetched)** = the page was read through the fetch tool, which returns a machine summary, not the raw page, so figures from such pages are leads to verify before a pack cites them. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result summary.
- **[I]** = our arithmetic or inference. **UNVERIFIED** = asked for and not found.
- The Treasury, Provost Marshal General (Fry) and Quartermaster figures are public-domain primary sources (0033's sourcing rule). Wikipedia and web articles are used to check and to fill gaps; none of their text is copied.
- Fiscal years run 1 July to 30 June. "FY1863" means July 1862 to June 1863.

**Does the evidence contradict the settled design?** No. It sharpens three things: (1) the Treasury could not make money by decree, because the big instruments (greenbacks, taxes, bonds) each needed an act of Congress, so "money" has two holders (Treasury and Congress); (2) the governors' real power was authorising *new* regiments, which is exactly the split 0033 asks for; (3) rail was not a stock but a throughput that private railroad presidents, not the War Department, mostly owned.

### 1.2 Burn rate (how fast the money went)

- Payments (excluding debt principal) were $111.1m in the quarter to 30 September 1862 (about $37m a month), $49.2m in October and $59.8m in November 1862 [TR62] (pdf-read).
- Yearly averages: about $40m a month in FY1862, $60m in FY1863, $72m in FY1864, $108m in FY1865 [I] from the table.
- Chase's FY1863 estimates, made in December 1862, missed in both directions: spending $693.3m estimated against $714.7m actual, and internal revenue $85.5m estimated against $37.6m actual, a 56% miss [TR63]. Early revenue forecasts were unreliable; the player's "treasury forecast" should be wrong by tens of percent.

### 1.3 Sequence of decisions, and who stalled or helped

**Before Chase (the inheritance).** Under Buchanan the Treasury borrowed small sums at high rates: a $10m note issue of December 1860 drew bids for only $500,000 at 12% and ended with banks taking it at 12% [CWE-1861] (fetched). Chase found under $2m in the Treasury [ALC-FIN] (fetched).

**1861: bank syndicate, demand notes, specie.**
- Chase's own early offers sold poorly: on 2 April 1861 he offered $8m and placed $3.1m at an average price of 94 [CWE-1861] (fetched).
- Congress authorised a $250m loan on 17 July 1861, including $50m of non-interest demand notes [CWE-1861], [WP-GREEN] (fetched).
- New York, Boston and Philadelphia banks took $50m of 7.30% three-year notes in August 1861, another $50m on 1 October and $50m of 6% twenty-year bonds on 1 December at 89.8; the public appeal through about 500 agents sold far less than hoped [CWE-1861] (fetched).
- The banks suspended specie payments on 28–30 December 1861, because Chase's rule that the Treasury take and spend coin had drained their reserves [CWE-1861], [ALC-FIN] (fetched). **Stall:** Chase insisted on specie and would not sell bonds below par; Montgomery Blair criticised his delays [ALC-FIN] (fetched).

**1862: greenbacks.**
- The Legal Tender Act of 25 February 1862 authorised $150m of United States notes and $500m of six-percent "five-twenty" bonds [TR62] (pdf-read), [WP-GREEN] (fetched). Chase disliked legal tender in principle and "came with reluctance to the conclusion" that it was a necessity [CWM-LT] (fetched). **Helped:** Spaulding and Stevens in the House, Fessenden in the Senate, Lincoln who signed it [CWM-LT] (fetched). **Opposed:** most Democrats, led by George Pendleton [CWM-LT] (fetched). Senate vote 30–7 (index of Spaulding's history, snippet) [SPAULD-SNIP]; House 93–54 (same snippet; another count gives 59 noes, unresolved).
- Two further $150m issues followed (July 1862, early 1863), capping the total at $450m [ALC-FIN] (fetched). Chase's December 1862 report puts US notes issued or in circulation at $222.9m against a $250m limit at that date [TR62] (pdf-read). By 30 June 1864, $431.2m were out against a $450m limit [TR64] (pdf-read).
- **Bonds stalled.** In December 1862 Chase wrote that selling five-twenties at their market value was "regarded as impracticable" and that only $23.75m had come in, by holders of greenbacks exchanging them at par [TR62] (pdf-read).

**1862–64: bond drive.**
- Chase made Jay Cooke special agent for the five-twenties in the autumn of 1862 (Cooke's appointment date, October 1862, is from a snippet) [COOKE-SNIP]; commission one-half of 1% on the first $10m and 0.375% after; about 2,500 sub-agents; the $500m was sold, plus $11m more that Congress then sanctioned [COOKE-WP] (fetched). Issued five-twenties stood at $510.8m by 30 June 1864 [TR64] (pdf-read). A search summary says the loan closed oversubscribed in January 1864 and that Cooke sold about $325m in the seven months after June 1863 (snippet) [COOKE-SNIP].
- So the private subscription agent, not the Treasury's own machinery, turned a stalled bond into about $500m in roughly 15 months, about $33m a month on average [I].
- In 1865 Cooke sold $830m of seven-thirty notes in about five months, February–July 1865 [COOKE-WP] (fetched).

**Taxes (Congress).**
- August 1861: tariffs raised and the first income tax enacted (3% on incomes over $800), but it was never collected in practice [ALC-FIN], [CWE-1861] (fetched). The Morrill tariff had already taken effect on 1 April 1861 [CWE-1861] (fetched).
- July 1862: the Internal Revenue Act taxed a wide range of goods and services, set income tax at 3% on $600–$10,000 and 5% above, and created the Bureau of Internal Revenue [ALC-FIN], [ECW-FIN] (fetched), [TREAS-CHASE] (fetched).
- Chase blamed the FY1863 revenue shortfall on a new law that was imperfectly executed and altered by Congress after his estimate [TR63] (pdf-read). **Congress stalled by rewriting; the Commissioner's collection machine lagged.** Receipts then tripled in FY1864 and doubled again in FY1865 (table above).
- Income tax yielded about $20m in FY1864 and $32m in FY1865 [ECW-FIN] (fetched); the same page also says "less than $20m in 1863" and "$2m in FY1863", an unresolved conflict.
- A 1864 revision raised rates on incomes over $5,000 and added an emergency tax [ECW-FIN] (fetched).

**National banks.** A national banking system (1863) created a uniform currency and tied banks to government bonds [TREAS-CHASE] (fetched). Details of its slow take-up were not researched.

**1864: the gold crisis and Chase's exit.**
- Chase resigned in mid-1864 and was replaced by William Fessenden [TREAS-CHASE] (fetched). The resignation date was not on the page.
- In June 1864 Congress passed a Gold Act (17 June, snippet) that tried to close the Gold Room and was quickly repealed [GOLD-SNIP], [BHC-GOLD] (fetched).
- Fessenden's December 1864 report: gold "in the course of a few days" rose from about $1.50 to $2.85 in paper, fell to $1.87 and rose to $2.50, which he attributed to "criminal efforts of speculators" [TR64] (pdf-read).

## 2. Men

### 2.1 The calls and the numbers

| 4 Aug 1862 | 300,000 militia for nine months by state draft | [OR-V-610] (fetched) |
| 3 Mar 1863 | Enrollment Act: draft of men 20–45 by congressional district, $300 commutation or a substitute | [WP-ENR] (fetched) |
| Oct 1863–Dec 1864 | Calls of 17 Oct 1863 (300,000), 1 Feb 1864 (to 500,000), 14 Mar 1864 (200,000), 18 Jul 1864 (500,000), 19 Dec 1864 (300,000) | [OR-IV-1264], dates by search summary (snippet) [CALLS-SNIP] |

- All calls, 15 April 1861 to 14 April 1865: quotas $2,759,049; men credited into the Army, Navy and Marine Corps 2,656,533 (one transcription reads 2,656,553); deficiency 102,496 when the war closed [OR-V-519] (fetched). These are *enlistments*; re-enlisting veterans are counted again, so individuals were fewer: at least 2,128,948 men served [WP-UA] (fetched), about 2 million [NPS-WFF] (pdf-read).
- The 75,000-man call was easily met, including over 10,000 German-Americans in New York and Pennsylvania [WP-UA] (fetched). Many three-month men re-enlisted for three years [WP-UA] (fetched).
- A search summary says the July 1862 call for 300,000 "actually produced 421,000 new troops" (snippet) [ENC-UNION-SNIP].
- The August 1862 militia draft: about 87,000 of the 300,000 called were credited as drafted; desertion before they could be sent from their states reduced the number, and "only a small portion actually joined the Army" [OR-V-610] (fetched).
- The last call, 19 December 1864 (300,000), is the one call broken down in the Official Records: 194,635 raised, of which volunteers 157,058 (white 130,620, Black 10,055, regulars 6,958, seamen 9,106, Marines 319) and drafted men and substitutes 37,577 (drafted men held to service 12,566; substitutes for drafted men 12,014; substitutes for enrolled men 12,997) [OR-V-519] (fetched). So the draft supplied about 19% of that call [I].
- Over the whole war "barely 2%" of Union soldiers were actual draftees, because of bounties and substitutes [NPS-WFF] (pdf-read).
- Strength on the rolls (aggregate, from Livermore via the war-scale note): July 1861 186,751; January 1862 575,917; spring 1862 637,126; 1 January 1863 918,121; 1 January 1864 860,737; 1 January 1865 959,460 [WARSCALE] (cites Livermore p47). Net change per month [I]: about +65,000 (Jul 1861–Jan 1862); about +20,000 (Jan–spring 1862, then recruiting closed); about +31,000 (spring 1862–Jan 1863); about −5,000 (1863, as nine-month and two-year terms ran out); about +8,000 (1864). The share of the rolls actually present fell from about 92% to about 67% between January 1862 and March 1865 [WARSCALE].
- The regular army had 16,367 men in April 1861; only 62,000 regular commissions and enlistments were issued in the whole war because recruits preferred volunteer service [WP-UA] (fetched). The regulars are a small lever.
- Monthly or quarterly enlistment series: **UNVERIFIED.** Fry's Final Report (1875) and Shannon's *Organization and Administration of the Union Army* are the places to look; Shannon was not readable.

### 2.2 How a call became regiments: the governors

- Early war: volunteer regiments were raised by the states. Field and company officers were commissioned by the President or the governor, and company officers were usually elected by the company [WP-UA] (fetched). Localities contributed unequally "due to differing patriotism and other causes" [OR-V-610] (fetched).
- Quotas went by population, with credit for men already furnished; governors then apportioned quotas among counties and subdivisions [OR-V-609], [OR-V-610] (fetched). Records of prior contributions were incomplete, causing disputes with the Provost Marshal General's Bureau later [OR-V-610] (fetched).
- **Governors authorised new organisations in July–August 1862 even though the War Department wanted old regiments filled.** The Provost Marshal General's report says special efforts to recruit the old regiments failed and that the July call "would be filled mainly by new organisations", because of "misapprehension of the real needs of the service" and a belief that individual recruiting would not happen without commissions in new regiments [OR-V-609] (fetched).
- Stanton wrote New York on 11 August 1862 that its quota had been filled only for new regiments and cited a need for 54,120 men in its old ones (snippet) [OLDNEW-SNIP].
- On 1 November 1862 Curtin objected to filling old regiments with drafted men, because regiments elected their own officers and wanted to stay distinct; the order sending drafted men to old regiments had been issued at McClellan's urgent request [OR-II-714] (fetched).
- October 1863: Fry revoked Ohio's authority to raise more regiments (2 October), and told the governors of Maine and Vermont on 21 October that no new regiments would be authorised (snippet) [OLDNEW-SNIP], [OR-III-859] (fetched). His letter sets out the bounties: $100 for a new man in a new regiment, $300 for a new man in an old regiment, $400 for an old soldier, and says Ohio's Governor Tod was "recruiting exclusively for old regiments" [OR-III-859] (fetched).
- December 1864: Governor Brough asked to raise ten new regiments; Stanton said he preferred filling old regiments but would grant it if field commanders agreed; Morton ordered eleven one-year regiments, one per congressional district [OR-IV-1013] (fetched).
- State bounties ran $100–$500 to new volunteers [NPS-WFF] (pdf-read). Bounty jumping (collect, desert, re-enlist elsewhere) was common (snippet) [IA-BOUNTY].
- The Altoona governors' conference (September 1862) bargained quotas; see the 1861–62 note [UNION-1861-62].

### 2.3 The draft, substitutes and desertion

- The Enrollment Act's section 13 let a drafted man hire an acceptable substitute or pay up to $300 and be discharged from that draft [COMMUT] (pdf-read). A July 1864 amendment limited commutation to conscientious objectors, so after that the only way out was a substitute, which poorer men could not afford: in Cincinnati's First District in March 1864, 18% of drawn labourers paid commutation and 4% hired substitutes; in the last two 1864 calls only 6% of labourers could hire one against 22% of professionals [COMMUT] (pdf-read).
- Quoted outcomes of draft drawings: 249,259 names drawn in total with about 6% actually serving (secondary, search summary, snippet) [IA-BOUNTY]. A forum figure puts substitutes at 116,188 and a National Cemetery Administration piece gives 73,607 substitutes between March 1863 and April 1865 (snippet; unresolved) [DRAFT-SNIP].
- The draft's main effect was to push states to pay bounties and fill quotas so as to avoid it (Fry's design: the draft covers any deficiency against the quota) [WP-ENR] (fetched).
- **Desertion** (Fry): 260,339 reported as deserters from the army; Fry cuts that by 25% to about 195,255 actual; 76,253 arrested by his Bureau, 1,775 surrendered under the 11 March 1865 proclamation, leaving 117,247 at large; plus 161,286 drafted men who failed to report, of whom he treats about 112,901 as deserters [OR-V-109] (fetched). Another compilation prints 278,644 and about 201,000 (snippet) [DESERT-SNIP]. Against about 2.1 million men, 195,000 is about 9%; Lonn's "one in seven" uses a different base (snippet) [DESERT-SNIP].

### 2.4 Lags (men)

- April 1861: Minnesota's governor offered a regiment on 14 April, it mustered in on 29 April and fought at Bull Run on 21 July [WP-1MN] (fetched): 15 days to muster, about 14 weeks to a battle.
- July 1862: the 20th Maine mustered in on 29 August 1862, 58 days after the 2 July call, and was held in reserve at Antietam in September [WP-20ME] (fetched). One regiment each, so only a band.
- Militia draft: called 4 August 1862, drawing began 3 September (30 days), and most of the paper total never reached the army [OR-V-610] (fetched).
- Enrollment Act signed 3 March 1863; the New York drawing was in July 1863 and the riots ran 13–16 July [WP-ENR] (fetched): about four months from law to first big draft.

## 3. Munitions and supply

### 3.1 The Ordnance Department and Ripley

- Brigadier General James W. Ripley was Chief of Ordnance from 1861 until 15 September 1863, when he was replaced "in disobedience to his orders from President Lincoln" over his resistance to breech-loaders [WP-RIPLEY] (fetched).
- He refused to buy more rifle-muskets for infantry, arguing that stocks of smoothbores could be re-rifled, and strongly opposed repeating rifles on the ground that they would waste ammunition [WP-RIPLEY] (fetched). His letter of 9 December 1861 to Cameron opposed adopting the Spencer (snippet) [SPENCER-SNIP]. The Henry was rejected for its weight and special cartridge; the government bought only 1,731 [ECC-ARMS] (fetched).
- Lincoln test-fired the Spencer in Washington on or about 17–18 August 1863 and pressed the department to adopt it (snippet) [SPENCER-SNIP]. The government bought over 95,000 Spencer carbines [ECC-ARMS] (fetched); Ordnance totals of 106,667 Spencer rifles and carbines are also quoted, with deliveries into 1866 [WP-RIPLEY] (fetched). Historians dispute whether this resistance prolonged the war, because Union logistics may not have supported higher rates of fire [WP-RIPLEY] (fetched).
- **How Ripley could stall:** by not buying (rifle-muskets, repeaters), by condemning and selling old stock (he ordered 5,000 Hall's carbines sold, which were resold to Frémont at a profit) [WP-RIPLEY] (fetched).

### 3.2 Arms supply: numbers

- Springfield Armory made 9,601 rifles in 1860 and 276,200 in 1864 (a 29-fold rise; Wikipedia's prose says "25-fold") [WP-SPRING] (fetched). It made 265,129 Model 1861 rifle-muskets from 1 January 1861 to 31 December 1863, and contractors made about twice that number [CWN-IMPORT] (fetched). It averaged 600 rifle-muskets a day in 1863 [ECC-ARMS] (fetched). Over the war it placed 797,936 first-class rifle-muskets with the army (snippet; includes other marks) [ECC-ARMS].
- Harpers Ferry was lost early, leaving Springfield alone until Rock Island Arsenal began in 1862 [WP-SPRING] (fetched).
- Imports mattered mainly in 1861–62: the Lorenz was second only to the Enfield, with over 225,000 bought by the federal government over the war (snippet) [ARMS-SNIP]; Marcellus Hartley bought 80,000 Austrian rifles at $10.40 each [CWN-IMPORT] (fetched). Colonel George Schuyler was named purchasing agent in Europe on 29 July 1861 and reported that suitable arms "do not exist in the markets at any price" (snippet) [ARMS-SNIP2]. A Prussian lot of 142,823 old muskets was imported in 1861–62 (snippet) [ARMS-SNIP].
- Cameron told the Senate on 27 July 1861 that arming 500,000 volunteers would need $10m more (snippet) [ARMS-SNIP2].
- Dawes showed in Congress that all but 64,000 of the 1,903,000 arms contracted between August 1861 and January 1862 were ordered under Cameron's direction [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched), [NICKLASON-SNIP]. (The number counts contracts, not deliveries.)
- Stanton on 29 January 1862 voided contracts with foreign manufacturers and barred new ones; Seward warned it could "complicate the foreign situation" [WP-STANTON] (fetched).
- Gunpowder: about 40–50% of the Union's powder came from the du Pont mills (snippet) [POWDER-SNIP]. Saltpeter came from British India; Lammot du Pont was sent to buy 3 million pounds and the Trent crisis led Britain to ban exports of saltpeter, powder and lead in late 1861, lifted by proclamation on 5 February 1862 [POWDER-SNIP], [HD-SALTPETER] (snippet then fetched).

### 3.3 The Quartermaster Department and Meigs

- Meigs became Quartermaster General on 15 May 1861 and held the post to the end of the war [WP-MEIGS] (fetched). Quartermasters handled most of the roughly $3bn the war cost; Blaine put Meigs's own disbursements at no less than $1.5bn [WP-MEIGS] (fetched). Sixteen major depots formed the supply system [WP-MEIGS] (fetched).
- From May 1861 to June 1865 the Philadelphia, New York and Cincinnati depots bought ready-made clothing worth over $292m, including 8,437,216 pairs of shoes ($1.33–$3.24 each), 7,803,066 blankets, 6,025,169 sack coats and 20,319,896 pairs of stockings [CWM-CLOTH] (fetched; from Meigs's November 1865 report).
- In FY1865 alone, 141,632 cavalry horses and 58,818 mules were bought [QM-SNIP].
- Meigs was the department's honest centre and Cameron's agents were not (next section). He also ran the Fort Pickens relief with Porter in April 1861 without telling the Navy or War Secretaries [WP-MEIGS] (fetched).
- Congressmen watched the system closely to get contracts for their districts [WP-MEIGS] (fetched).

## 4. The War Department as a seat

### 4.1 Cameron (5 March 1861–January 1862)

- Mid-1861: the department was understaffed, so Cameron relied on governors and private agents whose buying competed with the Ordnance, Quartermaster and Commissary bureaus [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched). His department had 90 employees administering an army of about 17,000 at the start [WP-CAMERON] (fetched).
- In July 1861 Congress twice demanded a list of contracts since 4 March and Cameron refused both times [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched). He kept poor records, "often the only record" being notes in his pocket, and his agent Alexander Cummings spent about a quarter-million dollars, much of it wasted [WP-CAMERON] (fetched).
- Examples from the House committee: a Boston agent paid by commission made $20,000 in one week; Cummings ordered 75,000 pairs of overpriced shoes from a firm that had lent him money; condemned Hall carbines were sold for a nominal sum and bought back at $15 and later at $22 [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched).
- The House Select Committee on Government Contracts sat from 8 July 1861 to 3 March 1863, reported 17 December 1861 (nearly 1,100 pages) and filed three reports over 3,000 pages [WP-HCOMM] (fetched); it took 265 witnesses in eight cities for the first report (snippet) [NICKLASON-SNIP]. Cameron was not shown to have enriched himself but was "plainly an incompetent administrator" [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched).
- Lincoln replaced him in January 1862: the dismissal letter of 11 January was softened, Cameron resigned on 13 January and left on 20 January, and the Senate confirmed him as minister to Russia on 14 January by 28–14 [WP-CAMERON] (fetched). The House censured him on 30 April 1862 [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched).
- In his own defence, Lincoln told Congress on 26 May 1862 that the President and all department heads shared responsibility, citing emergency advances such as $2m to Dix, Opdyke and Blatchford [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched).

### 4.2 Stanton (from 20 January 1862)

- Lincoln nominated him on 13 January; confirmed 15 January; sworn in 20 January [WP-STANTON] (fetched). The department had been called "the lunatic asylum" [WP-STANTON] (fetched).
- He appointed Tucker and Watson as assistant secretaries, added more than 60 staff, and held up officer appointments to review more than 1,400 promotion cases [WP-STANTON] (fetched).
- Contracts: a Special Commission on Ordnance and Ordnance Stores under Joseph Holt and Robert Dale Owen was appointed about 13 March 1862, met from 17 March and took up 104 cases of suspected fraud [HD-COMMISSION] (fetched). Stanton's auditors saved almost $17m by cancelling or adjusting contracts; Congress required open competitive bidding and made contractors subject to court-martial for fraud [AH-CORRUPT] (fetched). The anti-fraud act is dated 2 June 1862, and a 17 July act suspended its operation until January 1863 (snippet) [FRAUD-ACT-SNIP].
- He moved the central military telegraph office from McClellan's headquarters to the War Department [WP-STANTON] (fetched); the Military Telegraph stayed under civilian control, with Anson Stager (captain and assistant quartermaster from 11 November 1861) and Thomas Eckert, who ran the War Department telegraph office (snippet) [TELEGRAPH-SNIP].
- He pushed through the act letting the government seize railroads and telegraphs; northern railroads cooperated, so the power was rarely used [WP-STANTON] (fetched).
- He suspended recruiting on 3 April 1862 believing the Peninsula campaign would end the war, and restored it in June or July [OR-V-608], [WP-STANTON] (fetched).
- He ran the 1863 rail transfer to Chattanooga (below).

### 4.3 Thomas A. Scott

- A Pennsylvania Railroad vice president, Scott was made Assistant Secretary of War in August 1861, having had command of the army's railroad and telegraph lines since May 1861; he wrote a January 1862 report that anticipated the Military Railroad and advised draft exemptions for experienced mechanics and engineers [WP-SCOTT] (fetched). In 1863 he went west to supervise the Louisville–Bridgeport leg of the Chattanooga transfer [WP-USMRR] (fetched).
- He was a railroad owner serving the buyer, an obvious conflict of interest. The Wikipedia page does not address it (not stated); the point is for the design, not a sourced claim.

## 5. Rail capacity

- **Mileage, 1861:** about 22,000 miles in the North and 9,500 in the South [BF-RAIL] (as cited by the war-scale note [WARSCALE]).
- **April 1861:** a mob in Baltimore on 19 April attacked Massachusetts troops; the B&O was the only rail route north to Washington, and regiments went by ship to Annapolis instead (snippet) [RAIL-SNIP].
- **Organisation:** Congress authorised the President to take possession of railroads on 31 January 1862; Stanton appointed Daniel McCallum director of Military Railroads on 11 February 1862; the only federally run line at first was the seven-mile Washington–Alexandria road [NARA-USMRR] (fetched). Haupt was commissioned colonel on 27 April 1862 and ran field operations until 14 September 1863, when he refused Stanton's demand to accept his commission unconditionally [NARA-USMRR], [WP-HAUPT] (fetched).
- **Scale at war's end:** 2,105 miles operated; 419 engines and 6,330 cars bought, built or captured; nearly $30m; parts of 17 railroads in the east and 23 in the south; the Construction Corps grew from about 300 men (1863) to nearly 10,000 [NARA-USMRR] (fetched).
- **Private roads remained private.** The government paid rates and the railroads stayed with their owners in the north; the power to seize was rarely used [WP-STANTON] (fetched). After the war the lines were returned to owners without charge for federal repairs, a point records show as disputed [NARA-USMRR] (fetched).
- **Haupt's rule** was that the military must not interfere with railroad operations and rolling stock must be emptied and returned promptly [WP-HAUPT] (fetched). **Generals stalled the railroad:** on 23 August 1862 General Sturgis stopped four trains and arrested Haupt; Haupt held Halleck's dispatch that no officer could interfere, and Sturgis replied "take your damned railroad"; Haupt claimed the delay kept 10,000 men out of Second Manassas [NARA-USMRR] (fetched).
- **Potomac Creek Bridge** was rebuilt in nine rainy days in May 1862 by unskilled infantry; Lincoln said it was "nothing in it but cornstalks and beanpoles" [NARA-USMRR] (fetched).
- **Gettysburg:** the Hanover Branch and Gettysburg railroads were under military control from 9 July to 1 August 1863 and carried 15,580 wounded [WP-HAUPT] (fetched).

### The Chattanooga transfer (September–October 1863)

- Decision: a meeting in Washington on 23 September with Lincoln, Stanton, Halleck, Seward and McCallum; Stanton wanted about 30,000 men, Lincoln approved about 23,000 (XI and XII Corps) [CWM-XFER] (fetched). Three railroad presidents set the schedule at noon [CWM-XFER] (fetched). Hooker was given authority to commandeer railroads and equipment [CWM-XFER] (fetched).
- Planning figure: five days [CWM-XFER] (fetched); Stanton's proposal elsewhere is "more than 20,000 men in 7 days" [ECW-RAIL] (fetched).
- Actual: about 25,000 men and 10 batteries; 11.5 days; nearly 1,200 miles, with four railroad changes and an Ohio River ferry crossing [ECW-RAIL], [NARA-USMRR] (fetched); 1,157–1,233 miles by other counts [WP-USMRR], [CWM-XFER] (fetched). Another count puts it at about 15,000 men, 5,000 horses and over 700 wagons "in roughly two weeks" [MASTERS-XI] (fetched). Counts of men therefore range from 15,000 to 25,000.
- Per the war-scale note: first trains reached Bridgeport on 30 September, all of the XI Corps's men by 3 October, and everything by 12 October (snippet) [WARSCALE]. So from decision to first arrivals about 7 days, and to completion about 19 days [I].
- Problems: units were loaded on several short trains; at Columbus trains became mixed and some cars went to Urbana, Cleveland and Cincinnati [MASTERS-XI] (fetched). The Confederate counter-move of Longstreet's corps (about 1,000 miles over 10 railroads, 9 September to about 25 September) is the comparison [ECW-RAIL] (fetched).

### Supply by rail: Sherman and Atlanta

- A single-track line of about 473 miles (Louisville–Nashville–Chattanooga–Atlanta, three railroads) supplied about 100,000 men and 35,000 animals for 196 days, 1 May to 12 November 1864; planning figure of 130 cars a day carrying 10 tons each arriving at Chattanooga (snippet) [ATL-SNIP]. Grant wrote that the single line was often interrupted and was only enough for the troops' daily wants without a surplus (snippet) [ATL-SNIP]. McCallum's Military Railroads kept it running [NARA-USMRR] (fetched).

## 6. Design implications

### 6.1 The four resources, their real-world proxies, and the holders

| Resource | Real-world proxy | Holders who can stall | How each stalled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Money** | Dollars per turn (spending rate $40m to $108m a month over the war); the stock of debt; the gold premium as a confidence meter (about 130 to 285) | Secretary of the Treasury; Congress (Ways and Means, Senate Finance); the Commissioner of Internal Revenue; the private subscription agent (Cooke) and the New York banks | Chase: specie rule and refusing to sell below par (1861), reluctance on legal tender and a bond drive that sold nothing until Cooke (1862). Congress: rewriting the tax law so that receipts missed forecast by 56% in its first year. Banks: suspended specie in December 1861 |
| **Men (new regiments)** | Quota calls and regiments mustered | Governors (commissions, apportionment of quotas); Adjutant General; Congress for the law | Governors authorised new regiments to keep commissions, against War Department wishes (1862–64); Curtin objected to drafted men in old regiments; governors asked for new regiments even in December 1864 |
| **Men (refilling old regiments)** | Recruits, drafted men, substitutes sent to existing regiments | Provost Marshal General; colonels' recruiting parties; governors who still hold the bounty and the draft credit | Fry revoked governors' authority (Oct 1863); the draft delivered about 19% of a call and about 2% of soldiers overall; in Cincinnati's March 1864 drawing about 22% of drawn labourers paid or hired out, and 37% of merchants |
| **Munitions** | Rifles and carbines per month; clothing and animals bought; powder | Chief of Ordnance; Quartermaster General; Secretary of War (contract policy); private contractors | Ripley refused repeaters and new rifle-muskets (1861–63); Cameron's agents bought uncontrolled (1861); Stanton voided foreign contracts (Jan 1862) |
| **Rail capacity** | Miles of track under military control; cars per day on a given line; men moved per day | Director of Military Railroads (McCallum); railroad presidents (Garrett of the B&O, Scott of the Pennsylvania); generals who commandeered trains | Generals stopped trains (Sturgis, Aug 1862); Haupt would not accept a commission (Sept 1863); mixed trains and gauge breaks slowed the transfer |

(Garrett's role in the Chattanooga move was asked for and **not found**.)

### 6.2 Levers the game should offer (recommendation)

1. **Money: two holders, not one.** Treasury sets the bond and note programme; Congress sets taxes and the note limit. The player picks the Treasury Secretary (Chase's replacement is a real choice) and spends political capital for taxes. The gold premium should be a visible meter that moves on victories and defeats and on note issues, as the primary "confidence" signal. Forecasts shown to the player should be wrong by tens of percent in early years.
2. **Men: raising and refilling as separate levers (0033).**
   - *Raising new regiments* is held by governors, who gain commissions for their patrons and will do it when asked whether or not it helps the army.
   - *Refilling old regiments* is held by the War Department and, after March 1863, the Provost Marshal General; its costs are the draft's anger and the commutation fee.
   - The data support a "governor's price": new regiments cost $100 bounty per man, refilling an old regiment $300, a veteran $400 (federal, Oct 1863) [OR-III-859].
3. **Munitions: three small holders with different faults.** The Chief of Ordnance is slow on technology; the Quartermaster General is honest and reliable; the Secretary of War controls contract integrity. A replace-the-chief lever costs a political fight (Lincoln removed Ripley 15 September 1863).
4. **Rail: a capacity per route, not a pool.** Model it as men or tons per day on a named line, owned by railroads (which cooperate if paid and respected) and held up by generals. A military-control lever should exist but should usually be left unused: northern railroads cooperated, and the 1862 seizure power was rarely used.

### 6.4 Interactions and cautions

- The 1862 tax law's weak first year is a ready-made "forecast was wrong" situation, with a historical default (accept the shortfall and use greenbacks) and a staff recommendation (wait for the 1863–64 amendments).
- The July 1862 call, the August militia draft and the Militia Act all land in the same weeks, a natural 1862 crisis cluster that the Altoona conference (September 1862) and the 1862 midterms follow.
- Casualty and desertion pressure feed the draft; the draft feeds Copperhead strength (see 0023, FOUNDATION section 7). Whether "commutation" is a lever or a narrated rule falls under the sensitive-history gate (below).
- Fiscal-year labels do not match the game's turn calendar; a pack should store monthly rates derived from the yearly figures (the table in section 6.3), and say which are interpolated.

## Findings outside my scope

1. **Foreign clock tied to munitions.** Britain's late-1861 ban on exports of saltpeter, powder and lead, and its lift on 5 February 1862, plus Stanton's order of 29 January 1862 voiding foreign arms contracts over Seward's warning, show the foreign-powers clock (0023) reaching into the munitions lever. The Trent crisis belongs in the situations list with a munitions consequence [POWDER-SNIP], [HD-SALTPETER], [WP-STANTON].
2. **Emergency militia of summer 1863 and the draft riots.** Over 120,000 "emergency men" from New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and the Morgan-raid states served two to three weeks in summer 1863 and are excluded from the call totals [OR-V-519]; the New York riots (13–16 July 1863) and the "rich man's war" slogan sit under the sensitive-history gate (0021, FOUNDATION section 7). Whether commutation is a lever needs a gate ruling before it is authored.
3. **Black enlistment and pay.** The Militia Act of 17 July 1862 let Black men serve, with pay of $10 a month less $3 for clothing, against $13 for white soldiers, until the law of 15 June 1864; 178,895 Black troops served in all [WP-MILACT], [WP-UA]. Per 0026 this is not part of the "men" resource; it needs the gate and its own record.
4. **The Commissary Department (subsistence) was not researched.** Rations are a fifth supply line that is not named in 0023.
5. **The Cameron annual report of late 1861**, from which Lincoln removed a paragraph proposing to use freed slaves, was found in passing [WP-CAMERON]; it bears on emancipation and the 1861–62 political note.
6. **Governors also held money and arms in 1861.** States floated their own war loans (for example Pennsylvania $3m, Connecticut $2m, New York City $1m, about $15m across the states listed) and bought arms in competition with Ordnance (New York State bought 5,000 Remington rifles) [CWE-1861], [ECC-ARMS]. So the governor is a money and munitions holder in the first months, not only a regiment holder; it bears on the Phase 1 seat list and on 1861 start-state data.
7. **Presidential action before statute.** Lincoln's 3 May 1861 call for up to 500,000 men on one-to-three-year terms came before Congress met and was confirmed by the acts of July and 6 August 1861 [OR-IV-1264]. This is a precedent for an "act now, ratify later" verb that costs legitimacy rather than a vote; for the lead to weigh against 0016 and 0023.
8. **Telegraph office as an information flow** (Eckert, Stager, Lincoln's own telegrams) belongs to the information-flows note [TELEGRAPH-SNIP].
