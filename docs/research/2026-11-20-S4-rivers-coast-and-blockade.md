# Rivers, coast and blockade operations through November 1862

S4 research, 20 November 2026. Proposal and evidence for review under 0106; no mechanics, gate wording or signatures are accepted here. Read with the [land campaign note](2026-11-20-S4-campaigns-and-organisation.md), [strength register](2026-11-20-S4-military-strengths-and-losses.md) and [source register](2026-11-20-S4-sources.md). Citations use printed pages. M = A. T. Mahan, *The Gulf and Inland Waters* (1883); A = Daniel Ammen, *The Atlantic Coast* (1883). Both are retrospective naval histories, not contemporary returns. OR = *Official Records*, series I. Their judgments about courage, civilians and responsibility are not adopted as facts or personal scores.

## What kind of control did a naval operation establish?

**[I]** The evidence below warrants separate questions: can a vessel cross the bar; pass a battery; bring transports through; land and sustain troops; hold a fort; patrol a reach; prevent a particular enemy movement? It does not warrant one universal naval-control flag. Passing Vicksburg and occupying New Orleans are different operations, even when some of the same ships performed both.

Mahan describes Cairo as the river depot and emphasises the river's bends and seasonal variation: high water in late winter/early spring, another rise in early summer, low water in August–October. His stated Cairo-to-mouth river distance is about 1,097 miles against about 480 in a straight line. **[I]** A route graph should use the navigable course and dated stage, with vessel draft, rather than straight-line distance or an annual average movement cost. These are the author's general descriptions, not a daily gauge series. [M pp. 9–11.]

## The Western flotilla: procurement and authority before the battles

Mahan dates the contract for Eads's seven armoured gunboats to **7 August 1861**, with delivery promised for 10 October; the first was launched on **12 October** and the boats were received on **5 December**. The named boats are Cairo, Carondelet, Cincinnati, Louisville, Mound City, Pittsburg and St. Louis. He describes approximately 175-foot length, 50-foot beam, six-to-seven-foot draft and thirteen guns. Benton drew about nine feet and had different handling and speed. **[I]** A contract, launch and service-ready vessel are three dated states; these hulls are not interchangeable transport counters. [M pp. 12–17.]

Foote's crew shortage is also dated. Mahan says that on 12 January 1862 the boats had roughly a third of their complements, and that only fifty of 1,100 men promised in December had arrived by February. He describes Halleck's wish to retain regimental officers over soldiers serving afloat and the resulting conflict over shipboard authority. **[I]** An army's ability to assign men does not establish that a trained naval crew and an agreed command relationship exist. These figures are attributed evidence, not a rule that every new ship starts at one-third capacity. [M pp. 16–17.]

There is an internal date conflict to preserve: Mahan p. 21 places the flotilla's transfer to the Navy in July; his detailed account on p. 107 says it took effect **1 October 1862**, when the official name became Mississippi Squadron. **[I] Proposed ruling:** use the specific dated passage provisionally and flag the contradiction for an order-level check. Rejected: silently citing the same book as though its two statements agreed, or treating July as a verified legal effective date. [M pp. 21, 107.]

At Henry, Mahan's injury categories are **2 killed, 9 wounded, 28 scalded** in the fleet, with subsequent deaths among the scalded. He separately mentions soldiers aboard Essex and further scalding details. **[I]** Preserve the categories and reporting scope; do not add every sentence's number into a new aggregate without establishing whether the cohorts overlap and when later deaths were counted. [M pp. 23–24.]

The Island No. 10 account describes a canal completed for light transports and gunboats running the batteries to protect an army crossing. **[I]** A fleet passing upstream/downstream is not the same prerequisite as transports crossing under protection. A proposed checkpoint should name the movement, banks, vessels and army objective, rather than simply ask whether the river is “open.” [M pp. 32–35.]

At St. Charles on **17 June**, Mahan describes Kilty's expedition with Mound City, St. Louis, Lexington, Conestoga and Fitch's Indiana troops. A shot entering Mound City's boiler causes a catastrophe: he says only twenty-five of a crew of 175 escaped uninjured. Falling water subsequently prevents the expedition going beyond Crooked Point. **[I]** This is evidence for a machinery casualty and a draft/stage limit, not a defensible generic loss percentage for ironclads. The allegation about fire on men in the water remains a gated source lead. [M pp. 50–51.]

## New Orleans: obstruction, passage, surrender and occupation

Mahan dates Farragut's assignment to **9 January 1862** and assumption at Ship Island to **21 February**. His account of the bar crossing describes ships being lightened and brought through shallow water. **[I]** The seagoing squadron's availability at Ship Island does not put its heavier ships immediately inside the river. [M pp. 53–55.]

The river obstruction was a separate target. Caldwell in Itasca, with Pinola supporting, worked on the chain/hulk barrier on the night of **20 April**. A second examination on the night of **23 April** checked the suspected remaining chain and reported the passage clear at 11 p.m. Mahan describes current, grounding risk, darkness and the difficulty of verifying the opening. **[I]** The relevant information was that a passage had been physically checked and reported, not a permanent “obstruction removed” fact known to every actor simultaneously. [M pp. 65–67, 72–73.]

Farragut changed the intended two parallel columns into a line ahead to reduce collision risk in darkness and smoke. Bailey's leading division was headed by Cayuga, followed in the stated order by Pensacola, Mississippi, Oneida, Varuna, Katahdin, Kineo and Wissahickon. The signal came at 2 a.m. and Cayuga passed the booms at about 3.30 a.m. on **24 April**. Thirteen of sixteen vessels passed; Varuna was subsequently sunk in the fighting. **[I]** Orders, formation and passage outcome should remain distinct from the claim that every ship followed the plan without deviation. [M pp. 71–73, 79–82.]

Mahan places the fleet before New Orleans at noon on **25 April**; he then distinguishes the demand/flag events, the marines sent on **29 April**, the forts' surrender on **28 April**, and Butler's arrival on the evening of **1 May**, when the city passed to his care. **[I]** Do not give an army an occupied city merely because the fleet passed the forts on the 24th, and do not make continued fort possession a prerequisite for the fleet's already accomplished passage. [M pp. 86–89.]

Mahan's discussion of flag removal, surrender terms and the fort garrison's refusal to continue fighting is retained as a source lead for S7. His ethnic characterisations and claims about French intentions are not adopted here; the latter need S5's diplomatic sources. [M pp. 86–89.]

Mahan's discussion of the 28 June action also identifies an orders problem: vessels which failed to pass could cite written instructions to stop and drift down if the enemy continued the action, while Farragut expected them to follow him through. **[I]** This is evidence for examining the actual order and observable movement before attributing disobedience; it does not establish an officer's permanent character score. [M pp. 95–97.]

For the autumn, Mahan describes Davis and Curtis arranging combined expeditions from Helena, including one departing **14 August** and returning after eleven days, and preparation of light vessels for Tennessee/Cumberland work. The retrospective value claimed for destroyed property is not converted into a resource score. He dates Farragut's **report** that Galveston, Corpus Christi, Sabine Pass and adjacent waters were held to **15 October**. **[I]** That report date is not an exact capture date for each place, nor proof of durable control through later years. The 1863 reversals are outside S4. [M pp. 107–108.]

## Hatteras: primary reports constrain the retrospective summary

Use **28–29 August 1861**, not a one-day capture on the 28th. Weber dates his landing to the 28th, and the capitulation signed by Stringham, Butler, Barron, Martin and Andrews is dated the **29th**. Ammen's narrative gives the 28th for the operation and its summary; retain that as a retrospective date error, not a rival signed capitulation. [OR I-IV pp. 583, 586–587, 589–590; A pp. 163–170.]

| Measurement | Statement in the read record | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Planned army detachment | Wool's 25 August order first specifies 800 troops plus an artillery company; its provisioning paragraph says 860. Wool's later report also says 860 | A provisioning/order strength, not the number landed in the surf. OR I-IV p. 580. |
| Butler's embarkation list | 500 Twentieth New York, 220 Ninth New York, 100 Coast Guard, 60 regular artillery | **[I] Sum 880**, different from the 860 order. Preserve the source difference. OR I-IV p. 581. |
| First landing | Butler says about 315 including 55 marines; Weber's counted detachment totals 318 and includes 45 marines and 28 sailors | Different reports, not one number to reconcile by adding their contingents. OR I-IV pp. 582, 589. |
| Prisoners | Wool initially reports 715; Butler relays Barron's statement of 615 in the fort; the editor says the omitted muster roll totals 691 | Keep the author, date and basis. Ammen's 615 is not automatically a final roll total. OR I-IV pp. 580, 583–584; A p. 169. |
| Union casualties | Ammen says none; Weber reports one man slightly wounded in the hand by a shell from the Union fleet while his troops occupied Fort Clark | Do not replace the specific friendly-fire report with an unconditional zero. OR I-IV p. 589; A p. 169. |

The boats were swamped or damaged, surf stopped further landing, flags were misread and ships grounded on the bar. Butler initially received an expedition expected to return when its object was achieved; he then decided with the naval officers to hold the position pending further government instructions. **[I]** The durable base requires an occupation and resupply decision, not just a naval victory notification. His report identifies coaling/shelter and access for light-draft craft, but also a fresh-water problem. [OR I-IV pp. 580, 582–585.]

## Port Royal and the South Atlantic coast

Ammen explains Port Royal as a needed coaling/base position, reducing the need to return to Hampton Roads. Du Pont's flag was hoisted in Wabash on **10 October 1861**; the expedition sailed **29 October**, with T. W. Sherman's approximately 12,000 troops among about fifty vessels. A **1 November** storm dispersed the convoy. **[I]** Fifty vessels is a mixed expedition, not fifty combat ships or a simultaneous arrival guarantee. [A pp. 13–18.]

For **7 November**, Ammen gives the main naval line as Wabash, Susquehanna, Mohican, Seminole, Pawnee, Unadilla, Ottawa, Pembina and Vandalia, the last towed by Isaac Smith; the flanking line includes Bienville, Seneca, Penguin and Augusta. Du Pont's ships repeatedly passed the forts; the flanking force also checked the Confederate river steamers. Drayton's fort command and the later landing of Wright's brigade are different commands. **[I]** The account supports naval neutralisation followed by land occupation; it does not put all the embarked troops into an amphibious assault under fire. [A pp. 20–27; OR I-VI pp. 2–3 for the operation date/report list.]

In March 1862, Ammen describes Fernandina as abandoned before occupation, not stormed with a known battle loss; Wright's brigade arrives on **5 March**. A naval force reaches St. Augustine on **8 March** in his account. He prints Lee's **24 February** instruction authorising retirement from Cumberland and Amelia Islands after removing guns and equipment where practicable. **[I]** A coastal withdrawal can be a deliberate concentration decision; it does not establish that an army has been annihilated. The exact surrender acts at each Florida place require their own reports before a finer checkpoint is set. [A pp. 53–58.]

### Hampton Roads, 8–9 March: two days and opposing accounts

The OR chronology dates the engagement to **8–9 March 1862**. The Union Navy Secretary's December report describes Cumberland sunk by ramming and Congress destroyed on the 8th, with Minnesota aground; Monitor arrives during the night and fights Virginia on the 9th. Buchanan's **27 March** report identifies his squadron as Virginia, Patrick Henry, Jamestown, Teazer, Beaufort and Raleigh, totalling twenty-seven guns in his list. It says he was wounded on the 8th and passed ship command to Catesby Jones. **[I]** Preserve the command replacement and the separate days; a single duel entry would erase the first day's destruction. [OR I-IX pp. 1–3, 8–10.]

The embedded Jones account says Virginia's length and draft made her difficult to work, records grounding and backing off, damage and leakage, and reports 2 killed / 19 wounded. That is a statement in the ship's account, not a loss total for the whole engagement. The Union report calls the opponent overmatched; the Confederate report calls the operation a triumph. **[I]** Neither label resolves every tactical claim. The read evidence supports draft, channel, damage and the prevention of another attack on Minnesota; it does not justify an automatic national blockade collapse or immunity from future damage for either ironclad. Accounts of surrender/fire from shore require S7's separate review. [OR I-IX pp. 2–3, 7–10.]

### Pulaski and Macon: land batteries in a naval campaign

The OR dates Fort Pulaski's bombardment/capture to **10–11 April 1862**. Hunter reports fire opening at 8 a.m. and surrender at 2 p.m. on the 11th; Benham says about 7.30 a.m. for the opening. Hunter reports 360 prisoners; Benham reports an effective garrison of 361, including 24 officers, **besides** about eighteen sick/wounded. **[I]** These are differently stated counts; do not silently turn them into one exact whole-garrison total. [OR I-VI pp. 133–136.]

The reports identify Gillmore's direct engineering/siege work, Benham's division/district command and Hunter's department command; they also credit naval personnel serving guns ashore. Benham describes the breach and preparations for an assault which surrender made unnecessary. **[I]** “A naval theatre” does not mean ships inflicted every loss or that an assault occurred. The authors' broad forecasts about every future masonry fort are judgments, not established weapon coefficients. [OR I-VI pp. 134–138.]

At Fort Macon on **25 April**, Ammen describes the army's breaching batteries and the naval participation of Daylight, Georgia, Chippewa and Gemsbok; rough sea limits the ships' continued firing. **[I]** Attribute the operation to its joint parts and preserve the sea-state constraint rather than treating the fleet as a constant added battery. A matched garrison/army casualty return is held here. [A pp. 193–194.]

### Local operations and the limits of patrolling

Ammen's North Edisto account describes Rhind's **19 April** operation and Hale grounding on **29 April** until late afternoon; his Stono discussion describes Pawnee striking the bar repeatedly on **29 May**. His May Winyah reconnaissance finds some supposed armament to be dummy guns. **[I]** Sounding, local pilots and confirmation of armament are operational prerequisites. A map label is insufficient evidence of an occupied, supplied heavy battery. [A pp. 63–67.]

His account of the Pocotaligo bridge expedition identifies Steedman's naval command and Brannan's troops. Missed night signals, taking the wrong side of channel lights, grounding and a collision delay much of the force by five hours; it does not obtain the intended lodgment on the Charleston–Savannah railroad. The troops re-embark on a **23rd**, but these pages do not supply an explicit month/year for the opening. **Held:** exact calendar key pending a dated primary report; no unsourced date is supplied here. **[I]** The event still provides evidence for transport assembly and arrival delay, not a date-triggered automatic railroad cut. [A pp. 71–72.]

## Proposed rulings and checkpoint questions

1. **[I] Make the object of control explicit.** A Vicksburg passage checkpoint can ask whether named vessels passed and survived on a given bank/reach; a November strategic checkpoint should still recognise Confederate-held Vicksburg–Port Hudson. Rejected: using the June fleet meeting to declare the Confederacy divided.
2. **[I] Preserve the joint sequence.** For Hatteras, Port Royal and New Orleans distinguish preparation, naval action, landing, capitulation, occupation and resupply. Rejected: one victory flag granting every consequence on the first firing date.
3. **[I] Date availability and command separately.** Retain contract/launch/receipt, crew state and Army/Navy authority. Rejected: counting every contracted hull as an available crewed vessel or assuming the War Department rams always obeyed the naval commander.
4. **[I] Use reported readiness as local evidence.** Water stage, draft, machinery, coal, ammunition, pilots and friendly troop proximity can constrain an operation. Rejected: deriving universal probabilities or exact performance constants from one narrative episode.
5. **Held:** narrow numeric checkpoint bands for naval casualties, blockade efficiency and coastal garrison strengths until paired returns and a named representation are selected. **[I]** Broad event/date comparisons can be proposed from these pages; invented statistical precision would not improve them.
