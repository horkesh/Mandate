## 1. Congress's calendar and the limits of an election result

McPherson prints these dates for the 37th Congress: first session, **4 July–6 August 1861**; second, **2 December 1861–17 July 1862**; third, **1 December 1862–4 March 1863**. He separately heads the 38th Congress's first session **7 December 1863–4 July 1864**. These are the source's session boundaries; a boundary is not necessarily an ordinary legislative day. [MP, pp. 121, 140.]

| Period within S3 | Legislative position | Representation implication [I] |
|---|---|---|
| 1–6 August 1861 | First session still sitting | Its closing legislation belongs to the existing Congress. |
| 7 August–1 December 1861 | Between the first and second sessions | Distinguish executive action from a bill passed by Congress. A later inquiry or ratification is a separate act. |
| 2 December 1861–17 July 1862 | Second session | Bills, appropriations and investigations can proceed through Congress. |
| 18 July–30 November 1862 | Between the second and third sessions | The preliminary proclamation and autumn elections do not occur in a sitting regular congressional session. |
| 1 December 1862 onward, outside the checkpoint | The 37th returns | Do not seat the newly elected 38th House on election night. |

[I] A November checkpoint should therefore keep three quantities distinct: the serving House, returns for its successor, and the successor's eventual membership. The sources do not justify turning all three into one national support number. This follows FOUNDATION's election scope and 0084's daily chamber calendar without changing either.

### Membership is dated, not a fixed national faction table

The 37th's roster names Grow as Speaker and Etheridge as Clerk. McPherson's changes list records, among others, Breckinridge's expulsion on 4 December 1861, Bright's on 5 February 1862, and the replacement of Missouri's expelled senators. [MP, pp. 122–123.] [I] Keep a dated membership history rather than copying a retrospective roster as though every listed member served throughout S3.

[I] Congress contains officeholders with individual positions. Party, willingness to fund the war, willingness to abolish slavery, and willingness to support a particular commander are different attributes. The roll calls below give examples; they do not measure a person's immutable faction membership.

### 2.1 First confiscation: what Congress actually changed

Section 1 makes property knowingly used to aid an insurrection subject to seizure and condemnation. Section 4 supplies a defense against an enslaver's claim when the person claimed had been required or permitted to perform hostile military work. It does **not** contain the July 1862 act's general wording that specified people shall be forever free. Avoid both extremes: universal emancipation in August 1861 and the claim that Congress did nothing to the enforceability of enslavement. [ST12, p. 319; MP, p. 195.]

The Globe's 60–48 tally is followed by a procedural note: McKnight and Riddle sought leave to vote after their names had been called, objections were made, and both would have voted affirmatively. The announced vote remained 60–48. McPherson's printed 61–48 is a conflicting tally, not an alternate phase of passage established by these pages. [G61, p. 431; MP, pp. 195–196.]

[I] Use 60–48 for the House passage anchor, preserve the conflict in provenance, and do not average the two. A missed or prospective vote is not a cast vote. This is a research correction, not a change to existing pack data.

### 2.2 Legal tender: an emergency instrument with a contested distribution of costs

The first act authorizes **$150 million** in non-interest-bearing United States notes, including **$50 million replacing demand notes**, rather than $150 million added on top of those notes. It authorizes **$500 million** in six-percent bonds, redeemable after five years and payable after twenty. Notes are legal tender with stated exceptions: customs duties and interest on federal bonds and notes retain coin requirements. [ST12, pp. 345–346.]

Chase's 29 January letter accepts the necessity while expressing aversion to making anything but coin legal tender. He links the notes' credit to funding in bonds, a banking arrangement and taxation. Stevens tries to extend coin payment to soldiers, sailors and government supplies; that amendment fails **67–72**. The House then accepts the coin-interest exception **88–55**. [MP, pp. 358–359.]

[I] That sequence supports a political dispute about who is protected from depreciation, not a simple opposition of war supporters to war opponents. Treasury authority, notes actually emitted, and purchasing power must remain separate. The **11 July** additional **$150 million** issue is within S3, whereas the 1863 issue and a resulting $450 million ceiling cannot be backdated to February or November 1862. [MP, p. 356 for the dated authorizations.]

### 2.3 District emancipation: freedom, compensation and colonization are separate provisions

Section 1 ends slavery in the District. The statute then establishes a claims process for claimants loyal to the United States; the compensation appropriation is **$1 million**. The act also appropriates **$100,000** for the separate, consent-dependent colonization provision. The statute's aggregate valuation formula is historical evidence about that law, **not** a permissible player price for a human being. [ST12, pp. 376–378; MP, pp. 211–213.]

Lincoln's signing message supports the act and its compensation and colonization principles while asking Congress to repair the ninety-day claims deadline's omission of minors and other claimants. [MP, pp. 212–213.] [I] The political fact is not exhausted by “Lincoln frees the capital”: Congress legislates, people obtain freedom, officials administer claims, and Black people's decisions about remaining or emigrating are their own. Gate review must govern any eventual wording or consequences.

### 2.4 Homesteads: enactment precedes entry and ownership

The act permits specified citizens or declared intending citizens, heads of families or adults, who have not aided the United States' enemies, to enter surveyed, unappropriated public land under stated conditions. Its standard maximum is a quarter section, **160 acres**, with a different **80-acre** provision for the higher-priced land described in section 1. It requires an affidavit, a **$10** payment and other prescribed land-office fees; the ordinary patent follows five years' residence or cultivation and proof. Section 8 allows purchase under the described preemption route before five years. Fourteen days' wartime service removes the under-21 disability in section 6. [ST12, pp. 392–393.]

[I] Do not award November 1862 farms, agricultural output or tax receipts on the strength of the May signature: entry starts on **1 January 1863**. “Free land” is period political language, not evidence that the land had no occupants, competing rights or costs. A representation of Indigenous land loss would require its own sources and gate review; this statute alone does not establish local dispossession events.

### 2.5 July confiscation: several legal routes, followed by a constitutional compromise

The second act distinguishes conviction for treason or assistance to rebellion, seizure of specified rebels' property, and the freedom provisions in sections 9–10. Section 6 gives other persons described there **sixty days after presidential warning** to cease assistance and return to allegiance. Sections 7–8 require judicial proceedings for condemnation and sale. Section 9 addresses people escaping enslavers engaged in rebellion, people coming under United States control in its stated circumstances, and people in places passing from rebel to United States occupation; it declares those covered forever free. Section 10 bars military and naval personnel from deciding or surrendering a person's claimed service. Sections 11–13 separately authorize employment, consent-dependent colonization, and amnesty. [ST12, pp. 589–592.]

Lincoln signs the bill and explanatory resolution together and submits his previously drafted objections. His objections include perpetual forfeiture of real estate, the absence of an adequate way to determine which people fall within section 9, and procedural protection in property proceedings. The resolution limits real-estate forfeiture to the offender's life and narrows the state-office provision. [MP, pp. 197–198; ST12, p. 627.]

[I] This supports Congress as a negotiating institution with constitutional and practical constraints. It does not support an instantaneous conversion of a percentage of the Confederate population into a federal resource or an assumption that every legal entitlement was immediately enforceable everywhere.

### 2.6 The September notice is neither the January proclamation nor a congressional bill

The preliminary proclamation states an intention to renew compensated state emancipation proposals when Congress next meets and to continue colonization efforts with the emigrants' consent and the receiving government's consent. It sets the January condition and says executive authority will recognize and maintain the freedom of those covered, without repressing their efforts for actual freedom. It repeats the March Article of War and July confiscation provisions. [MP, pp. 227–228.]

It also makes good-faith representation in Congress, after an election in which a majority of qualified voters participated, presumptive evidence that a state is not in rebellion, absent strong countervailing testimony. [MP, p. 228.] [I] This is a substantive connection between representation and the proclamation, but not a warrant to invent eligible-voter counts in occupied districts.

The **11 December** tabling of Yeaman's hostile resolutions **95–47** and **15 December** adoption of supportive resolutions **78–52** belong to the returning **37th**, after S3's November checkpoint. They are useful later comparison points, not September authorization or votes cast by the newly elected House. [MP, p. 229.]

## 3. Political positions evidenced by acts and statements

| Position or relationship | Evidence read | What it does and does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Congressional antislavery pressure | Trumbull's July 1861 amendment; the 1862 territorial, District and confiscation legislation | Shows distinct policy steps and changing coalitions. It does not supply an immutable list or size of a “Radical faction.” [MP, pp. 195–198, 209–213; ST12, p. 432.] |
| A Republican can defend Lincoln's caution | Sumner's **5 June 1862** letter defends Lincoln's antislavery purpose, compensated state emancipation, District emancipation and recognition of Haiti and Liberia while acknowledging impatience | A named relationship on a date; it contradicts treating every antislavery leader as always hostile to Lincoln. [MP, p. 233.] |
| Border Unionism can oppose abolition | Majority reply **14 July 1862** rejects the compensated-state proposal while asserting support for restoring the Union, appropriations and recruitment | The authors defend slavery and state control while rejecting secession. Neither opposition to emancipation nor a Union label alone proves a wish for Confederate victory. [MP, pp. 214–216.] |
| Border representatives are not unanimous | McPherson prints a **minority reply** after the majority | Preserve the split; do not give one answer to every border-state holder. [MP, pp. 216–217.] |
| Commanders intervene in policy | McClellan's **7 July 1862** letter offers Lincoln a general civil and military policy, explicitly beyond the army's immediate situation | A private political intervention, not a public party platform or a presidential order. [MP, pp. 385–386.] |
| Executive management of a political military quarrel | Lincoln's **6 August 1862** speech defends Stanton from allegations of withholding men and distinguishes men on rolls from men fit for duty | Public attribution and competing strength claims; not independent proof that each factual defense was correct. [MP, p. 334.] |
| Governors organize support | The Altoona address endorses the proclamation and requests a reserve of at least **100,000** one-year volunteers after existing quotas | A political request, not a muster or an enacted new call. Its date conflict is retained in the companion note. [MP, pp. 232–233.] |

Do not infer a numerical caucus membership, a voting discipline, or an individual's complete politics from these labels. This packet supplies observable acts; S6 owns personality/stat proposals.

## 4. Institutional constraints worth carrying into 1B

1. [I] **A bill requires its own history.** Identify introduction, passage, disagreement, conference, signature and implementation. This matters especially for the confiscation and currency compromises.
2. [I] **Congress can investigate a supporter.** Lincoln's defense of Cameron acknowledges the House's censure and shared executive responsibility. Support for prosecution of the war does not remove oversight. [MP, pp. 333–334.]
3. [I] **A recess changes access, not legal reality.** Congress's absence does not turn an executive proclamation into a statute, and a later election does not repeal an existing act. [MP, pp. 121, 140, 227–229.]
4. [I] **The result does not measure every resident's opinion.** These returns record votes cast under each jurisdiction's franchise and voting arrangements; they cannot supply a universal national opinion poll. Soldier participation and its absence need separate treatment.
5. [I] **Seat losses are not losses of war authority one for one.** Party labels do not alone identify voting coalitions on emancipation, finance or military appointments. Preserve separate chamber and election representations, with explained reasons.
