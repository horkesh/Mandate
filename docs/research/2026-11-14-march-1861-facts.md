## 1. How and when Lincoln learned the commissioners were in Washington

**Settled:** who arrived and when. Forsyth's own letter of 8 March says he "reached this Capital on the 5th" and that Roman had not come ([Rosenbach, transcript](https://civilwar.rosenbach.org/?p=686)); Dickinson's site gives 5 March for Forsyth and Crawford ([House Divided](https://hd.housedivided.dickinson.edu/node/35826)); Epperson has Crawford on 3 March and Forsyth on 5 March ([chronology](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)). Roman was still in Montgomery on 14 March ([Rosenbach search result](https://civilwar.rosenbach.org/?p=673)) (snippet). Davis's 27 February letter introducing them was addressed to Lincoln but "Lincoln did not receive the commission" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_peace_commission)).

**Not settled:** how and when Lincoln learned. I found no source that says. What the record shows:
- The commissioners deliberately avoided any official move. They reached the Seward group through "a late distinguished Senator" and held back until they had "reasonable assurance" ([Forsyth and Crawford, 8 March](https://civilwar.rosenbach.org/?p=686)). Burlingame names Gwin first, then Hunter, then Justice Campbell as go-betweens ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). Epperson dates Gwin's proposal of a 20-day pause to 8 March and Seward's meeting with Hunter to 10 March ([chronology](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)).
- On 11 March they asked through a senator for an unofficial interview; Seward refused on 12 March; a sealed formal note reached the State Department on 13 March; Seward wrote his memorandum on 15 March and says he "cheerfully submitted" it to the President, who "coincides generally" ([Seward's memorandum](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterSewardMemoCommish0408.htm)). Seward adds he could have decided without asking Lincoln. That is the first moment Lincoln is shown to know.
- Burlingame says Lincoln "forbade" Seward to receive them but gives no date ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). Whether Seward consulted Lincoln during the go-between talks is called unclear in secondary material ([search summary of the Wikipedia and blog accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_peace_commission)) (snippet).
- I found no Evening Star or New York paper item of 5–6 March announcing them. Both papers were unreachable.

**[I]** The most defensible reading for the game: Lincoln learns of them from Seward, in conversation, by about 9–13 March, and Seward decides what else to tell him. Treat "informal source, filtered by Seward" as the channel.

## 2. The route of Anderson's letters

Anderson's own description, in his letter of 27 January (received by the Adjutant General on 30 January): letters "are sent by boat, daily, at 12 m., to Fort Johnson in a sealed package, addressed to the postmaster in Charleston", and the return boat brings mail "in a package bearing the post-office seal" ([Epperson transcript](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterAndersonRations012761.htm)). From Charleston it travelled as ordinary mail. I found no source for any telegraph link at the fort.

**Delays, measured:**
- 27 Jan letter, received 30 Jan: 3 days ([same page](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterAndersonRations012761.htm)).
- Anderson's letters of 28 Feb and 2 March both reached the War Department "before noon" on 4 March, which Holt reports on 5 March ([Epperson, Holt/Scott/Lincoln papers](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterHoltScottLincoln0305.htm)). That is 2 days for the 2 March letter. The 28 February letter probably waited and went with it **[I]**; this corrects [SW]'s "5 days".
- Washington to Sumter: Cameron's letter of 4 April reached Anderson "by yesterday's mail" per Anderson's letter of 8 April, so on 7 April: 3 days ([OR I,1 p. 294](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150854-294-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)).
- Anderson's 1 April letter produced Cameron's reply of 4 April ([Burlingame ch. 22 fn. 207](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf), [Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)), so it reached Washington in about 2–3 days.

**Interference:** the whole route ran through the enemy's post office and boats.
- Pickens stopped the mail 1–14 January, then restored it (two auction-house lot descriptions: [Siegel](https://siegelauctions.com/exhibits/CalhounCharleston.pdf), [Rumsey](https://www.rumseyauctions.com/auctions/lot/78/1967)) (snippet).
- 29 March: the Confederate War Secretary orders Beauregard to allow "no further communications with Sumter without inspection"; 8 April the mail is cut off; 9 April Sumter's mail is "seized and read" ([Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)). Epperson also notes Anderson "is warned by ordinary post" on 4 April, which fits Cameron's letter arriving 7 April.
- Historynet says Lincoln sent Fox partly because Anderson's letters were "probably guarded" ([HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/mission-to-relieve-fort-sumter-september-97-americas-civil-war-feature/)).

## 3. Why the Pickens order took 19 days out and the refusal about 5 back

Source: Welles's own article, read as raw text ([Welles, "Fort Pickens", *The Galaxy*, Jan 1871](https://fcit.usf.edu/florida/docs/f/ftpickens.htm)).

**Outbound (Washington to Pensacola, 12 to 31 March, 19 days).**
- Scott's order to Captain Vogdes is headed "Headquarters of the Army, Washington, March 12". Scott told Welles he might not spare an officer, so the Navy carried it: Welles chose the sloop *Crusader* (Commander Craven) on 11 March, with orders to put "a bearer of despatches" on board the *Brooklyn* off Pensacola and then go on to Key West.
- Welles says "there was delay in the departure" because of "difficulties in the Adjutant-General's office in detailing the companies which were to stop at Key West", and that "the voyage of the *Crusader* was also somewhat protracted". He gives no reason for the slow passage.
- Departure from New York on 15 March ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/InitialProb/Mar11_12.2.html)).
- Vogdes's covering note to Adams, dated 1 April, says he received the order "last night", i.e. 31 March (same Welles text).
- No telegraph: Pensacola was in Confederate hands and the line was not usable. **[I]** from the fact that every message went by hand.

**Return (Pensacola to Washington, 1 to 6 April, 5 days).**
- Adams wrote on 1 April refusing to land the troops and asking instructions. He sent Lieutenant Washington Gwathmey "express". Welles: Gwathmey arrived "on the afternoon of the 6th of April", "travel-stained and much exhausted", with the letter in a belt under his clothes. Tulane gives the notification time as about 3 p.m. Saturday 6 April ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/FinalOrder/FApr6.2.html)).
- Route: the sources say "night and day" and give no stations. It must have been overland by rail through the Confederacy **[I]**. Worden's trip the other way confirms the pattern: left Washington 7 April, reached Pensacola 11 April, delivered the order to Adams 12 April ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf), [OR I,1 p. 462](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/151022-462-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html), [OR I,1 p. 376](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150935-375-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)).

So the asymmetry is: a ship, a bureaucratic delay and a slow passage out; a single officer on the railways back. Overland by a courier took 4–5 days; by sea 19.

**Correction to [SW].** Adams's own report of 14 April says Worden's order reached him on 12 April and "that same night" Vogdes landed ([OR I,1 p. 376](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150935-375-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)). [SW] gave 11 April "per Battlefields". The 12th is better sourced. Reply time is 5 days, not about 6.

## 4. How Fox reached Sumter, and the date of his report

- Lincoln asked Cameron on 19 March for a "competent person"; Scott chose Fox; Fox told his wife on 19 March he was off ([Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm), [Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 77). He went by Richmond and Wilmington to Charleston, arriving 21 March ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/HMar19.html), citing Nicolay and Hay).
- He saw Governor Pickens and Beauregard; Pickens allowed the visit, according to one summary on condition of a pledge of "pacific purposes" ([search summary of Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/HMar19.html)) (snippet).
- Into the harbour: Captain Foster's letter from Sumter, 22 March, says a "special messenger, Mr. Fox, arrived from Washington" the night before "under the escort of Captain Hartstene", a former US naval officer now in South Carolina's service; Anderson's covering letter says "I have examined the point alluded to by Mr. Fox last night" ([OR I,1 p. 211](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150771-211-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)). He arrived "after dark and remained about two hours" ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/HMar19.html)). A Confederate steamer carried him (snippet; [Tulane via search](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/HMar19.html)). Hartstene stayed within earshot, so Fox hinted at his plan without describing it ([Albany Evening Journal, 27 March](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/AEJMar27.html)).
- What Anderson told him: supplies to 15 April; any attempt would bring war; relief impossible ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)).
- **Fox's own account confirms the visit.** His 1865 memorandum, printed in the Navy Official Records, says he left Washington on 19 March, went by Richmond and Wilmington, reached Charleston on the 21st, was taken out by Hartstene after seeing Pickens and Beauregard, "reached Fort Sumter after dark and remained about two hours", and agreed with Anderson that he "might report that the 15th of April at noon would be the period beyond which he could not hold the fort"; he says he "made no arrangements with Major Anderson" and "did not inform him of my plan" ([ORN I,4 p. 247](https://archive.org/details/officialrecordso0004unse)). On his return he was "called frequently before the President" (no date).
- **Report date: about 25 March.** Epperson: "Fox returns and reports to Lincoln" on 24–25 March ([chronology](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)); Tulane's page is headed Monday 25 March ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/HMar25.html)). Burlingame gives no date, only that Fox reported "upon his return" and then produced Stringham as a naval endorser. [SW] and the walkthrough have "about 23", which is too early. Not found: whether the report was written or spoken (Tulane's page does not say).
- Before the visit, Fox first met Lincoln on 13 March, brought by Blair, who had summoned him by telegram on 12 March ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf), [Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/InitialProb/Mar13.html)); confirmed by Fox's memorandum, which adds that Scott told Lincoln the plan had been practicable in February but was impossible in March ([ORN I,4 p. 246](https://archive.org/details/officialrecordso0004unse)).

## 5. Fox's relief plan in his own words

Fox wrote the plan down three times before the visit, all printed in the Official Records. The post-visit version is not a separate document; Burlingame and Tulane say he told Lincoln the visit "convinced him his plan would work".

- **6 February (St. Germain Hotel, New York):** "I propose to put the troops on board of a large, comfortable sea steamer, and hire two powerful light-draught New York tug-boats, having the necessary stores on board; these to be convoyed by the U. S. steamer Pawnee ... and the revenue cutter Harriet Lane." Why: after the *Star of the West*, he assumed the channels were blocked, but "the bar is more than four miles in length" and cannot be closed; Charleston's steamers would "throw themselves alongside of the relief vessels". "At night, two hours before high water, with half the force on board of each tug within relieving distance of each other, I should run in to Fort Sumter." The armed ships were to "destroy or drive" off Confederate vessels at the bar, and Anderson was to "prevent any naval succor being sent down from the city" ([OR I,1 pp. 203–204](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150764-204-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)).
- **8 February (to Scott):** "I ask no reward but the entire conduct of the part, exclusive of the armed vessels." He asks that the *Pawnee* go to the Delaware Breakwater, the *Harriet Lane* be readied, and a Collins steamer be engaged quietly ([OR I,1 p. 203](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150763-203-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)).
- **23 February (to Blair):** he rejects the "boats at night" plan as "inferior at every step": five miles to the open sea, "an hour's pull", a bar to cross under fire, and "if a single tug (they have four) eludes Major Anderson's vigilance, she would run in amongst these boats ... and utter destruction to them." His plan: "I simply propose three tugs, convoyed by light-draught men-of-war"; machinery packed with "bales of cotton or hay"; "the first tug to lead in empty, to open their fire. The other two to follow, with the force divided, and towing the large iron boats of the *Baltic*" ([OR I,1 p. 204](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150764-204-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)).
- **1 March (to Blair):** Charleston authorities were bargaining to buy two of his three chosen tugs, "the only three really fit for the work in the whole city" ([OR I,1 p. 205](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150765-205-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)). So the plan had lost a resource before Lincoln heard it.
- **How it stood in April,** per the secondary accounts: the *Baltic* anchors near Swash Channel about four miles out with three small warships; soldiers and stores move to armed launches towed by three tugs, passing within 1,300 yards of the batteries on Morris and Sullivan's islands ([HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/americas-civil-war-unions-mission-to-relieve-fort-sumter/)). The *Powhatan* was to carry the launches and the 300 sailors: Fox's memorandum says his plan "required 300 sailors, a full supply of armed launches, and three tugs. The *Powhatan* carried the sailors and launches", and gives the batteries as 1,300 yards from the centre of the channel ([ORN I,4 pp. 246, 250](https://archive.org/details/officialrecordso0004unse)); verified 2026-11-14.

## 6. Lamon and Hurlbut

**Ward Hill Lamon.**
- Post: none that mattered to the errand. Burlingame: Lincoln sent him "to accompany [Hurlbut] as an informal bodyguard", over Cameron's protest ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). Tulane says he went as a formal agent "to examine some post office matters", a pretext ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/HMar21.html)). He is called "Colonel" in the sources, an Illinois title.
- He was **not yet marshal of the District of Columbia** on this evidence: Burlingame has a dozen Washingtonians presenting papers recommending his appointment on 2 April, with Lamon described as promised the Paris consulate ([ch. 21](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap21.pdf)), and the Senate consented to the nomination only on 26 July 1861, 21–14 ([Voteview](https://voteview.com/rollcall/RS0370069)). Wikipedia says he was appointed "shortly after" the inauguration ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_Hill_Lamon)). I did not settle the date of his commission or whether he acted under a recess commission.
- Errand: he left 22 March with Hurlbut, met Pickens on the morning of Monday 25 March and saw Anderson. He told both the garrison would soon be withdrawn, promised to come back and help, and wrote Seward from Charleston on 25 March that he was "satisfied of the policy and propriety of immediately evacuating" ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 83). Authority is disputed: Lincoln later denied he had any ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_Hill_Lamon)).
- Consequence: on 29 March the commissioners showed Campbell a telegram from Pickens complaining that Lamon had not returned; on 31 March Seward told Campbell that Lamon "did not go to Charleston under any commission or authority from Mr. Lincoln" ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)).

**Stephen A. Hurlbut.**
- Post: Charleston-born Illinois lawyer and member of the Illinois House of Representatives ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_A._Hurlbut)); travelled as a private citizen to stay with his sister; only James L. Petigru knew his link to the President ([report, 27 March](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterHurlbutLincoln.htm)). Lincoln asked him on or about 21 March ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)).
- Dates: left on a night train on Friday 22 March; reached Charleston about 8 a.m. Sunday 24 March; met Lamon on the northbound train Monday night, 25 March; report dated 27 March ([report](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterHurlbutLincoln.htm)). Lincoln had it by 28 March (Epperson: "Lincoln receives Hurlbut's report", [chronology](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)); Burlingame says Lincoln read it to the cabinet and had Hurlbut repeat it to Seward.
- Findings: "Separate Nationality is a fixed fact", "no attachment to the Union", and a provisions-only ship "would be stopped & refused admittance" ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). [SW] and the walkthrough's wording (Charleston "would resist even an unarmed supply ship") slightly overstates it: he said the ship would be stopped, not fired on.

## 7. The Sumter relief expedition (ships, numbers, ports, dates)

Plan of 29–30 March: Burlingame dates Lincoln's instruction to 29 March; Fox's own memorandum says "On the 30th of March the President sent me to New York with verbal instructions to prepare for the voyage, but to make no binding engagements"; on the afternoon of 4 April Lincoln told him the expedition would go ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf); [ORN I,4 p. 248](https://archive.org/details/officialrecordso0004unse)). ## 10. Whether the Senate sat in March for confirmations

- **Yes.** Special (extraordinary) session from 4 March to 28 March 1861 ([House Divided](https://hd.housedivided.dickinson.edu/node/42810)). **Verified 2026-11-14:** the *Congressional Globe* prints the Senate's special session and ends with "ADJOURNMENT SINE DIE" on 28 March, after a committee reported that the President had no further communication ([Globe, Senate special session of March 1861 (37th Cong.), paged on from the 36th Cong., 2nd sess., p. 1519, issue of 29 Mar 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-congressional-globe_1861-03-29_95)).
- **Cabinet:** on 5 March it "swiftly confirmed" all seven (Seward, Chase, Cameron, Welles, Smith, Blair, Bates) ([House Divided](https://hd.housedivided.dickinson.edu/node/35791), citing Lossing). **Verified 2026-11-14:** the *Times*'s Washington dispatch of Tuesday 5 March reports the cabinet nominations "immediately confirmed to-day", "all confirmed without objection except in the case of Messrs. Bates and Blair, against whom some Southern Senators voted" ([NYT, 6 Mar 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-06_10_2950)). No roll call is printed.
- **Others:** Stanton wrote on 16 and 19 March that a cabinet member did not know of the appointments to England and France until he saw them "announced in the papers as having been sent to the Senate" ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 61). So the ministers to Britain (Adams) and France (Dayton) were before the Senate by about 19 March; their confirmation dates I did not find.
- **Business that bears on the game:** Republican senators caucused on 13 March about demanding Sumter be held (Wade) ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). **Verified 2026-11-14 in the *Globe*:** (a) the Senate resolved on 25 March to ask for Anderson's dispatches "if in my opinion not incompatible with the public interest"; Lincoln's message of 26 March, read 27 March, declined: "at the present moment, the publication of it would be inexpedient" (p. 1511); (b) Breckinridge moved on 26 March, and again on 27 March, that the Senate "recommend and advise the removal of the United States troops from the limits of the confederate States", and Clingman offered a substitute asking the President to withdraw the troops and stop collecting revenue in the seven states; no quorum could be kept to vote (pp. 1511–12); (c) Trumbull offered his resolution on 28 March, the last day: "the true way to preserve the Union is to enforce the laws of the Union ... it is the duty of the President to use all the means in his power to hold and protect the public property of the United States" in the seven states; it could be taken up only by unanimous consent and was not voted on (p. 1519) ([Globe, issue of 29 Mar 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-congressional-globe_1861-03-29_95)).
- **After 28 March** the Senate was out until the special session of 4 July, so appointments in April were made without it. Lamon's was one still pending (see question 6).
- Not found: the Executive Journal itself, and any full list of March confirmations. The verification pass of 2026-11-14 could not reach it either (not on archive.org; the Library of Congress was blocked from the lane's environment).

## 11. How often Lincoln's cabinet met, March–April 1861

- There was no fixed schedule. The regular Tuesday and Friday noon meetings came later, after members complained ([Mr. Lincoln's White House](https://www.mrlincolnswhitehouse.org/the-white-house/upstairs-at-the-white-house/upstairs-white-house-mr-lincolns-office/mr-lincolns-office-cabinet-meetings/index.html)). The page gives no date for the change. Welles wrote on 16 September 1861 that early meetings were "infrequent, irregular, and without system", and blamed partly Seward, who notified colleagues when the President wanted one (same page).
- Documented meetings (all from [Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) unless noted):
  - 6 March: Scott briefs Welles, Cameron, Seward and others at Lincoln's request; Epperson calls it the "first brief Cabinet meeting" ([chronology](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)).
  - 7 March: a small informal group at the White House (Scott, Totten, Welles, Stringham, Seward). Not a full cabinet.
  - 9 March: first full cabinet; Bates wrote that he was "astonished" Sumter must be evacuated.
  - 14 March: two sessions on Fox's plan, no decision ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/InitialProb/Mar13.html), summary).
  - 15 March: the cabinet is briefed by Fox, then answers Lincoln's written question (answers dated 15–16 March).
  - 28–29 March: Scott's recommendation to give up both forts lands on the evening of the 28th (the state dinner); Lincoln convenes the cabinet the next day, and Blair's account has him reading Scott's letter in an "agitated manner". Epperson puts a cabinet reversal on the 28th and the formal endorsement on the 29th, so the exact split is not settled ([Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)).
  - 29 March, noon: second written round, Cameron out of town.
  - 4–6 April: Lincoln decides with Welles, Fox and Blair; in 1862 he said "the entire cabinet was against him" on giving notice (Browning's diary, via Burlingame).
  - 12 April (Friday): met the cabinet "during the day" ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/AndTheWarCame/AApr12.5.html)).
- So at least six full or near-full sessions in the 40 days from 4 March to 12 April, and none weekly.

## 12. What the main Northern papers printed in March 1861

I could not open any March 1861 Northern paper. This is from Burlingame's footnotes and secondary compilations.

**Inaugural (4–5 March).** The *New York Times* called it "highly conciliatory towards all who have been led to entertain unjust and unfounded apprehensions" (verified 2026-11-14: the words describe how the address was received in New York, where about 100,000 extras sold that afternoon; [NYT, 5 Mar 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-05_10_2949)); the Cleveland *Herald* praised it; the *New York Herald* said it showed either that Lincoln had not made up his mind or wanted to keep his intentions to himself; the *Tribune* read it as showing "a man at the head of the government" ([GenealogyBank compilation](https://www.genealogybank.com/blog/reaction-to-lincolns-1st-inaugural-address.html)) (snippet). Southern papers read it as war: Richmond *Enquirer* on 5 March ([Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)); Charleston *Mercury* ("insolence", "brutality") (snippet, same compilation).

**Evacuation rumours (10–13 March).** Verified 2026-11-14 in the *Times*: "Shall Fort Sumpter be reinforced?" (11 March), "The proposed evacuation of Sumpter" (12 March), "Sumpter to be evacuated" (13 March) ([11 Mar](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-11_10_2954), [12 Mar](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-12_10_2955), [13 Mar](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-13_10_2956)). Burlingame cites the *New York World*, "Proposed Evacuation of Fort Sumter", 12 March; Washington letters in the *Tribune* of 11 and 12 March; and the Charleston *Mercury* of 12 March predicting that Sumter would be given up "without a fight" ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 26; [Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)). He says the leaks probably came from Seward. They set off a "firestorm of indignant protest": letters to Trumbull, the Republican senators' caucus of 13 March, Blair's near-resignation. That is a stronger reaction than [SW]'s "unfortunate but unavoidable"; that phrase fits a later, smaller group (Neal Dow of Maine, 13 March; Aspinwall of New York, 31 March) who accepted evacuation as a "military necessity" if Pickens were held ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)).

**Drift (late March).** "Shilly shally, namby pamby course" (Washington letter in the *Morning Express*, 31 March); the *Herald* "The Revenue Question and the Do Nothing Policy", 28 March; *Daily News*, "Has the Administration a Policy?", 29 March; *Tribune*, "The Policy of Forbearance", 27 March and "Come to the Point!", 3 April; *Times*, "Wanted — A Policy!", 3 April (verified 2026-11-14: "He is required to act,—and action requires decision"; [NYT, 3 Apr 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-04-03_10_2974)). Burlingame says the *Times* and Weed's *Albany Evening Journal* pieces carried Seward's 1 April arguments ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 55, 181). The *Albany Evening Journal* of 27 March wrote that "Fort Sumter must be abandoned, or civil war inaugurated" and said Fox's plan would probably end in bloodshed ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/Hesitation/AEJMar27.html)).

**Election effect:** Burlingame reports spring election losses blamed on the apparent inaction (St. Louis, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Brooklyn, Rhode Island, two Connecticut seats) ([ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). This conflicts with the walkthrough's row on Connecticut as "a narrow Republican hold" and with [SW]; the Connecticut claim is a Cincinnati correspondent's letter to Lincoln, so check before using.

## 13. Virginia's Unionists before the 4 April vote (Baldwin)

- **Yes, and on a dated sequence.** 1 April: Seward telegraphs George W. Summers that Lincoln "desires your attendance at Washington as soon as convenient"; Summers suspects a plot. 3 April: a messenger brings Lincoln's own message, "I want to see him at once ... If Mr. Summers cannot come himself, let him send some friend." The Unionist caucus in Richmond picks John B. Baldwin, who leaves at once. 4 April: Baldwin reports to Seward, who takes him to the White House that evening; "as the Virginia convention was voting 89-45 against secession" ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). Lincoln also had earlier indirect contact with Baldwin through Herring Chrisman in February (same page).
- **The disputed offer.** Baldwin testified in 1866 that Lincoln showed little enthusiasm and made no offer to withdraw the garrison. John Minor Botts, who saw Lincoln on 5 April (Epperson dates Botts to 7 April), said Lincoln told him he had offered to evacuate Sumter if the convention adjourned *sine die*. Lincoln told Garrett Davis in late April, and a Western Virginia delegation on 1 May, that he had made the offer; Hay confirmed the delegation's report as "substantially correct" in January 1863. Burlingame concludes Lincoln probably did make it; Fox in 1864 recalled the same offer, followed by Lincoln telling Fox on 4 April to sail ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf); [Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)). Pierpont's version: Baldwin asked that Pickens be abandoned too.
- **Vote count** (4 April): 89–45 (Burlingame, Epperson); 90–45 ([Library of Virginia](https://uncommonwealth.lva.virginia.gov/?p=2734), [SW]); 88–45 ([spirit61](https://spirit61.info/timeline/)) (snippets). **Verified 2026-11-14:** the next day's *Times* reports Harvie's motion to substitute an ordinance of secession, to be submitted to the people in May, "rejected by a vote of 45 to 89" ([NYT, 5 Apr 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-04-05_10_2976)). Use 89–45, the figure the North read.
- **Design note:** the "address" and "promise" verbs could reach Virginia's Unionists only inside 1–4 April, and the offer's existence is a hidden fact the player should not be able to verify.

## 14. How much of Lincoln's time went to patronage and office-seekers, March 1861

No source gives an hour count by subject. The evidence is consistent and strong ([Burlingame ch. 21](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap21.pdf)):
- "Lincoln rose early and spent at least twelve hours a day meeting with callers"; one report had him up at 6 a.m. and working past midnight (fn. 59, *New York Times*, 16 March).
- "The office-seekers demanded all his time" (his own remark: "like a man so busy letting rooms at one end of his house that he cannot stop to put out the fire at the other").
- Hay, 15 March: "The throng of office-seekers is something absolutely fearful. They come at daybreak and still are coming at midnight." Nicolay, 24 March: haunted by people who "want to see the President for only five minutes".
- Lincoln's first walk outside the White House grounds was 12 March; by 23 March he had ridden out twice. On 13 March he cut office hours short to nap; on 23 March he declined callers.
- 18 March (*Tribune*): his time "almost wholly engrossed in hearing applications for office".
- Late March: Nicolay got business hours limited to 10 a.m.–3 p.m., then two hours shorter, with no Saturday visits. Hay says Lincoln broke every rule as fast as it was made.
- He later estimated, "with some disgust", 30,000 office-seekers.
- The cabinet was besieged too (Blair "nearly run to death", 8 a.m. to 2 a.m.).

## 15. Union troops in and around Washington, March 1861

- 2 January: Scott made Colonel Charles P. Stone Inspector General of the District of Columbia militia. Early February: Scott distrusted the militia and brought regulars in (infantry, cavalry, artillery, Engineer Sappers and Miners). At the 4 March inauguration, riflemen lined the route, artillery and cavalry were kept out of sight, and local militia guarded the platform ([NPS, Civil War Defenses of Washington, ch. 4](https://npshistory.com/publications/cwdw/hrs/chap4-1.htm)).
- Numbers: 653 regulars and about 2,000 volunteers under Stone at the inauguration, plus Marines (snippet, blog-grade; the page was one of several results and I did not identify it); an older account puts the regulars, Marines at the navy yard and ordnance men at the arsenal at about 1,000 in February (snippet, also unidentified; possibly the 1911 Croggon reminiscence at bytesofhistory.com). Eighteen April, per the NPS: about 600 District militia, 200 Marines and about 600 regulars, just before the first volunteers arrived ([NPS ch. 4](https://npshistory.com/publications/cwdw/hrs/chap4-1.htm)). So the regular garrison barely changed through March and early April.
- Commanders: Scott, General in Chief; Stone for the militia; Colonel E. V. Sumner took part in the inauguration plan (snippet) and was named brigadier general on 16 March ([Freeman at Thayer](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/People/Robert_E_Lee/FREREL/1/25*.html)); Captain J. A. Duane's engineers marched before the carriage (snippet). The Department of Washington under Brevet Colonel Charles F. Smith was created only after 15 April ([NPS ch. 4](https://npshistory.com/publications/cwdw/hrs/chap4-1.htm)).
- Stone's defence plan was barricades and strongpoints (City Hall hill, the Capitol, Executive Square, the Treasury as the last stand), no forts ([NPS ch. 4](https://npshistory.com/publications/cwdw/hrs/chap4-1.htm)). On 1 April Lincoln asked Scott for daily reports; on 2 April he visited the Navy Yard to discuss its defence with Commander Dahlgren (same page). Scott asked for ten more District companies on 9 April (snippet; source page not identified among the search results). A late-March Scott order to raise "Silver Brigade" and "Clay Battalion" home-guard units appears in one popular article with no primary citation, so I do not rely on it ([Dorris](https://www.alincolnbygadorris.com/blog/can-we-defend-washington-city-article-59)).

## 16. How fast news reached Washington, and by what means

I have solid evidence for the channels and weak evidence for the four named events.

**Channels, measured.**
- The telegraph linked Washington to Montgomery, Charleston and Richmond through at least 12 April. The commissioners wired Montgomery regularly (letter book: "72 letters, dispatches, and telegrams", [Rosenbach](https://civilwar.rosenbach.org/?p=699)); Wigfall's telegrams of 11 March; Crawford's telegram to Pickens on 1 April ([Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm)); James Harvey's wire of 6 April to Charleston beat Lincoln's messenger, who arrived 8 April ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 158, 215).
- Mail from Washington to Montgomery: "How far is it to Montgomery?" "Three days" (Seward and Campbell, 15 March, same chapter). Charleston to Washington: 2–3 days (question 2). By rail courier, Pensacola to Washington 5 days, Washington to Pensacola 4 days (question 3).

**The four events.**
- **Confederate constitution.** Adopted unanimously 11 March ([Confederate Congress journal extract](https://150yearsago.library.virginia.edu/?p=6191)); the secrecy injunction was lifted and the text printed in a Georgia paper dated 13 March and noted in a Richmond paper of 13 March (snippet; [search result](https://gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu/lccn/sn82015388/1861-03-13/ed-1/seq-2)). So the Southern press had it in 2 days. A New York Herald issue dated 19 March carries it (dealer listing, snippet; [listing](https://napoleonexhibit.com/store/1861-pre-CIVIL-WAR-newspaper-CONFEDERATE-GOVERNMENT-CSA-PASSES-NEW-CONSTITUTION_374820072270.html)). Northern first-printing date of the full text: still UNVERIFIED. **Verified 2026-11-14:** the *Times* of 12 March carried a Montgomery dispatch of Monday 11 March: "A permanent Constitution has been adopted. It has been kept secret", with some provisions leaked (a six-year presidential term) ([NYT, 12 Mar 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-12_10_2955)). So news of adoption reached New York in one day by wire; the walkthrough's "11–13" window holds for the news, not for the text.
- **Missouri convention (19 March).** The date is firm ([Missouri State Archives](https://www.sos.mo.gov/archives/history/timeline/3)). Vote 89–1 or 98–1, unresolved (the printed *Journal and Proceedings* of the convention would settle it: [archive.org](https://ia600402.us.archive.org/15/items/proceedings00knaprich/proceedings00knaprich.pdf), not read). Delay to Washington: UNVERIFIED. **[I]** Same day or next, by wire, via St. Louis papers.
- **Stephens's "Cornerstone" speech (21 March).** The reporter's text comes from the Savannah *Republican*; Atlanta's *Southern Confederacy* printed it on 25 March ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornerstone_Speech)). A book description says more than 200 papers reprinted it "within a few short weeks" ([JSTOR description](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.28697817)) (snippet). **Verified 2026-11-14:** the *Times* printed the speech, including "its corner-stone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man", on 27 March, six days after delivery ([NYT, 27 Mar 1861](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-york-times_the-new-york-times_1861-03-27_10_2968)); it returned to it on 2 April. Whether another Northern paper had it earlier is not checked.
- **Virginia's 4 April vote.** Baldwin was in Washington the same day (Burlingame). A Washington letter of 5 April in the *Philadelphia Inquirer* reports Lincoln's remarks the day after, and Conrad's letter to his wife of 6 April reports callers' news from Washington ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 196, 204). **[I]** Same day by wire from Richmond.

## 17. How Lincoln heard of the Campbell "faith" misunderstanding (8–10 April)

Not documented. The pieces:
- The Seward and Campbell exchanges: 15 March ("Sumter will be evacuated" within days, which Campbell wrote to Davis and the commissioners as confidence it would be "in the next five days"; Wikipedia's Campbell page says "ten days" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Campbell)); Burlingame says five); about 20 March; 1 April (note: the President "may desire to supply Fort Sumter, but will not undertake to do so without first giving notice to Governor Pickens", which Seward took to Lincoln and returned with a modified text); 7 April ("Faith as to Sumter fully kept; wait and see") ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)).
- Seward "consulted briefly with Lincoln" on the 31 March / 1 April notice. That is the last point at which Lincoln is documented as in the loop. The 7 April note is attributed to Seward alone, and Burlingame calls it Seward's reassurance after Campbell relayed the commissioners' alarm at the fleet reports.
- 8 April: Seward's memorandum of 15 March is delivered to the commissioners; they send a blistering letter and leave; Campbell writes of "systematic duplicity" in a letter to Seward of 13 April ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf) fn. 219–220; [Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/sumter.htm), which has the commissioners leaving Washington 11 April).
- Lincoln "emphatically" denied having authorised the evacuation statement (a Washington letter in the *New York Herald*, 15 April, via fn. 87). Whether Lincoln knew of the "faith" note before the firing is UNVERIFIED; the first attested trace is Campbell's 13 April letter. I found no source for how it reached him.
- Historians differ on whether Seward spoke for Lincoln: James Welling said Seward's predictions were "with the full knowledge of Mr. Lincoln" (no source given); Burlingame calls Lincoln's authorisation "possible but highly unlikely"; Potter says Seward's views matched Lincoln's until late and bought four weeks ([SW 2.4]).

## Design implications

These change turn timing or situations in `docs/design/FIRST-MONTH.md`. Row numbers refer to its tables.

1. **Fox's report arrives on 25 March, not about 23 (row 173).** He was in Charleston the night of 21 March and reported in person on return, about 25 March. On the walkthrough's turn dates (23 Mar, 28 Mar) the report lands in turn 6, with Hurlbut's, not turn 5. Turn 5 (23 Mar) holds only the departures: Fox gone since 19 March, Hurlbut and Lamon left 22 March.
2. **Hurlbut's report lands on 27–28 March (row 195): confirmed.** Dated 27 March, read by Lincoln on 28 March. He was in Charleston 24–25 March.
3. **Lamon was not yet marshal (row 179).** He was Lincoln's friend, "Colonel", with a post-office pretext; the marshalship petition was presented on 2 April and the Senate consented on 26 July. The walkthrough's remark that "neither holds an office relevant to the errand" holds. Lamon's report is a letter to Seward (25 March), not Lincoln.
4. **New situation, 29–31 March: the Lamon promise.** Pickens telegraphs complaint (29 March) that Lamon did not return as promised; Seward tells Campbell on 31 March that Lamon had no authority. This is an agent exceeding his errand, with a cost to Lincoln's credit in Charleston, and it dates the point at which Seward's story to the commissioners turns. Fits G6.
5. **The Pickens edge is two edges, not one (line 262).** Outbound by sea, 19 days (12 to 31 March) with a 3-day Army delay at the start; the return courier overland took 5 days (1 to 6 April). Worden, Washington to Pensacola, took 4 days. Telegraph unusable. A good pack edge: `washington→pensacola`, ship about 16 days plus handling; rail courier 4–5 days. The walkthrough's "19–20 days and about 6" becomes "19 and 5".
6. **Pickens landing on 12 April (not 11).** Adams's own report puts the landing the night of the 12th, after Worden delivered the order that day.
7. **A false inference worth a situation, late March.** Lincoln saw the *Brooklyn* had gone to Key West and assumed her troops had been landed or carried off. In fact the troops were on the *Sabine* and nothing had been done; he concluded his order had "fizzled out" (Meigs's diary, via Burlingame). This is a report that is wrong in a way the player cannot detect.
8. **Non-compliance by Scott, found a week late.** Lincoln gave Scott a verbal order on 5 March to hold Pickens; a week later he found nothing done and repeated it in writing (11 March). Burlingame also says Scott ignored the 9 March directive. That is the insubordination ladder's first rung, documented, and the delay in noticing is the point. Fits the report model in [IF].
9. **Cabinet meetings are event-driven, not weekly.** No Tuesday/Friday schedule until later in 1861. Model the cabinet as summoned on the player's action or by Seward, with documented dates 6, 9, 14, 15, 28–29 March and 4–6, 12 April. Walkthrough row 108 (9 March) is right.
10. **Senate: sitting 4–28 March, out after.** A `politics` state of "special session, sitting" for turns 1–6 and "adjourned" from 29 March matches G17. Effects: cabinet confirmed on 5 March (so no confirmation fight to model); ministers to Britain and France were pending around 19 March; patronage nominations (Lamon) hang over to July. Also two Senate pressure events: the 13 March Republican caucus and the Trumbull resolution at the end of March.
11. **Anderson's mail is the adversary's mail (row 315, 68).** Delay 2–3 days; the route ran through Fort Johnson and the Charleston post office; inspection ordered 29 March; cut 8 April; seized and read 9 April. So from 29 March, Anderson's letters are readable by the enemy and Anderson knows it, which is a reason Lincoln sent Fox. Row 315 (Anderson's letter of 1 April, "by mail") should read: sent 1 April, in Washington about 3–4 April, Cameron's reply of 4 April reaches Anderson on 7 April. Row 68 (letter "dated 28 February, about 5 days") should read: reached Washington 4 March, with the 2 March letter.
12. **Thomas's title (row 107).** He took over on 7 March; one field letter of 17 March still calls him "Assistant Adjutant-General". Put "Thomas, acting" in the pack, and do not make an Adjutant General's resignation list a decision.
13. **Lee (row 198).** The commission forwarded 28 March was for a vacancy opened 16 March; Lee was a lieutenant colonel, possibly with a brevet. Note he was in Washington from 1 March, so a "Lee to be colonel" list on 28 March is a list item the player can see arrive.
14. **Commissioners: Lincoln knows by 15 March at the latest (row 70).** The walkthrough has them known "the same day" via press and callers; the evidence is that they stayed out of view and worked through Seward's channel. Change the source type to informal, through Seward, and the date to 9–13 March **[I]**. Their memorandum of 15 March waits until 8 April by consent. Seward's promise to Campbell is dated 15 March, with a second assurance about 20 March.
15. **The press is not mild (row 111).** The evacuation rumour of 10–12 March produced a real backlash within days, including a Republican caucus on 13 March, and the late-March papers called the government drifting. A "front page" item on 12 and 13 March and again on 27–28 March matches the record better than a flat weekly item. The inaugural gets a mixed Northern reception on 5 March.
16. **Virginia is a four-day window (1–4 April) driven by Seward's telegram, with a disputed outcome (row 320).** The offer to Baldwin exists in the record as three versions; model it as a promise whose terms the player cannot confirm. The vote itself should reach Lincoln the same day by wire, and the vote count should be left unstated or shown as 89–45 to 90–45.
17. **The Sumter expedition's departures are staggered across 6–10 April, from three ports (row 341).** Powhatan sailed 6 April and was diverted; *Harriet Lane* 8 April; *Baltic* 9 April; *Pawnee* and *Pocahontas* 10 April from the Virginia capes (verified in the Navy Official Records, 2026-11-14); rendezvous planned for 11 April; actual arrivals 12–13 April (*Harriet Lane* was there before 3 a.m. on the 12th and may have come on the 11th, which only secondary sources say: unresolved). The notice to Pickens leaves 6 April at 6 p.m. and is delivered 8 April; Harvey's telegram of 6 April arrives first.
18. **Campbell's "faith" note (row 342) is a leak, not a message to Lincoln.** The 7 April note appears to be Seward-to-Campbell with no sign that Lincoln saw it. If a wire or informal source tells Lincoln anything on 8–10 April, it is the commissioners' departure and, after 12 April, Campbell's letter of 13 April. Model it as a misunderstanding Lincoln learns of after the first shot.
19. **Patronage (G18).** Twelve hours a day with callers is the right order of magnitude; hours were cut late March. A weekly list with defaults plus an attention tax on turns (a caller-load that eats the player's actions) fits the record better than a per-office card. The tax should be higher in the first two weeks.
20. **The 4 April cabinet choice is documented as a one-man call.** Lincoln acted on 4–6 April after consulting only Welles, Fox and Blair; the cabinet as a body was against giving Pickens notice. That supports a decision where the player overrides a seat-majority opinion, with the cost visible later.

## Findings outside my scope

1. **Seward as a shadow executive, and a rival under the kernel's rules.** Burlingame documents Seward wiring the commissioners' go-between to Montgomery by revising Gwin's message; wiring Summers; his friend Harvey's wire to Charleston on 6 April; his telegram restoring the *Powhatan* signed with his own name; and the 1 April "Some Thoughts" memo. Welles and Blair believed it sabotage; Seward's biographer denied it ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). Candidate for the rivals and insubordination ladder.
2. **Anderson was suspected.** Lincoln told Scott on 29 March that "Anderson had played us false" and asked Holt whether he doubted Anderson's loyalty. Fox noted Anderson's "Southern sympathies" on 21 March. A hidden-loyalty stat for the Sumter commander is justified by the record, though most of the evidence is hindsight ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)).
3. **Lincoln's health and stress as a recorded variable.** He was "so severely indisposed" and "nearly exhausted" on 1 April, did not sleep the night of 28 March, called himself "in the dumps" on 29 March, and Mary Lincoln said he "keeled over with sick headache". Relevant if the player's own attention or morale is modelled (same source).
4. **A compartmentalisation example.** A cabinet member told Stanton he knew nothing of the ministerial appointments until they appeared in the press; Welles knew nothing of the Pickens expedition ordered through Seward; Welles's rival orders of 1 April nearly reorganised the Navy under Barron ([Burlingame ch. 22](https://www.knox.edu/documents/LincolnStudies/BurlingameVol2Chap22.pdf)). Useful for "who knows what" edges between seats.
5. **Errors in [SW] and the walkthrough, to fix:** Fox's report date (23 to 25 March); Pickens landing date (11 to 12 April); reply time (6 to 5 days); "5 days" for the Anderson letter (2–3 days); Hurlbut's "would resist even an unarmed ship" (said it would be "stopped & refused admittance"); the Connecticut election line (see section 12); the 4 April vote count.
6. **Source gaps for the next research pass:** the Senate Executive Journal for March 1861 (still unread after the 2026-11-14 verification pass); the Navy Official Records, Series I, vol. 4 (read 2026-11-14, see section 7); any March 1861 Northern newspaper text (the *New York Times* for March–April 1861 is on archive.org and was used 2026-11-14; the *Tribune*, *Herald* and *World* items remain second-hand); Heitman on Lee's brevets and Thomas's commission; Campbell's "Facts of History" (*Southern Historical Society Papers* 42, 1917) for the 7 April exchange; Lamon's own *Recollections* for his March errand.
7. **Side-channel evidence on recruits and legality.** Burlingame notes the Pickens expedition was funded from the State Department's secret-service fund ($10,000 to Meigs) because Congress had adjourned and made no appropriation. A reminder that the "money" system needs a "discretionary fund the President alone controls" with a visible audit trail, if one is wanted.
8. **Cross-service orders are a refusal path.** Adams's stated reasons for not landing the troops were that Scott's order came from an Army officer and could not supersede his earlier orders from the Navy Secretary, and that landing would break the January truce ([Tulane](https://www2.tulane.edu/~sumter/FinalOrder/FApr6.2.html)); Burlingame adds that three of his sons served the Confederacy. A seat can be compliant to its own department and still refuse another's directive. Relevant to the compliance model.
9. **Anderson's own numbers disagree.** His 28 February letter, as printed by Epperson, speaks of 70,000 men, while Holt's covering letter says 20,000 ([Epperson](https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterHoltScottLincoln0305.htm)). [SW] uses 20,000. **Settled 2026-11-14 in favour of 20,000:** the Official Records do not print the letter in full, but Cameron's written answer to Lincoln's question of 15 March, filed with the President's inquiry, quotes it: "I confess that I would not be willing to risk my reputation on an attempt to throw re-enforcements into this harbor ... with a force of less than twenty thousand good and well-disciplined men" ([OR I,1 p. 197](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150757-197-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)). The papers before the cabinet that day also tabulate the other officers' estimates of 28 February, from 3,000 men (J. C. Davis, Talbot) to Foster's 20,000 volunteers to take the batteries and 30,000 to hold them ([OR I,1 p. 202](https://civilwar.com/official-record/569-charleston/150762-202-series-i-volume-i-serial-1-charleston.html)). The 70,000 appears nowhere in the OR and is probably a transcription error. The pack should carry 20,000.
