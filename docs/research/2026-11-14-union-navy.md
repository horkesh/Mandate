# The Union Navy as a seat, a set of posts and a clock, 1861-65 (research for decision 0028)

Status: first pass complete (2026-11-14), history research lane, Sonnet at high effort. Written for decision 0028 (the navy works through people; the blockade is a pressure clock on Confederate will and foreign patience; river and sea fleets support joint operations as support requests with their own compliance), 0016 and 0026 (goals, plans, support requests) and 0031 (map edges and off-map boxes). Nothing here contradicts those decisions. Two findings sharpen them: the blockade did not break the Confederacy on its own, and joint operations failed far more often from timing and personality than from lack of ships. ## 1. The Navy Department as a seat

**Welles.** Lincoln appointed Gideon Welles, a Connecticut Republican, in March 1861, largely for political and regional balance; Lincoln did not know him well [PRO-SYMONDS], [MLWH-WELLES]. He inherited a department disrupted by the resignation of Southern officers [WIKI-WELLES]. He served all four years, as did only Seward besides [PRO-SYMONDS]. Lincoln called him his "Neptune" [WIKI-WELLES]. Symonds, as summarised in an interview, says Lincoln came to value his earnestness, candour and loyalty even though his blunt manner made enemies [PRO-SYMONDS]. A review of Symonds's book says the relationship was one of trust and that Lincoln kept disappointed admirals on side with kind words (snippet) [SYMONDS-SNIPPET].

**How he ran it.** Efficient, orderly, brusque, a believer in procedure [MLWH-WELLES]. His diary is a major source and is openly one-sided: it shows contempt for Seward, Chase and Stanton and a habit of showing himself well, for instance on the *Powhatan* affair [MLWH-WELLES]. Treat every diary-derived claim as Welles's view. Policy choices that mattered: he kept the navy's focus on the blockade and resisted calls to pull ships off it to hunt raiders or defend the coast (snippet) [NHHC-SNIPPET]. He bought and built at speed (below).

**Fox.** Gustavus Fox, a former naval officer, briefed Lincoln on a Sumter relief plan in the first week of the presidency; Lincoln put him in charge, liked him at once, and created the Assistant Secretary's post for him [PRO-SYMONDS]. The appointment was 1 August 1861 [WIKI-FOX]. He was the brother-in-law (by marriage) of Postmaster General Montgomery Blair [secession note, TUL-Fox]. One source calls him an able administrator, often closer to Lincoln personally than Welles was [MLWH-WELLES]. He pushed hardest for a naval attack on Charleston [PRO-1861NAVY]. He approved the *Monitor*'s name and watched the Hampton Roads fight from the *Minnesota* [WIKI-MONITOR]. **[I]** Welles is the administrator and shield; Fox is the planner with a direct line to Lincoln and personal ties to flag officers (Du Pont was a close friend in autumn 1861 [MLWH-DUPONT]).

**Welles and Seward.** The sharpest rivalry [MLWH-WELLES]. The *Powhatan* episode: Lincoln signed orders on 1 April 1861 for a secret Fort Pickens expedition (Meigs and Lieutenant Porter) that attached the *Powhatan*, which Welles had separately committed to the Sumter fleet; Welles learned of it only after dark on 5 April; Lincoln ordered the ship restored to Sumter, but Meigs and Porter presented orders signed by Lincoln on 6 April and the ship sailed for Pickens (snippet) [TUL-POWHATAN]. Sources disagree on blame (Seward's trickery, Lincoln's inattention to the orders, or nobody's knowledge of the clash) (snippet) [TUL-POWHATAN]. On the blockade, Wikipedia says Welles argued strongly against Seward's proposal and Lincoln overruled him [WIKI-WELLES]; the State Department historian says Welles favoured a de facto, undeclared blockade that would have denied foreign governments grounds to grant belligerent status, and that Lincoln sided with Seward [STATE-BLOCKADE]. These are compatible. The secession-winter note already flags Welles's position as unresolved.

**Welles and the army.** Welles's diary is hard on Stanton, whom he says whispered with Lincoln instead of debating [MLWH-WELLES]. The best-known scene is the cabinet on 9 March 1862, after the *Virginia* sank the *Cumberland* and *Congress*: Welles calls Stanton the most frightened man in the room, urging that McClellan's campaign be abandoned and Burnside recalled, while Welles himself argued the ironclad could not leave the Capes or pass the Potomac shoals and rested his hopes on the *Monitor* [ECW-FRIGHT]. Welles also won Butler's cooperation for New Orleans by letting the expedition go under Butler's name, against McClellan's opposition (McClellan wanted 30,000 to 50,000 troops and the force was cut to about 18,000) [WIKI-JACKSON]. The Western gunboat flotilla began as an Army force with naval officers; Congress's act of 16 July 1862 moved it to the Navy, effective 1 October 1862 (snippet) [ORN-TRANSFER-SNIPPET], [WIKI-MISS-SQ].

**Lincoln as the joint-operations arbiter.** Foote compared army and navy to shears, working only together, and there was no combined command: cooperation was by mutual agreement [BF-GUNBOATS] (the shears line is from a search summary, snippet) [FOOTE-SNIPPET]. Foote preferred to answer only to the navy chain; relations with Halleck and Welles were marked by lack of clear procedure, and Lincoln ended up directing joint operations himself [ECW-FOOTE]. Lincoln used Lieutenant Henry A. Wise at the Washington Navy Yard as a go-between: telegrams from Cairo, Illinois, reached Wise, who read them to Lincoln, took dictated replies and relayed instructions to the officers on the spot [PRO-SYMONDS]. In May 1862 Lincoln went to Fort Monroe (6 May), visited the *Monitor* (7 May) and directed Goldsborough to attack Norfolk; troops landed 10 May and Norfolk was abandoned [NOAA-AFTER].

**Bureaus.** Revised in summer 1862 (adding Steam Engineering under Benjamin F. Isherwood): Yards and Docks (Joseph Smith); Equipment and Recruiting (Andrew H. Foote, then Albert N. Smith); Navigation (Charles H. Davis); Ordnance (John A. Dahlgren, then Henry A. Wise); Construction and Repair (John Lenthall); Provisions and Clothing (Horatio Bridge); Medicine and Surgery (William Whelan) [WIKI-UNIONNAVY].

**The department's growth.** Start of the war: 42 ships in commission and 48 laid up (Wikipedia notes 42 should probably be 41); about 90 in all, only 24 of them steam [WIKI-UNIONNAVY], [BF-READY]. Only 3 were fit for immediate blockade duty [WIKI-BLOCKADE]; one NHHC summary says 12 of the 42 were available (snippet) [NHHC-SNIPPET]. Sailors rose from 7,600 (March 1861) to 22,000 (December 1861) [BF-READY]; ships in commission from 42 to 264 by the end of 1861 [BF-READY]. By 1865: 671 vessels [WIKI-UNIONNAVY]. A search summary of an NHHC publication says Welles bought 418 vessels and built 208 warships (snippet) [NHHC-SNIPPET]; another summary says the 418 are converted merchant ships among the 671 [UNIONNAVY-SNIPPET]. Contracts for 23 *Unadilla*-class gunboats were signed 29 June 1861 [BF-READY]. About 10,000 Black sailors served, roughly 17 percent of the navy's sailors, and refugees were allowed to enlist from September 1861 [WIKI-UNIONNAVY], [BF-READY]. Late 1863 and early 1864 saw a manpower crisis, with men transferred from the army and Confederate prisoners used as crew [PRO-1861NAVY].

## 2. The Blockade Strategy Board

Ordered by Welles on 25 June 1861, at Treasury Secretary Chase's urging. Members: Captain Samuel F. Du Pont (chair), Commander Charles H. Davis (secretary), Army engineer Major John G. Barnard, and Coast Survey superintendent Alexander D. Bache [WIKI-BSB]. It met at the Smithsonian in July to September and then never again [PRO-1861NAVY]. Report count differs: seven reports (5 July to 19 September) [WIKI-BSB] or ten [PRO-1861NAVY].

What it planned [WIKI-BSB]: seize Fernandina as the southern anchor; take at least one South Carolina sound (it preferred St. Helena Sound, closer to Charleston; the administration chose Port Royal); split the Atlantic squadron at Cape Romain; divide the Gulf into six regions with Ship Island as a staging base; attach a Coast Survey vessel to each squadron (adopted and useful). The Atlantic plans were mostly adopted, with Fernandina postponed; Stringham and Butler overruled the advice to block Hatteras Inlet rather than take it. The Gulf plans diverged more because hydrography was poorly known. The board opposed an immediate move up the Mississippi, saying available weapons could not do it; the navy used Ship Island to stage Farragut's attack. Wikipedia calls it the first American attempt to plan a war on rational principles, a rudimentary substitute for a staff [WIKI-BSB] (the judgement is Wikipedia's). Welles split the Atlantic command after Stringham left [PRO-1861NAVY]. The board's minutes are in National Archives Record Group 45 [PRO-1861NAVY].

## 3. Officers who resigned in 1861

- In December 1860 the navy had 1,554 officers; 373 resigned in 1861, of whom 157 were dismissed; about 311 became commissioned or warrant officers in the Confederate navy [PRO-RESIGN].
- Other counts: 677 Southern-born officers, of whom 321 resigned and 350 stayed loyal (snippet) [CWE-SNIPPET]; 259 resigned or were dismissed after the inauguration, per the Secretary's report of 4 July 1861 (snippet) [CWE-SNIPPET]; "almost half" of officers on active duty left, per one battlefield-trust article [BF-READY]. **[I]** "Almost half" probably means almost half of the Southern-born; 373 of 1,554 is about a quarter of all officers.
- Examples: Raphael Semmes resigned to the previous Secretary, Toucey, on 15 February 1861 and later commanded the *Sumter* and *Alabama* [PRO-RESIGN]. Franklin Buchanan resigned, which opened the Washington Navy Yard command that Lincoln wanted Dahlgren to have; Lincoln got Congress to pass a special act because Dahlgren was below the required rank [WIKI-DAHLGREN]. Josiah Tattnall later commanded the *Virginia* for the Confederacy [WIKI-HAMPTON].
- Welles was hard on divided loyalties: he ordered a Marine lieutenant dismissed and sent under guard to Fort Warren in November 1861 [PRO-RESIGN].
- The Gosport (Norfolk) yard was abandoned and burned on 20 April 1861; the Confederates took over 1,000 naval guns, a dry dock and eight warships including the *Merrimack*, leaving the Union with no fuel or repair base between the Chesapeake and the Gulf [BF-READY], [secession note, BF-Gosport].
- Wikipedia notes Du Pont chaired the 1855 efficiency board that removed 201 officers, nearly half later reinstated, and that Farragut, a Virginia-born Unionist, first sat on the Navy Retirement Board in 1861 [WIKI-DUPONT], [WIKI-FARRAGUT]. **[I]** The service's age problem was an old grievance before the war.

**Temperaments and records** (what the sources say; the game's hidden stats are the designer's call, and the cited-centre rule in 0026 applies).

- **Foote:** devout, earnest, a temperance reformer; Du Pont called him earnest, persevering, indomitable. Unusually good at working with Grant, and his backing persuaded Halleck to authorise the Tennessee River attack. Wounded in the foot at Donelson on 14 February; relieved 9 May 1862 largely to recover; later Chief of the Bureau of Equipment and Recruiting; named to the South Atlantic command in 1863 and died 26 June 1863 of kidney trouble, stress and the effects of his wound [ECW-FOOTE], [WIKI-UNIONNAVY], [MLWH-DUPONT]. (The NPS Donelson page says he "died of his wounds", which is looser than the biography [NPS-DONELSON].)
- **Davis:** a scholar more than a sailor, in Welles's phrase; won Memphis (6 June 1862, seven of eight Confederate ships sunk or captured); withdrew from Vicksburg to Helena on his own authority on 28 July 1862 when disease hit his crews; moved to the Bureau of Navigation in September-October 1862 [WIKI-DAVIS], [WIKI-ARKANSAS].
- **Porter:** energetic and resourceful, with ambition Welles called excessive and not always scrupulous, given to boasting; Stanton called him a gas bag. Jumped from commander to acting rear admiral; the rank was made permanent on 4 July 1863. Got on with Grant, was close to Sherman, disliked McClernand, quarrelled with Banks over cotton and with Butler over Fort Fisher. Farragut's foster brother [WIKI-PORTER]. Porter claimed credit for the New Orleans plan; some name Fox as its main author [WIKI-PORTER].
- **Farragut:** Southern-born, Union loyalist; aggressive and, in Wikipedia's phrase, "not always cooperative", as at Port Hudson on 14 March 1863, when he moved his run up to the night before Banks's diversion was in position [WIKI-FARRAGUT]. Rear admiral 16 July 1862; vice admiral 21 December 1864 [WIKI-FARRAGUT].
- **Du Pont:** see table. Warned Welles and Fox that ships alone could not take forts and called a renewed monitor attack "madness" [MLWH-DUPONT]. Lincoln told a journalist "too late", calling the six months of preparation a long grace for two hours of fighting; he did not intervene on Du Pont's behalf, but privately told a congressman he had not censured him [MLWH-DUPONT].
- **Dahlgren:** rocky relations with Gillmore after the September 1863 failures but kept his command [NPS-SUMTER].
- **Wilkes:** combative and high-handed. *Trent* (8 November 1861); Congress thanked him; placed on the retirement list 21 December 1861; commodore 16 July 1862; his West Indies actions drew protests from Britain, Spain, the Netherlands, France, Denmark and Mexico; he seized the *Vanderbilt* for his flagship against her commander's orders; recalled June 1863; court-martialled March 1864, sentenced to reprimand and three years' suspension, which Lincoln cut to one year [WIKI-WILKES].
- **Goldsborough:** see table. **Stringham:** see table.

**Documented friction with army commanders** (full cases in section 6): Goldsborough vs McClellan; Farragut vs Banks (Port Hudson); Du Pont vs Welles/Fox over a navy-only attack; Dahlgren vs Gillmore; Porter vs Butler, McClernand and Banks; Foote vs Halleck's lack of procedure; Stringham and Butler criticised for seeking personal credit after Hatteras [WIKI-HATTERAS].

## 5. The blockade

**Legal basis and growth.** Proclaimed 19 April 1861 for South Carolina to Texas; extended to Virginia and North Carolina on 27 April [PRO-1861NAVY], [secession note, ARCH-Prologue2001]. It covered more than 3,500 miles of coast and 12 major ports, with about 180 possible ports of entry (Wikipedia) [WIKI-BLOCKADE]; one popular article estimates over 200 minor ports [HN-BLOCKADE]. In May 1861 two ships watched about 400 miles of North Carolina coast [HN-BLOCKADE]. Wikipedia gives blockading vessels as about 160 by the end of 1861, and 282 steamers plus 102 sailing vessels in November 1862 [WIKI-BLOCKADE]. The *Prize Cases* (1863) upheld the blockade [WIKI-BLOCKADE]. The Supreme Court's *Springbok* decision (December 1866) condemned the cargo of a British ship captured on 3 February 1863 bound for Nassau, extending the doctrine of continuous voyage; the ship itself was freed (snippet) [SPRINGBOK-SNIPPET].

**Ports and the events that closed them.** Hatteras Inlet (28-29 August 1861; first combined operation) [WIKI-HATTERAS]. Port Royal (7 November 1861), the coaling and repair base the Atlantic coast lacked [WIKI-PORTROYAL]. Roanoke Island (7-8 February 1862) [WIKI-ROANOKE]. New Orleans (April 1862). Galveston briefly occupied, lost on 1 January 1863 with the *Harriet Lane*, blockade lifted for four days; Galveston stayed the last major open port, with the last successful escape in May 1865 [WIKI-GALVESTON], [WIKI-BLOCKADE]. Charleston's ironclads broke out and the Union restored the blockade the next day (January 1863) [ECW-PARIS]. Charleston stayed in Confederate hands until evacuated on 15-17 February 1865 [BF-CHARLESTON] (the Du Pont biography page says April 1865, which conflicts [MLWH-DUPONT]). Mobile Bay (5 August 1864) [WIKI-MOBILE]. Wilmington, behind Fort Fisher, the last open Atlantic port (fort fell 15 January 1865); over 300 ships entered it in 1864 [HN-BLOCKADE], [BF-FISHER]. **[I]** Four ports, Wilmington, Charleston, Mobile and Galveston, matter; Nassau, Bermuda and Havana are where cargoes changed hands, and Matamoros was the Texas bypass (the Strategy Board noted Brownsville and Matamoros as a diplomatic problem) [WIKI-BLOCKADE], [WIKI-BSB].

**The debate.** Wise treats blockade running as a lifeline; reviewers report that he thinks supplies never decided a battle and that manpower and the cotton-withholding policy mattered more [WISE-REVIEWS]. Symonds argues the main effect was economic, not military: it cut non-military goods, fed inflation (made worse by paper money), and wore down households, though he concedes that cannot be measured statistically [DEADCONF]. Trammell argues the blockade largely failed and that inflation, paper currency, cotton policy and poor railways did most of the damage [HN-BLOCKADE]. Wikipedia says historians estimate smuggled supplies extended the war by up to two years and that economists give the blockade a prominent role [WIKI-BLOCKADE]. Surdam (*Naval War College Review*, 1998, and a 2001 book) argues that counting goods that slipped through misses the blockade's main effects, including on cotton exports (snippet; the article itself was not read) [SURDAM-SNIPPET]. A Confederate counter-move: from late 1863 the government claimed one-third and then, by March 1864, half of cargo space on runners (the "New Plan", mostly Wilmington and Charleston) [EOA-RUNNING].

**Diplomatic weight.**
- Britain recognised Confederate belligerency on 13 May 1861, Spain 17 June, Brazil 1 August [STATE-BLOCKADE].
- The Declaration of Paris (1856) says a blockade binds only if effective; the US never signed, but Britain and France demanded the test and followed the Union blockade closely (Admiral Milne told his captains to observe and report) [ECW-PARIS].
- Lord Lyons doubted in April 1861 that an effective blockade of such a coast was possible; by November he called it "very far from a mere paper blockade"; Russell instructed on 15 February 1862 that escapes by some ships did not defeat it in law and never officially departed from that [ADAMS-VIII]. Gregory's Commons motion of 7 March 1862 that the blockade was ineffective was withdrawn [ADAMS-VIII].
- Friction: the "Southern Ports" bill (June-August 1861) was read in London as a paper blockade, and Seward dropped its offending section by the proclamation of 19 August; the "Stone Fleet" scheme to sink ships in Charleston harbour drew Russell's protest on 20 December 1861 and was dropped in February 1862 [ADAMS-VIII]. British objections to the waiting-outside-territorial-waters practice, the treatment of crews and seizures of mail [STATE-BLOCKADE]. Seward's offer to adhere to the Declaration of Paris failed over a privateering condition [ECW-PARIS].
- *Trent*: Wilkes stopped her on 8 November 1861 without orders from Washington; Britain demanded release and apology and sent troops to Canada (over 8,000 or 11,000 depending on source, snippets); Seward's note of 26 December released Mason and Slidell, conceding Wilkes should have taken the ship to a prize court [STATE-TRENT], [TRENT-SNIPPET].
- Limits on British action: abolitionist sentiment limited Palmerston; Napoleon III wanted joint action with Britain and was busy in Mexico [STATE-BLOCKADE]. Britain's cotton: about five-sixths of its 1860 imports came from the US, and British cotton prices rose 150 percent by summer 1861; British investment in runners was about GBP 50 million, about half profitable [WIKI-BLOCKADE]. **UNVERIFIED:** the size and timing of the Lancashire "cotton famine" and the autumn 1862 mediation question; I did not research them.

**Raiders.** Welles refused to strip the blockade to chase them (snippet) [NHHC-SNIPPET]. Wilkes's squadron failed to catch the *Alabama* or *Florida* [WIKI-WILKES]. The *Kearsarge* sank the *Alabama* off Cherbourg on 19 June 1864 [PRO-RESIGN].

## 6. Rivers and joint operations: named cases

There was no combined command in the Civil War; cooperation rested on mutual agreement between a general and a flag officer [BF-GUNBOATS], [NPS-DONELSON]. The pattern below is **[I]**, built from the cases.

|---|---|---|---|
| Hatteras Inlet | 28-29 Aug 1861 | Stringham's 7 ships and Butler's about 880 troops; surf swamped landing boats, only about a third landed at first; forts fell; 691 prisoners; first combined operation. ## 7. Ironclads and technology, as events

- 3 August 1861: Congress appropriates $1.5 million for armoured steamers; Welles sets up the Ironclad Board (Joseph Smith, Hiram Paulding, Charles H. Davis), which reviewed 17 designs and recommended 3 [WIKI-MONITOR], [WIKI-HAMPTON]. Wikipedia says Lincoln overruled the board's doubts about Ericsson's design; the contract was signed 4 October 1861 for $275,000; launched 30 January 1862; commissioned 25 February [WIKI-MONITOR]. *Galena* and *New Ironsides* were started the same year [BF-READY].
- 8-9 March 1862, Hampton Roads: the *Virginia* sank the *Cumberland*, forced the *Congress* to surrender and grounded the *Minnesota*; the *Monitor* arrived the night of 8 March and fought her to a draw on 9 March [WIKI-HAMPTON]. It kept McClellan's fears alive until the *Virginia* was burned on 11 May after Norfolk fell on 9-10 May [WIKI-HAMPTON], [NOAA-AFTER] (Wikipedia's Hampton Roads page dates the Norfolk evacuation 9 May; the NOAA page says 10 May).
- Limits found immediately: the *Monitor*'s turret could not elevate guns for high forts (Drewry's Bluff, 15 May 1862), and the turrets of the monitors jammed at Charleston on 7 April 1863 [WIKI-MONITOR]. Sixty ironclads were built (Wikipedia) [WIKI-MONITOR]; twenty *Casco*-class river monitors were planned but not completed as monitors [PRO-1861NAVY].
- The *Monitor* sank under tow on 31 December 1862 with 16 men lost [WIKI-HAMPTON].
- Mines and spars: the *Tecumseh* lost to a mine at Mobile Bay, 5 August 1864 [WIKI-MOBILE]; Cushing sank the ironclad *Albemarle* with a spar torpedo on 27-28 October 1864, ending her control of the Roanoke after she had helped Hoke take Plymouth in April 1864 [WIKI-ALBEMARLE]; the Confederate submarine *Hunley* sank the *Housatonic* off Charleston on 17 February 1864 (snippet) [HOUSATONIC-SNIPPET].
- The *Arkansas* run through two Union fleets on 15 July 1862 [WIKI-ARKANSAS].

**[I]** These are events the shell can raise (a scare, a new ship, a loss) with effects on a few typed terms, not a tech tree.

## Design implications

1. **Posts and holders.**
   - **Navy Department** is one post with two seats: the Secretary (Welles: allocates ships and priorities, resists diversion to raiders and coast defence, quick to relieve) and the Assistant Secretary (Fox, from 1 August 1861: plans, pushes aggressive options, has his own line to Lincoln and flag-officer friendships). Their compliance differs: Welles obeys Lincoln but resists Seward and the army; Fox favours action. The player directs them through goals, not ships.
   - **Squadron posts** as in the table, **created by directive**, not at the start. The blockade exists from 19 April 1861; the Atlantic and Gulf squadrons form in May; the splits of 29 October 1861 and early 1862 are decisions the Department (and Strategy Board, 25 June) makes, and the player can push or refuse. Add the **West India (flying) squadron** (Wilkes) as the raider-hunting post, in tension with blockade strength.
   - **Western Flotilla** starts Army-owned, held by naval officers (Rodgers, Foote, Davis) and **moves to the Navy Department on 1 October 1862** as the Mississippi Squadron under Porter. Model this as one post changing departments, which changes who its compliance answers to, and as an event for the player to prepare for or ignore. Ellet's ram fleet is a second Army-owned post that passed to the Navy with the squadron after Memphis fell (June 1862) [WIKI-MISS-SQ].
   - **The Strategy Board** is a temporary post (25 June to September 1861) with four holders; its seven (or ten) reports are plans the Department adopts, adapts or ignores.
   - **Bureau chiefs** are advisers or traits (Dahlgren at Ordnance, Foote at Equipment, Davis at Navigation) rather than posts to command.
   - **Resignations** are a timed event: 373 of 1,554 officers leave over 1861 (157 dismissed), draining and replacing post-holders from February; the Gosport loss on 20 April removes a base.
   - **Relief** is usually Welles's act after a reputation event (Stringham 18 September 1861 after Hatteras; Goldsborough September 1862; Davis September-October 1862; Du Pont 5 July 1863), with Lincoln able to override or soften (Wilkes's sentence).
   - **Traits worth tracking** (designer's call, citing the pack): caution against forts (Goldsborough, Du Pont) versus aggression (Farragut, Porter); willingness to subordinate to the army (Foote and Porter with Grant versus Goldsborough with McClellan, Dahlgren with Gillmore); loyalty to the Department chain; health (Foote, Davis's crews, Farragut's sick men); ambition and credit-seeking (Porter, Stringham); patronage (Fox, Blair, Du Pont).

2. **The blockade clock.** Two clocks, not one, plus per-port state.
   - **Confederate will clock (slow drain, capped).** The sources do not support the blockade breaking the Confederacy alone: Wise's reviewers say supplies never lost a battle; Trammell says it largely failed; Symonds says its effect was economic and wearing and cannot be measured. So cap the blockade's share of the will terms and let it act mainly through finance, prices and household hardship, with armies and territory carrying the rest **[I]**. Show its terms (hard rule 6).
   - **Drivers (inputs):** coverage (ships on station per coastal sector against coastline length: 3,500 miles; 12 major ports); fast steamers on station versus sail; bases (Port Royal, Key West, Hampton Roads, Ship Island, Pensacola); the stock of open ports and which are closed; runner interception rate; cotton export index; price index. Closing a port is an event with a typed cause: Hatteras (Aug 1861), Port Royal (Nov 1861), Roanoke (Feb 1862), New Orleans (Apr 1862), Mobile Bay (Aug 1864), Fort Fisher (Jan 1865), Charleston (Feb 1865). Remaining open ports carry more traffic (Wilmington over 300 entries in 1864) so the last ports matter most.
   - **Confederate leak channels:** Nassau, Bermuda, Havana, Matamoros; the "New Plan" (one-third then half of cargo space) for the Confederate side.
   - **Calibration points (counts, not single cells, per 0028):** 3 ships fit for blockade duty in April 1861 and 42 in commission; two ships for 400 miles of North Carolina in May 1861; about 160 blockading vessels at the end of 1861; 282 steamers plus 102 sailing vessels in November 1862; 671 vessels in 1865; runner success of about 1,000 in 1,300 (Wise), or about 2 in 3 to 3 in 4 for steamers in 1863-64 (Symonds); capture rate about 1 in 9 in 1861-62, 1 in 3 in 1863-64, about 1 in 2 by 1865 (one secondary source; verify against Wise before using); cotton exports down about 95 percent and shipping out of Confederate ports down over 90 percent in the first year; 1,504 runners captured or destroyed; 300-plus ships into Wilmington in 1864. Where sources disagree (see section 5), calibrate to the overlap, not to either extreme.
   - **Foreign-patience clock (separate, worked through the Secretary of State seat per FOUNDATION).** The legal test is "generally effective", and Britain accepted that from late 1861 (Russell's instruction of 15 February 1862) even while runners got through. **Raises patience risk:** incidents by flag officers or the Department (the *Trent*, 8 November 1861; the Stone Fleet, December 1861; Wilkes's actions in neutral waters, 1862-63; the *Springbok* capture, 3 February 1863; Brownsville and Matamoros); a visible leak that lets Parliament move that the blockade is ineffective (7 March 1862); British cotton hardship. **Lowers it:** a blockade tight enough to pass the test; the release of Mason and Slidell (26 December 1861); Union victories; abolitionist sentiment limiting Palmerston. Britain's response to the *Trent* was troop movements to Canada, which the off-map Britain and Canada box in 0031 can carry. Treat the *Trent* as a scripted crisis whose cause is a captain acting without orders, and let the player choose to release or not.
   - **The raider trade-off** is a real choice for the Welles seat: keep ships on the blockade (Welles's choice) or divert them to raiders (Wilkes's squadron, which failed).

3. **How joint-operation support requests should behave.**
   - **Both chains stay separate.** There is no joint commander; the request is a directive from the army commander's post to the naval post (or the reverse), answered by the holder's own compliance. The player can arbitrate, as Lincoln did, by a go-between (see outside scope) or by a direct order to one of them, which costs standing with the other.
   - **Task type decides base odds [I].** High: transport and ferrying troops, running batteries to open a crossing (Island No. 10, Vicksburg 1863), covering a landing, passing forts when the aim is passage (New Orleans, Mobile Bay), supplying a river line. Low: reducing a high fort by ships alone (Donelson, Drewry's Bluff, Charleston 7 April 1863, 151 rounds against 2,209) unless the army is on the ground.
   - **Compliance terms:** rapport between the two named people (Foote-Grant, Porter-Grant and Sherman, Terry-Porter good; Goldsborough-McClellan, Dahlgren-Gillmore, Porter-Butler, Porter-Banks and Farragut-Butler poor); service pride about command and credit; physical limits (river stage, draught, gun elevation, tides, mines, disease); orders from the other chain (Welles and Fox pressing Du Pont, Grant taking troops Canby needed).
   - **Failure modes to model:** desynchronisation (Port Hudson, Sumter assault, Fort Fisher December: both sides ready on different days); one side withdrawing without telling the other (Butler); the navy leaving because the army is not there (Farragut, July 1862); an army timetable fixed against a river calendar (Red River, with A. J. Smith's loan expiring at the end of April and the river not rising).
   - **Success modes:** a standing shared signal system and joint plan (Terry-Porter), a commander willing to take a risk for the army (Walke), and a department that sets up the plan jointly rather than navy-only (Charleston's counter-example).
   - **The navy can refuse the army and the Department can overrule the commander.** Flag officers can be released or ordered to stay by Welles (Farragut's departure was allowed on 23 July 1862; Du Pont was pushed to attack), so a support request needs the Department's consent as a second term.
   - **Reports about joint operations arrive through two chains** with different bias terms (army and navy), which fits 0032; Du Pont did not tell Lincoln of his doubts on 16 October 1862 [MLWH-DUPONT].

4. **What the river and sea edges of the map need.**
   - **Edge attributes:** draught or depth limit by season (Red River spring 1864; Mississippi stage; Tennessee and Cumberland floods in February 1862 helped and hindered); which flotilla can use the edge; obstruction state (chain, raft, torpedoes, dam); and, at each key-point region, whether the objective is **passage** or **reduction** (forts at Henry, Donelson, Island No. 10, Fort Pillow, Vicksburg, Port Hudson, Jackson and St. Philip, Drewry's Bluff, Mobile Bay, Charleston, Fort Fisher).
   - **Naval bases as key points:** Hampton Roads and Fort Monroe, Port Royal, Key West, Ship Island, Pensacola, Cairo, Beaufort NC. Gosport's loss on 20 April 1861 and Port Royal's capture are the two base events.
   - **Coast sectors, not 180 regions:** major ports as key-point regions; minor inlets grouped into coast segments with a coverage score.
   - **Off-map boxes (0031):** Nassau, Bermuda, Havana and Matamoros as blockade-running ports; Britain and Canada, France and Mexico; two or three high-seas raider zones. The Trent crisis and the Canada deployment justify keeping the Canada box.
   - **River edges are supply and troop highways** that a gunboat flotilla opens and that an ambush or falling water can close (Red River); a region on a river edge needs its own control state for the bank, since the *Arkansas*, Galveston and Port Hudson all show ships holding water while soldiers decide the land.
   - **Army-owned river forces** (Western Flotilla to 1 October 1862, Ellet's rams) need a department owner on the edge.

## Findings outside my scope

1. **Black sailors and the sensitive-history gate (0021, 0026).** About 10,000 Black sailors served, about 17 percent of the navy's sailors, and the navy let refugees enlist from September 1861 [WIKI-UNIONNAVY], [BF-READY]. The navy took freedpeople before the army did **[I]**; any navy modelling must go through the placement rule (their agency, not a recruitment pool).
2. **Cotton, prizes and corruption link the Treasury seat to the blockade.** Prize money ($25 million) and the Porter-Banks race for cotton on the Red River (Porter's barges took cotton as prizes; Banks let speculators follow the army) [WIKI-BLOCKADE], [WIKI-REDRIVER] link naval conduct to Treasury permits and Army discipline, and they bear on how the civil-liberties and trade-policy features are authored. Neither the Treasury seat nor the cotton permits were in my questions.
3. **Lincoln's go-between and a naval route into the information model (0032).** Lieutenant Henry A. Wise at the Navy Yard relayed Western Theater telegrams to Lincoln and Lincoln's replies back [PRO-SYMONDS]; Wise later became chief of Ordnance [WIKI-UNIONNAVY]. The navy's reports reach the president by routes that bypass the War Department's telegraph office, which should be tested against the "filtering seat" design.
4. The Navy competes with the Army for manpower (men transferred from the army in late 1863 and early 1864) [PRO-1861NAVY]; this belongs to the manpower design.
5. Butler's record with the navy (Hatteras, New Orleans, Fort Fisher) fits the army-side commander dossier: he got the New Orleans command under a deal with Welles and was relieved on 8 January 1865 [WIKI-JACKSON], [WIKI-FISHER1].
7. The secession-winter note lists "the home-waters ship count" and Welles's blockade position as UNVERIFIED. This note partly answers both (42 ships in commission, about 90 in all, 3 fit for blockade duty; Welles preferred an undeclared blockade, Lincoln sided with Seward [WIKI-UNIONNAVY], [BF-READY], [STATE-BLOCKADE]). That note's open items can be updated. Also, the claim that Lincoln overruled the Ironclad Board on the Monitor rests on Wikipedia alone [WIKI-MONITOR].
6. Du Pont, Dahlgren and the Charleston failures bear on the 1864 election's "popular general" question (0026) only indirectly; I found nothing on admirals as candidates.
