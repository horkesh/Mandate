# Economic and foreign inputs as data drafts

S15 research, 20 November 2026. [Source register](2026-11-20-s15-sources.md), [economy drafts](../../packs/union-1861/source/drafts/economy/README.md), [foreign drafts](../../packs/union-1861/source/drafts/foreign/README.md). Facts cite pages actually read; **[I]** marks arithmetic and proposed rulings. No file is loaded, imported or generated. No clock formula, acceptance band, gate signature, situation wording or placement is added.

## Data contract and information timing

**[I]** Each row names a unit, measure, source perimeter and time window. A reported point is different from a source range, an approximate claim, a strict lower bound and an unavailable observation. Blank commodity cells are retained with `not_reported`, without filling them from adjacent months. Authority, payment, annual production, claimed monthly capacity and field inventory are different series. Later historical reconstructions supply retrospective comparison evidence; they do not put private information on Lincoln's desk.

The Treasury report is dated **4 December 1862**, while its cotton and breadstuffs appendices are dated **26 December**. A fiscal-year quantity in those tables describes a period ending **30 June**, not information delivered to Washington on 30 June. [T62 title/report p.1; pp.223,243] **[I]** The files preserve the appendix date and have no automatic knowledge route. This is especially important for a run ending at the midterms or in November.

The same discipline applies to the Navy's **7 December 1863** report. Its table ends with reports received to **31 October 1863** and includes earlier captures. [N63 report opening, PDF 9; p.562] **[I]** A capture's month is not the month its report reached the Navy Department, was adjudicated, became public or reached the President.

## Finance, law and prices

The finance CSV preserves the Treasury's FY1862 receipts, gross borrowing, gross expenditure and current disbursements; its initial internal-revenue quarter; dated note/deposit balances; and October/November current payments. The source treats **$529,692,460.50** of loan receipts as a gross instrument flow, while **$474,744,778.16** is spending after removing specified debt transactions. [T62 pp.3,10,31-32,43] **[I]** Their difference is not a defensible net deficit without reconciling turnover and opening/closing balances.

The February note authority of **$150m** includes **$50m replacing demand notes**, and July's additional **$150m** includes at least **$50m reserved** for deposit repayments. The acts preserve coin/demand-note exceptions for import duties and coin interest rules; authority alone is not money received or specie available abroad. [LT62 pp.345-346; LN62 pp.532-533] The July income-duty provisions assess/collect the previous calendar year's income beginning **1 May 1863**, payable by **30 June 1863**. At income above $10,000, the five-percent provision uses income above $600, not just the portion above $10,000; deductions and the government-security provision also matter. [TX62 pp.473-474] **[I]** Reject a July 1862 receipt equal to the whole later annual income-duty yield.

The July act also construes the earlier **$20m direct-tax** authority as **one tax**, with no further tax under it until **1 April 1865**. [TX62 p.489, §119] **[I]** Do not turn the earlier act's annual language into an automatic repeated $20m receipt during 1862-64. This is a later amendment affecting interpretation of S5's earlier-law summary; existing research notes are not silently rewritten.

Confederate receipts through **1 August** are **$302,555,196.60**; the **$328,748,830.70** expenditure statement includes **$26,193,634.10** credited but not yet paid, and **$18,524,128.15** of unwarranted requisitions is separate. The all-note total is **$206,044,035**; remaining general-currency authority is computed within another grouping. [OR42 pp.59-60] **[I]** Neither a tax-only account nor a gold cash balance is represented by these totals. The March account is a different window; its source misprints the start year and contains line discrepancies documented in S5. [M62 pp.3-6]

The price CSV preserves Schwab's monthly paper dollars per gold dollar and selected commodity multiples of **1860 = 1**, alongside Chase's dated Union quotation ranges. November Confederate exchange **2.9** means paper dollars for a gold dollar; salt **42** is a relative-price multiple, not forty-two dollars or a salt quantity. [SCH p.167; Appendix I, foldout opposite p.312] Chase's **29-30%** gold premium is specifically **29 November**, after election day. [T62 pp.13-14] **[I]** These are not a representative household index, wages, national scarcity percentages or measured confidence probabilities.

## Foreign pressure: a sequence rather than a countdown

The diplomatic CSV records a distinct incident, dispatch, receipt, presentation, decision or implementation. The Trent allowed-answer period is **seven days**, with formal presentation on **23 December 1861**, reply on the 27th, release **1 January 1862**, and London news on the 8th/9th. [A1 pp.212-213,230,232,234] **[I]** Starting a Washington countdown at the British dispatch date removes the actual communication route.

Russell's unofficial **10 February** interview with Mason did not accept credentials. His **15 February** instruction accepted the blockade's legal effectiveness. Those actions are different from recognizing a sovereign Confederate state or accepting every capture. [A1 pp.235,267] Gladstone's **7 October** speech lacked Cabinet authorization; the late-October six-month armistice proposal included suspension of the blockade; the formal French presentation was **10 November**, followed by British Cabinet discussions and a reply declining a present overture. [A2 pp.47-49,59-65] Private correspondence, public speeches and formally delivered proposals have different evidentiary status. The separate French dispatch/interview dating conflict noted by S5 remains unresolved; the October 31 row describes notice of an instruction **about to be sent**, exactly the stage Adams supplies. [A2 p.60; S5's CALL p.161 comparison]

Cotton arrivals, public relief and official exports are inputs to what observers might report, not measured foreign-policy coefficients. Adams's **11,500 bales** received from America in the six months through May 1862 is an importer-side series; the United States official FY1862 **5,064,564 lb** cotton export total has a different jurisdiction and accounting window. [A2 p.9; T62 p.223] Lancashire public poor relief reaches about **113,000** in March and **284,418** in December; December is later than this lane's November endpoint, and public/private relief may overlap. [A2 p.12] **[I]** No unemployment-to-recognition threshold or daily increment is calibrated.

## Wider industry and rail evidence

The prewar baselines use the same preliminary census tables as S5: annual pig/rolled iron output and commercial rail mileage by census state. [C60 pp.170,230-231] **[I]** They locate plant and network extent, not delivered wartime weapons or serviceable November rail capacity. The state entries are geographic, including all census Virginia; they are not faction-controlled track.

New contemporary evidence comes from *Scientific American*'s **15 February 1862** article. It reports **10,500** guns fabricated in the preceding month and **44,072** turned out from **30 June 1861 to 31 January 1862**, at Springfield. It separately gives carriage-department contracts for **150** battery wagons, **150** forges, **1,000** carriages, **4,000** harness sets and **60,000** accouterments during the preceding five months. [SA62 p.98] **[I]** “Preceding month” is interpreted as January from the dated issue. This is a magazine claim, not an independently read armory return; contract placement is not completed manufacture, and Springfield is not the whole Union armaments system.

The Confederate rows retain Schwab's account of **more than 14,000** small arms by a September report and public/private claimed capacities above **2,000/1,500 monthly**. He warns against assuming those capacities were achieved. His **200,000 lb** saltpetre figure by October is an input commodity, not powder. The separate million pounds of Augusta powder belongs to the year ending July 1863 and is excluded from November stocks. [SCH pp.269-271] **[I]** Original monthly ordnance returns and actual serviceable deliveries remain necessary.

Ripley's original **21 November 1862** report adds a stronger Union comparison. It reports articles **provided by fabrication and purchase** from the beginning of the rebellion through **30 June**, then separately supplies **furnished** in that period and stocks at arsenals/depots at fiscal close. Foot-soldier small arms are **867,303 provided**, **968,840 furnished**, and **335,896 in depot stock**; mounted-troop arms are **393,294**, **307,846**, and **134,009**, respectively. [OR32 pp.849-852] **[I]** Procurement is not domestic production, and issues can draw on earlier stocks. A negative provided-minus-furnished difference is not proof of impossible production or a reason to alter either total. Depot stocks exclude arms already with troops and do not establish serviceability or unit receipts.

The report separately claims Springfield capacity of at least **200,000 stand annually**, probably **240,000** from the following January; the scan corrects OCR's lost zero. It forecasts **220,440** small arms from private manufacturers in the next six months, then annual private rates of **350,000 muskets/rifles** and **115,000 other arms**. The source's overlapping total exceeds **700,000 annually**. [OR32 p.852] **[I]** The January forecast concerns 1863 and these are capacity/forecast claims, not monthly achieved production. The separate saltpetre reserve is **3,822,704 lb on 4 March 1861** and over **9 million lb** when reporting, claimed sufficient for **12 million lb of powder**. Ripley acknowledges imported saltpetre while claiming increased reserves and future independence. [OR32 p.853] **[I]** The powder equivalence is a source claim, not finished powder stock or a universal conversion rule.

The appendix supplies actual fabrication: **109,810 muskets/rifles at the National Armory** in its cumulative small-arms account, separate from foreign/domestic purchases. Its fiscal-year arsenal-production statement includes **309 field,321 siege,212 sea-coast carriages** and separately enumerated rifle, musket, carbine, pistol and blank cartridges. [OR32 pp.857-858] It also gives **9,054,435 lb saltpetre at June 30**. [OR32 p.858] **[I]** These actual-work periods differ from capacity and the January magazine observation; the fiscal arsenal year is expanded to 1 July 1861 through 30 June 1862, while the cumulative armory opening day stays unknown. The appendix's small-arms cartridges issued (**212,806,501**) and remaining (**58,668,402**) differ from the narrative by one and two. The five printed cartridge-fabrication categories sum to **234,968,863 [I]**, forty below the appendix's **234,968,903** fabrication aggregate. [OR32 pp.851-852,857,859] The latter balance table begins with opening rebellion stocks; its fabrication window is not explicitly the same as the fiscal statement's. **[I]** The difference must not be assigned to an error or an earlier month without reconciliation. The thirty-five-row original-return table preserves dated stocks, fabrication, source variants and forecasts without forcing agreement or assigning daily rates. A complete reconstruction requires resolving the printed variants and reporting periods first.

McCallum's **26 May 1866** official retrospective supplies line-specific 1862 observations. On taking his appointment, he describes a single seven-mile military line, Washington-Alexandria. His **14 March** order called for five locomotives and eighty cars to be held on vessels for the Peninsula; they reached White House early in May, with another engine added in June. [MC66 pp.3-4] **[I]** The order is not a March delivery or national stock.

The Orange and Alexandria opened to Manassas in late March, Warrenton Junction in April and Culpeper in August; the respective distances from Alexandria are **26,39,61 miles**. Six miles were relaid before the August opening. At the late-August withdrawal McCallum reports losses of **seven locomotives and 295 cars**. [MC66 pp.4-5] The same narrative describes limited November reopening, not continuous operation throughout the intervening months. [MC66 p.5] **[I]** Do not add overlapping reaches or subtract the same losses again through Meigs's broader approximate **11-engine/400-car** eastern account. [OR32 p.795]

The Aquia-Fredericksburg line, fifteen miles, was abandoned **7 September**, with **one engine and 57 cars** lost; repairs began **18 November** and it reopened to Falmouth on the **28th**. Norfolk and Petersburg's eastern portion came into military charge **22 July**, followed by gauge conversion over **44 miles** and an August connection to the Seaboard and Roanoke. The **35,153 tons** of stores on the same page cover **22 July 1862 to 30 June 1863**, so they are not adopted as a November or daily-throughput figure. [MC66 pp.5-6] **[I]** These observations support dated line states and repair explanations while leaving tons-per-day and equipment-serviceability coefficients unmeasured.

## An original Confederate company return

Richmond & Danville's superintendent, Chas. G. Talcott, reports on **6 December 1862** for the fiscal year **1 October 1861 to 30 September 1862**. Gross earnings including telegraph are **$724,430.00**, of which government transportation is reported separately as **$226,053.12**. [RD62 superintendent's unnumbered opening p.[231], PDF 23] **[I]** The amounts are company-account dollars and do not measure delivered physical supply or the government's cash position. The report is later than November.

The physical return gives **94,353.17 tons carried**, **4,741,354.19 ton-miles**, an average freight distance of **50.25 miles**, and reported freight-train means of **29.68 tons and eight cars**. Its previous-year means are **38.05 tons and ten cars**. The tonnage includes coal and company/log/stone work, as well as other customers and Confederate freight. [RD62 p.245] **[I]** These are annual actual-work measures and mean loads, not a rated maximum, a daily capacity or exclusively military deliveries. The average distance is rounded; its product with tonnage need not exactly equal the printed ton-miles.

The car inventory is explicitly dated **1 October**. Its seventeen categories include **180 eight-wheel box cars,41 eight-wheel flats,54 six-wheel iron coal cars** and passenger, service and other freight vehicles. [RD62 p.241] Their sum is **387 cars [I]**. The table of locomotive condition is instead dated **30 September**: **22 named engines [I]**, of which **seven [I]** have explicit shop/repair descriptions, including W. P. Tunstall described as presently unfit. Others include running engines needing repairs and Amelia in fair running order. Planet is excluded from that table after conversion to a stationary shop drive. [RD62 p.264, table O; p.240] **[I]** Nominal listed engines, running engines, work-in-shop and usable haulage are not identical quantities; the CSV preserves conditions rather than assigning a universal serviceability fraction.

Talcott describes timber replacement covering **23 miles of sills and eight miles of stringers**, about **22 miles of very worn heavy rail** and **four miles of flat bar needing renewal or substitution**. Machinery left Manchester for temporary shops in May; some returned after the fiscal close. He links unavailable machinery and heavy use to repair difficulties and accidents, as his explanation. [RD62 pp.236,238-240,242] **[I]** This supports a named-line maintenance problem with a reportable cause, not universal collapse of every Southern railroad or a quantitative accident multiplier.

The **5 September** War Department communication separately says the Piedmont company secured its charter and began operations, but the Department was not informed of progress. [RE62 unnumbered communication, PDF p.4] **[I]** A contract, government financial aid and work begun must not create a finished rail connection. This original return improves S5's missing named Confederate evidence while leaving route-by-route monthly serviceability and delivery returns open.

## Monthly captures: what the new series actually counts

The Navy's later table supplies a recoverable monthly subset after rotating its sideways pages. S15 transcribes the printed 1862 groups in the **named-vessel** section, with a separate bin for entries lacking a month. Explicit ditto dates are expanded [I]; blank dates remain year-only. Source names are retained with minimal punctuation/spacing normalization. [N63 pp.562-582]

**[I]** The resulting **337 named-vessel records** include **322 with a month** and **15 without one**. Monthly counts are **18,26,38,49,40,37,19,23,13,19,22,18**, January through December. The row register and aggregation CSV make the calculation inspectable. December is a later comparison, not a November input. These counts reproduce a declared source subset, not uncertainty-free estimates of all actual captures.

The table explicitly mixes blockade violations with battle captures and destruction. Cargo-only rows inside the named section include cotton, coal and molasses; these are excluded. The later unnamed/property tables include multi-boat rows and non-vessel property; those sections are excluded rather than counted one row equals one vessel. The printed year groups are preserved, including the T entries on p.581 under 1863; no unsourced date repair is applied. [N63 pp.565-566,575,581,583-586] **[I]** Consequently the monthly column is neither all blockade captures nor unique hulls, condemnations, tonnage, attempts or a capture probability.

The earlier **543 cumulative** Navy seizure figure, and its **390 additional** reports since the previous annual communication, have another cutoff and definition. They are not added to, or forced to equal, the monthly named-register series. [N62 pp.42-43] **[I]** A clean national blockade-only monthly total remains HOLD pending event-level operational classification, unnamed craft, missing dates and report revisions. A denominator of attempted passages and a compatible cargo-volume series are also absent.

## Proposed rulings and remaining evidence

1. **[I]** Match units, periods, territories, currencies, instrument groups and reporting stages before adoption. Reject arbitrary broad calibration bands around isolated points or silent averaging of source conflicts.
2. **[I]** Keep foreign communication routes and policy stages separate. Reject automatic Cabinet knowledge, speech equals recognition, and sufficient cotton distress forces intervention.
3. **[I]** Keep production, capacity, contracts, delivered arms, rail extent and line operation distinct. Reject prewar annual output as November stocks and later multi-year transport totals as 1862 throughput.
4. **[I]** Preserve the monthly named-vessel series as its explicit retrospective subset. Reject a complete blockade-count/rate label or source-year repairs unsupported by read records.

Remaining: monthly Confederate finance through November; original armory/ordnance deliveries; further named Confederate line operating returns; monthly equipment serviceability and depot deliveries; original French dispatch reconciliation; clean capture classification and attempted passages/cargo quantities; household wages and representative prices. The drafts identify these gaps rather than fill them with model tuning. S1/S2 own inherited runtime behavior/timing, S4 owns military operations, and S7/the gate own sensitive wording and application.
