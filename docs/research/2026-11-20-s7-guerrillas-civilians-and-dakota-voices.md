# S7 research: guerrillas, civilian coercion and a Dakota account

Research only, 20 November 2026, under decision 0106. The [S7 index](2026-11-20-s7-gate-research-index.md) explains scope and retention. This note supplies no placement, event wording, signature or calibrated effect. Its core is August 1861–November 1862. Big Eagle's account was recorded in 1894 and is identified as retrospective throughout. Citations use printed pages, not digital leaf numbers.

## 1. East Tennessee: orders, reported executions and detention

Secretary of War **Judah P. Benjamin's 25 November 1861 instructions to William B. Wood** separated alleged bridge-burners from other armed opponents. Those identified as having burned bridges were to be tried summarily by drumhead court-martial and, if found guilty, hanged near the bridges, with their bodies left hanging. Those taken in arms otherwise were to be treated as prisoners of war and sent to Tuscaloosa. Men who voluntarily surrendered and swore allegiance were offered different treatment. This was a Confederate government instruction, not merely a local commander's proposal. [II1, pp. 848–849.]

The instruction is not the only evidence reached:

| Document | What its author reports | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Leadbetter, 30 November 1861 | Two bridge-burners tried and hanged that day | The report does not establish an aggregate for all East Tennessee |
| Leadbetter, 8 December | Names **Henry Fry and Jacob M. Hensie** as those executed on 30 November; requests commutation for **Hugh A. Self**, described as sixteen | The account of guilt and confession is the commander's; a request for commutation does not establish its grant |
| Benjamin, 10 December | Says presidential approval is unnecessary for carrying out the specified sentence, while approving the action | Do not make a presidential approval step out of a statement that dispenses with it |
| Carroll, 11 December | Reports **A. C. Haun hanged at noon**; describes approximately 150 prisoners and 48 already sent to Tuscaloosa | The stock and transfer figures are not additional populations that can simply be summed |
| Carroll, 13 December | Reports approximately 100 sent or about to be sent to Tuscaloosa | Includes a prospective movement, and may overlap earlier figures |

[II1, pp. 851–856.]

Leadbetter also reported impressing horses and provisions from Unionists, with payment conditional on proof of loyalty, and claimed the executions had a beneficial effect. That is his justification and causal claim. The record does not turn civilian suffering or an execution into a measured reduction of guerrilla pressure. [II1, p. 853.]

Carroll's **11 December** proclamation put Knoxville and a one-mile surrounding area under martial law. On **11 January 1862**, Leadbetter described resistance from civil courts over habeas writs, acknowledged that circumstances were less urgent than immediately after the bridge burnings, and asked for instructions. He questioned whether local martial law alone could suspend the writ without Congress. An officer's assertion of necessity and his question about legal power must not be merged into an uncontested statement of law. [II1, pp. 855, 870.]

## 2. Confederate criticism of arrests: no automatic benefit from severity

H. R. Austin's memorial, received at the War Department **28 December 1861**, argued that restoring innocent people to their families would do more for Confederate loyalty than the severity urged by local partisans. His recommendation is evidence of disagreement inside the Confederate political world, not a measured result of releasing prisoners. [II1, p. 869.]

**John C. Burch's 20 January 1862 letter to Davis** reported information about forty-five or fifty arrests, alleged demands that men volunteer or go to Tuscaloosa, imprisonment without an investigating tribunal, and rearrest after release. He supplied names and said the people concerned had not been taken in arms, according to his information. Davis referred the allegation for investigation. Burch's account must remain attributed: referral is not a completed judicial finding. [II1, pp. 870–871.]

The same collection reproduces **Franck W. Lea's 6 January** and **Samuel A. Smith's 8 January** letters about James S. Bradford. Both argued that Bradford's earlier Unionism had not made him an active armed opponent and sought release or a trial. Smith identified himself as an original secessionist. These are named petitions from people arguing a particular prisoner's case, not proof that every prisoner shared Bradford's circumstances. [II1, pp. 871–872.]

Benjamin's letter **printed as 24 February** acknowledged the probability that innocent men had been confused with guilty men and sought advice about release. The following answer from Confederate representatives disputed the petitioners' claims and opposed return to East Tennessee. That answer is printed as **21 February**, earlier than the request: retain the edition's date discrepancy rather than silently making both the 21st. [II1, pp. 879–880.]

In April, Kirby Smith combined conditional clemency with continuing coercion. His **18 April proclamation** offered specified oath-based protection and a thirty-day return opportunity, suspended the militia draft for crop production, and maintained military control over habeas. An **19 April instruction** ordered arrest of Union leaders accused of circulating exaggerated draft reports. **21 April orders** required Mrs. Andrew Johnson and Mrs. Maynard and their families to leave Confederate lines within thirty-six hours; **23 April correspondence** allowed delay for Mrs. Maynard's health. These are orders and a modification, not independent proof of each family's subsequent movement. [II1, pp. 882–883.]

**Research conclusion:** the sources establish repression, threatened and reported executions, political detention, family expulsion orders, disputes over jurisdiction, and objections to arbitrary arrest. They do **not** establish a numerical relationship between severity and loyalty or guerrilla activity. Contradictory interested claims belong beside each other; neither justifies a stat bonus. [II1, pp. 848–856, 869–872, 879–883.]

## 3. Osceola: raid, uncertain date and conflicting casualty claims

James H. Lane's **24 September 1861 report** describes the approach to Osceola, resistance, shelling and burning the town, and the taking of property and transport. He estimates enemy killed and wounded at fifteen to twenty and reports no loss on his side. The *Official Records* editorial heading gives **22 September**, with a footnote suggesting the event may instead have been on the 20th or 24th. Preserve this uncertainty rather than treating the heading as a settled eyewitness date. [I3, p. 196.]

Benjamin R. Plumly's **3 October letter to Winfield Scott** provides another Union account and justification. He claims that stores of powder and arms were found and gives **fifty killed**, a different category and a different figure from Lane's fifteen to twenty killed and wounded. His description of what was found and Lane's characterization of the inhabitants are interested reports. Neither supplies a civilian death roll or a trustworthy combined casualty band. [I3, pp. 516–517.]

Lane's accompanying political language about slavery and military protection cannot establish the motives or subsequent choices of every person who left with his force. This selection still lacks enslaved people's own account of this raid and local civilian testimony. [I3, p. 516.]

## 4. Palmyra: contemporary reaction is not a complete execution file

The **11 November 1862 proceedings of officers of the First Brigade of Missouri Volunteers**, Confederate army, protested what they described as the shooting of one captain and nine privates at Palmyra in retaliation for the disappearance or death of Andy Allsman. Their preamble says **on or about 18 October** and names Captain Thomas A. Sidner. This is a contemporaneous collective allegation and demand for retaliation, reproduced in Confederate correspondence; it is not McNeil's own execution return or the record of each prisoner's trial. [I13, p. 909.]

Davis's **17 November letter to Holmes** said he had read a newspaper account, sought confirmation and names, and threatened a corresponding selection of the first ten United States officers taken if the account was true. The conditional language matters. It is not evidence that ten officers were actually selected or executed. [II4, p. 946.]

These sources add the political and retaliatory chain. A complete Palmyra file still needs the original demand, selection order, execution report and individual identities checked against one another. No new casualty range or verdict on every captive's legal status is supplied here.

## 5. New Orleans: civilian orders and Mumford

Butler's **General Orders No. 28, 15 May 1862**, threatened a woman who insulted or showed contempt for a United States officer or soldier by word, gesture or movement with treatment as a prostitute soliciting. Its premise that Union troops had shown scrupulous courtesy is Butler's assertion. The order supplies evidence of a gendered threat and coercive occupation policy, not proof of a specific assault or of universal enforcement. [I15, p. 426.]

For the neighboring issue of civilian provisioning, **General Orders No. 25, 9 May**, announced distribution of captured beef and sugar to the poor, including wives and children of men in Confederate service, through Captain John Clark. Its denunciation of Confederate elites and its claim about hunger remain the command's political account. An announced distribution is not a receipt showing how much each family received. [I15, pp. 425–426.]

**Special Orders No. 70, 5 June**, states that a military commission convicted William B. Mumford of treason and an overt act involving removal of the United States flag from the Mint, and orders execution on **7 June**. Butler's **10 June report to Stanton** says the execution took place on the 7th. Together these distinguish the sentence/order from a subsequent report of implementation. They do not supply the full trial proceedings, all commission members or an independently established trial date. Butler's account of the act and the crowd is the prosecuting commander's narrative. [I15, pp. 465, 469.]

The same correspondence records clemency in a **different case**: six men convicted of violating parole. Butler's **3 June correspondence and order** commuted those sentences to hard labor. His 10 June explanation also questioned the legal force of the parole terms. These men were not Mumford; their commutation cannot be transferred to his case or attributed solely to an abstract doctrine of mercy. [I15, pp. 465, 468–469.]

## 6. Big Eagle: a Dakota voice, through an 1894 interview

The *Minnesota Historical Society Collections*, volume VI, reproduces **“Chief Big Eagle's Story of the Sioux Outbreak of 1862,” pp. 382–400**. The editorial note says Robert I. Holcombe interviewed him in **June 1894** and that the account first appeared in the *St. Paul Pioneer Press*, **1 July 1894**. The introduction names **Rev. John Eastman and Nancy Huggan** as interpreters, says Big Eagle could not speak English, and describes the printed account as substantially what was related or understood. [BE, pp. 382–383.]

This is a Dakota participant's voice mediated by interpreters and an editor more than thirty years later, not a verbatim deposition recorded in 1862. The surrounding editorial language contrasts “barbarism” and “civilization”; it should not become the researcher's vocabulary. His stated willingness to have his name used differs from unnamed informants' requests in the introduction. [BE, pp. 382–383.]

### Causes and divisions in his account

- He links discontent to the **1858 land sale and relocation**, pressure to abandon customary ways, and disputes over traders' credit accounts. These are his recollections and explanations; the treaty's legal terms require the treaty text itself. [BE, pp. 384–385.]
- He reports differences among Dakota people over farming, political leadership and relations with the United States. He explicitly disputes treating Little Crow as the single principal chief at the outset. His account does not describe one united Dakota will. [BE, pp. 386–388.]
- He remembers perceptions that United States recruitment for the Civil War had weakened the local military presence, renewed war talk, the delay of the expected payment, hunger among people gathered at the agencies and conflict over favored access to provisions. Statements about what people believed the war in the South was doing are evidence of those remembered perceptions, not verified Confederate military success. [BE, pp. 386–388.]
- His account of the **17 August Acton killings** is explicitly what the four men told him. He describes the subsequent council, dissent and threats against opponents of war. Do not turn his secondhand description of Acton into personal observation or his recalled dialogue into a contemporary transcript. [BE, pp. 388–390.]

### Agency, surrender and limits

Big Eagle describes having a band of thirty or forty fighting men, reluctance followed by participation, attempts to protect particular people, and divisions over surrender. His own denials of taking part in murders are his claims, not a substitute for trial evidence. The point is to retain his account without presuming either that it represents every Dakota person or that its mediation makes it useless. [BE, pp. 390, 397, 399–400.]

He says intermediaries led him to expect treatment as a prisoner of war, and recalls imprisonment and trial after surrender. This establishes **his remembered expectation and grievance**. It does not, without the actual correspondence and trial record, prove precisely what Sibley promised him. His report of a three-year imprisonment extends beyond S7's November 1862 endpoint and is expressly retrospective. [BE, pp. 397, 399–400.]

This account narrows the earlier research's **Dakota-voice gap** for causes and participation. It does not fill the missing Fort Snelling/Davenport death counts, the commission's complete proceedings, individual clemency recommendations or the removal record. It does not independently decide the 38/39 question; the earlier note's separate order/execution distinction remains necessary.

## 7. Wisconsin draft resistance: a documented November response

Governor **Edward Salomon's 17 November 1862 telegram to Stanton** refers to his unanswered messages beginning on the 12th, reports **150 prisoners** arrested for violent resistance to the draft, and says he cannot both guard them and enforce the draft with his available force. This is the governor's reported custody and capacity problem, not an independently checked total of everyone participating in disturbances across Wisconsin. [II4, p. 718.]

On **18 November**, Buckingham replied that the prisoners' disposition had been submitted to the President and remained under consideration. On **21 November**, Salomon renewed his request, said many had been taken with arms, and also described claims for relief from the draft on grounds including alienage, disability and age, some of which he considered just. Do not treat an application for exemption, a drafted man and a prisoner accused of violent resistance as the same category. [II4, pp. 725, 742.]

On **24 November**, Buckingham said the presidential decision was still pending but, in the meantime, Stanton saw no objection to Salomon releasing on parole those he thought suitable and turning those who required restraint over to **General Pope**. The anticipated cabinet consideration the following day was a forecast, not a recorded decision in this letter. [II4, p. 751.]

This establishes a dated governmental response **within 1862**, rather than importing the New York response of July 1863. These selected documents do not themselves establish the precise riot chronology in Port Washington, participants' ethnic composition or motivations, the number actually transferred or paroled, the duration of detention or the eventual legal disposition of each case. No claim that the response had a measured effect on loyalty or draft compliance is supplied.

## Findings outside my scope

1. A rule giving reprisals a guaranteed favorable or unfavorable effect is not established here. The evidence records both coercion and competing explanations of its consequences.
2. The gate reviewer can assess the newly reached Dakota account alongside the already collected official and settler accounts. S7 does not author or sign R3.
3. Palmyra's original selection/execution file and the voices of people enslaved around Osceola remain research tasks; these notes do not imply those sources do not exist.
