#### S04 `evacuation-rumour`

##### (a) The event, dated and sourced

STRONG, contemporary newspaper (NYT, a Republican paper friendly to Seward, reprinting Washington dispatches and other papers' dispatches):

- NYT 12 Mar 1861 (Iss. 2955), p. 1, "Our Washington Dispatches", Washington, Monday, March 11: heading "THE PROPOSED EVACUATION OF SUMPTER". "Quite a sensation was created here this morning, at the Capitol and Departments, by a report that Mr. Chase had resigned the treasury-ship, on account of the determination to withdraw the troops from Fort Sumpter. There is, however, no foundation for the report, except that Mr. Chase is known to be strongly opposed to that policy." (OCR garbles the name "Chase"; sense clear.) Same dispatch: "Depend upon it, the statement is untrue that orders for the withdrawal have already been issued. That was a stock-jobbing canard." Also on 12 Mar: "Reports continue to prevail respecting a contemplated evacuation of Fort Sumpter. They may be true, but it is certain that up to 8 o'clock to-night he has given no order for that purpose" (second dispatch, Monday 11 March).
- NYT 13 Mar 1861 (Iss. 2956), p. 1, Washington, Tuesday, March 12, heading "SUMPTER TO BE EVACUATED": "The impression prevails here that the Cabinet to-day decided on the issue of an order for the evacuation of Fort Sumpter, although, of course, nothing official is known on the subject." "It is understood that the President and Secretary Chase have been strongly opposed to abandoning the post, and they are constantly receiving remonstrances from Northern radicals against the evacuation. Some of these even go so far as to threaten political retribution [OCR: retdbution] and the destruction of the party." "Rumors about the evacuation of Fort Sumpter are at least premature. No such orders have yet been given, but nearly all consider it inevitable."
- Same issue reprints a "Dispatch to the Baltimore American", Washington, Monday, March 11: "The battle of the Cabinet has been fought, and Mr. Seward has triumphed. The Cabinet has ordered the withdrawal of Major Anderson from Sumpter" ... "The decision has been received by the radical Republicans with great disapprobation. Mr. Seward left the Cabinet meeting and proceeded directly to the Senate Chamber, where he was soon surrounded by his party friends anxious to hear the result." This is the contemporary circumstantial pointer to Seward as the source (see below).
- NYT 14 Mar 1861 (Iss. 2957): "Dispatch to the Philadelphia Evening Bulletin", Washington, Wednesday, March 13: "There is no doubt about the determination of the Government to order the evacuation of Fort Sumpter."
- NYT 15 Mar 1861 (Iss. 2958): "Orders looking to the evacuation of Fort Sumpter have no doubt been issued, despite the bitter opposition of Mr. Chase and a large number of Republican Senators. The President has exhibited great nerve and determination despite this outside pressure." (OCR garbles "Chase" as "Cuasz".)

So the rumour wave is dated and sourced in PD newspaper text for 11-15 March. NOT reached: the three papers the spec names (NY World 12 Mar, NY Tribune Washington letters 11-12 Mar, Charleston Mercury 12 Mar). They are known only through Burlingame ch. 22 fn. 26 (not read); archive.org does not hold them. The RR1 "Diary of Events" (pp. 17-20) does not mention the evacuation rumours of 10-13 March at all; its March entries are 12 (London News), 14, 18, 20, 21, 25.

Republican senators' caucus, 13 March (Wade): no PD text found. NYT 15 Mar says only "bitter opposition of ... a large number of Republican Senators". The Wade quotation and caucus rest on Burlingame fn. 34 (Cincinnati Commercial, 14 Mar, by "Sigma"), not read. Chicago Daily Tribune 13 Mar is on archive.org (`per_chicago-daily-tribune_1861-03-13_14_218`) but its OCR is unusable (garbled columns; the words "WADE OF" and "caucus" appear without context).

##### (b) Who took which position, in their own words

Deny via a friendly paper:
- No adviser is on record urging a denial, and no Lincoln or Seward denial text is found. What the record shows is that the friendly paper itself ran partial denials in its own Washington column: NYT 12 Mar ("stock-jobbing canard", "no foundation") and NYT 13 Mar ("at least premature. No such orders have yet been given"). These are the paper's correspondent, unnamed, and not tied to the President or any secretary. Label: STRONG that the denials were printed; NONE that anyone in the administration asked for them.

Ask the cabinet who leaked:
- NONE. No Lincoln, Seward or other statement about leaks or an inquiry into leaks found in any source read (RR1, OR1, N&H ch. 23, NYT 11-16 Mar, Fox, Crawford). "Probably from Seward" is Burlingame's inference (fn. 26, via Adams diary 12 Mar 1861, not read); the only primary pointing at Seward that I read is the Baltimore American dispatch above (circumstantial, not an attribution).

Say nothing:
- No statement by Lincoln on the stories exists in the sources read; his recorded act in the week is the written question to the cabinet. N&H vol. 3 p. 385: "Lincoln now, on March 15, asked the written answer of his constitutional advisers to the following inquiry: 'Assuming it to be possible to now provision Fort Sumter, under all the circumstances is it wise to attempt it?'" That is a response in action, not a statement about the press. Silence on the press is therefore the observed default by absence of evidence, not by a source that says "he said nothing" (NONE for a positive statement).

The cabinet's written replies, 15-16 March (quoted by N&H, which cites the MSS "Chase to Lincoln, Mar. 16" etc.; the full MSS are in the Lincoln Papers, LoC, not reached; SECONDARY-PD quotations):
- Chase, N&H p. 385: "I have not reached my own conclusion without much difficulty. If the proposed enterprise will so influence civil war as to involve an immediate necessity for the enlistment of armies and the expenditure of millions, I cannot, in the existing circumstances of the country and in the present condition of the national finances, advise it." He answered yes, on the argument that "an immediate proclamation of reasons" would avert war (N&H's paraphrase).
- Blair, N&H p. 386: "You should give no thought for the commander and his comrades in this enterprise. They willingly take the hazard for the sake of the country, and the honor, which, successful or not, they will receive from you and the lovers of free government in all lands." (Blair to Lincoln, 15 Mar.)
- Seward, N&H p. 387: "I would not provoke war in any way now. I would resort to force to protect the collection of the revenue, because this is a necessary as well as a legitimate Union object." (Seward to Lincoln, 15 Mar.)
- Bates, N&H p. 388: "True, war already exists by the act of South Carolina--but this Government has thus far magnanimously forborne to retort the outrage. And I am willing to forbear yet longer in the hope of a peaceful solution of our present difficulties." (Bates to Lincoln, 16 Mar.)
- Cameron, Welles and Smith are paraphrased only by N&H (pp. 387-388).
- Bates's own diary of 9 March, quoted N&H p. 380 ("I was astonished to be informed that Fort Sumter, in Charleston harbor, must be evacuated") is a diary entry (contemporary private record, printed through N&H).

Blair's near-resignation:
- NOT sourced in any strong PD source. CRAW (1887, WEAK) p. 364: Francis P. Blair Sr. told Lincoln "it would destroy the formation of the Republican party, and that impeachment would probably follow" (OCR line "Mr. Blair then expressed his belief that such a course would not be endorsed by the people..."); this is the father's visit, from Blair's later statements to Crawford. A resignation letter by Montgomery Blair is asserted only in Burlingame fn. 32 (citing Welles Diary 2:248 in the Beale edition, a 1960 text, and a Blair Sr. letter of 12 Mar 1861 in the Lincoln Papers). Not read. Do not use as a sourced event.
- What NYT does contain is a reported *Chase* resignation rumour, denied on the spot (NYT 12 Mar above). Label STRONG for the rumour, none for any resignation.

#### S05 `lamon-promise`

##### (a) The event, dated and sourced

All STRONG unless marked.

1. Lamon at Charleston, Sunday 24 March to Monday 25 March 1861 (arrived by Sunday; interview with Pickens and visit to Sumter on Monday 25th).
   - OR1 p. 221, Foster's postscript to Totten, Sumter, 26 Mar 1861: "A messenger from the President of the United States arrived yesterday about 2 o'clock, and after delivering his dispatches and having an interview with Major Anderson, departed about 3 o'clock. Mr. Lamon, I understand, was the gentleman's name, and he was escorted to the fort from the city by Colonel Duryea, of the governor's staff." (The page header 222 follows; the postscript is on p. 221.)
   - OR1 p. 218, Anderson to Thomas (No. 84), 26 Mar 1861, received A.G.O. 29 Mar: "Mr. Lamon, from Washington City, visited me yesterday." Anderson says nothing there about a promise.
   - OR1 p. 281, Pickens to Beauregard, 25 Mar 1861 (Executive Dept., S.C.): "I sent Lamon to Major Anderson, and all right. ... I told him we would agree to evacuation upon any honorable and reasonable terms, but would not suffer any war vessel to come up to the fort ... He says Spencer will come out in a few days, certain." (Pickens's own note; "Spencer" is the name as printed.)
   - OR1 p. 281, Beauregard to Cooper, 25 Mar 1861: "I am just informed that Lamon, special messenger from Washington, has arrived, and is now conferring with the governor."
   - OR1 p. 282, Pickens to Beauregard, 26 Mar 1861: "If Lamon was authorized to arrange matters, Anderson ought now to say so, in reply to yours; and if he does not, I shall begin to doubt every thing."
   - OR1 p. 282, Beauregard to Walker, 26 Mar 1861: "Mr. Lamon left here last night, saying that Major Anderson and command would soon be withdrawn from Fort Sumter in a satisfactory manner." This is the clearest official statement of the promise as the South received it.
   - OR1 p. 222, Beauregard to Anderson, Charleston, 26 Mar 1861: "Having been informed that Mr. Lamon, the authorized agent of the President of the United States, advised Governor Pickens, after his interview with you at Fort Sumter, that yourself and command would be transferred to another post in a few days ... no such condition [formal surrender] will be exacted of you." Anderson's reply (inclosure 2, pp. 222-223) does not address Lamon.
   - NYT 26 Mar 1861 (Iss. 2967), Charleston dispatch of Mon. 25 Mar: "Col. Lamon had an interview with Gov. Pickens and Gen. Beauregard, this morning, and left in company with Col. Duryea for Fort Sumpter. The opinion is prevalent that Major Anderson will evacuate Fort Sumpter on Wednesday of this week." Same date in RR1 p. 20 (Diary): "March 25.--Colonel Lamon, a Government messenger, had an interview at Charleston with Governor Pickens and General Beauregard.--Times, March 26."
   - NYT 29 Mar 1861 (Iss. 2970), p. (page not shown by OCR): "Col. Lamon's Visit to Sumpter", reprinted from the Charleston Courier.
   - NYT 2 Apr 1861 (Iss. 2973), Charleston letter dated Wed. 27 Mar, "Charleston completely at a loss concerning Lamon's mission": "[Mr. Lamon] departed Monday night at 11 P.M., stating that he should return in a few days with the order for the evacuation, his visit now being simply to find out how, and under what specific instructions Major Anderson would be willing to evacuate." (correspondent's report of a Pickens aide's account; contemporary press, second-hand.)
   - Anderson's later reports show he *acted* on Lamon's words: OR1 p. 230 (No. 90, 1 Apr, received 4 Apr): "Having been in daily expectation, since the return of Colonel Lamon to Washington, of receiving orders to vacate this post, I have kept these men here as long as I could"; OR1 p. 231 (No. 93, 4 Apr): "The remarks made to me by Colonel Lamon, taken in connection with the tenor of newspaper articles, have induced me, as stated in previous communications, to believe that orders would soon be issued for my abandoning this work."; OR1 p. 294 (No. 96, 8 Apr): "I ought to have been informed that this expedition was to come. Colonel Lamon's remark convinced me that the idea, merely hinted at to me by Captain Fox, would not be carried out."
   - Fox, Fox to Blair, Sunday 31 Mar 1861, FOX vol. 1, p. 11 or 12 (page number garbled in OCR): "I called upon Mrs. Anderson. She showed me a private letter from her husband where he writes, 'I have just rec'd a letter from Gen'l Beauregard wherein he says Col Lamon told Gov. Pickens that in a few days I (Major A) would be transferred to another place.'" Contemporary letter; confirms Beauregard's letter of 26 March reached Anderson and was known in the North by 31 March.
   - Lamon's report to Seward of 25 March ("satisfied of the policy and propriety of immediately evacuating"): the MS is in the Seward Papers (Rochester); I found no PD print. Only via Burlingame fn. 83, not read.
   - N&H vol. 3 p. 390-391 (secondary): Lamon, "coming with an ostensible Government mission to examine some post-office matters, was looked upon as the real Presidential messenger, was treated to a formal audience with the Governor, and permitted to make a visit to Fort Sumter."

2. Confederate reaction.
   - OR1 p. 283, Cooper to Beauregard, Montgomery, 29 Mar 1861: "The Secretary of War directs that you will allow no further communications between the Government of the United States and Fort Sumter, unless the written instructions of the intermediary are first submitted to your inspection, with satisfactory assurances that there are no verbal instructions inconsistent with those which are written."
   - Pickens's telegram to the commissioners (spec: 29 March, shown to Campbell): the text itself was not found in any PD print. The only contemporary-origin statement of it is Campbell's own letter of 13 Apr 1861 to Seward, printed in OR53 pp. 162-163 and in RR1 Doc. 267 p. 427: "The 30th of March arrived, and at that time a telegram came from Governor Pickens inquiring concerning Colonel Lamon, whose visit to Charleston he supposed had a connection with the proposed evacuation of Fort Sumter. I left that with you, and was to have an answer the following Monday (1st of April). On the 1st of April I received from you the statement in writing, 'I am satisfied the Government will not undertake to supply Fort Sumter without giving notice to Governor Pickens.'" NOTE the two discrepancies with the spec: Campbell dates the telegram 30 March, not 29 (Burlingame, via "Facts of History", has 29); and the 1861 letter has Seward's answer on 1 April, not 31 March, and it is the "I am satisfied" note, not a disavowal of Lamon. #### S08 `virginia-unionists`

##### (a) The event, dated and sourced

- Convention vote, Thursday 4 April 1861. STRONG: VAJ p. 31 (Journal of the Committee of the Whole): "Mr. Harvie moved to amend the sixth resolution, by striking out the whole and inserting the following: Resolved, That an ordinance resuming the powers delegated by Virginia to the Federal Government, and provision for submitting the same to the qualified voters of the Commonwealth for their adoption or rejection at the polls in the spring elections in May next, should be adopted by this Convention. ... The question recurred upon the amendment submitted by Mr. Harvie, and being put, was decided in the negative--yeas 45; nays 89." (Entry under "Thursday, April 4, 1861"; the roll is printed on pp. 31-32.) The same issue's NYT, 5 Apr 1861 (Iss. 2976), reports it as "rejected by a vote of 45 to 89" and "Mr. Harvie's substitute was rejected--Yeas, 45; Nays, 89. This was not strictly a test vote, but the Union men were much rejoiced." So the figure is 89-45; the 90-45 version comes from Lincoln as quoted by Botts (JCR p. 114) and Crawford (1887) and has no journal support. The vote was in Committee of the Whole, not a final secession vote.
- The messenger and the summons. All RETRO-OFFICIAL or WEAK, none contemporary:
  - Magruder statement, BALD p. 22, dated 1866: "On the second day of April, 1861, I was requested ... to have an interview with Mr. Seward ... the President wished to send some gentleman to Richmond to communicate confidentially with Mr. Summers"; Lincoln said "Tell Mr. Summers, I want to see him at once, for there is no time to be lost. What is to be done, must be done quickly." ... "This is Tuesday. I will give him three days. Let him come by Friday next," and he added, "If Mr. Summers cannot come himself, let him send some friend of his, some Union man in whom he has confidence who can confer freely with him." The 1875 Atlantic version (ATL pp. 438-439) has the same words but dates it "Tuesday, April 3" (April 3 1861 was a Wednesday; Tuesday was 2 April, so 1866 is right and 1875 slips).
  - Summers to Baldwin, 18 May 1866, BALD p. 24: Seward sent "a note" (not a telegram) and "The next day after the receipt of this note, Mr. Allen B. Magruder ... came to me in the Convention"; "I received no letter from Mr. Lincoln himself, but have no doubt that Mr. Seward's note and message were both at his instance and request." So the 1 April "telegram" with the words "desires your attendance at Washington as soon as convenient" is not in any PD source I read; it is Burlingame (citing Crofts, not PD). Summers says note, and gives no date.
  - N&H vol. 3 p. 423: Lincoln "authorized Seward to invite Summers, or some equally influential and determined Union leader, to come to Washington" (secondary).
- The meeting, 4 April. Baldwin's JCR testimony (RETRO-OFFICIAL, sworn 10 Feb 1866), JCR p. 102-103: "I came here on the night of the 3d of April, 1861; I was here on the 4th day of April, 1861." ... "my interview with Mr. Lincoln was about 11 o'clock that day." Corroboration of the date from a contemporary newspaper: BALD p. 28 prints "From the Alexandria Gazette of Friday, April 5, 1861": "Col. Baldwin's Great Speech last Night. ... made last night by Col. John B. Baldwin ... at Liberty Hall." That puts him in Alexandria the evening of 4 April, as Baldwin and Magruder say (printed 1866; the newspaper extract itself is a contemporary item, original not read). Fox, below, also places Lincoln's decision on the night of 4 April.
- Botts's different dates: Botts says Baldwin came on Friday 5 April and that Lincoln told him on Sunday 7 April (JCR p. 114). Baldwin and the Alexandria Gazette favor 4 April; Botts is off by a day on the meeting (and wrong that Baldwin went to Lincoln after the fleet; see below).

##### (b) The three options: exact sources

`hear-only` ("hear him out and promise nothing"; Baldwin's 1866 account):
- Baldwin, JCR p. 103: Lincoln said "I am afraid you have come too late." ... "Said he, 'Why do you not all adjourn the Virginia convention?' Said I, 'Adjourn it!--how? do you mean sine die?' 'Yes,' said he, 'sine die; why do you not adjourn it; it is a standing menace to me, which embarrasses me very much.'" JCR p. 105: "You received from Mr. Lincoln no letter or memorandum in writing? Answer. Nothing whatever. Question. No pledge? no undertaking? Answer. No pledge; no undertaking; no offer; no promise of any sort." Also JCR p. 104: "He said something about the withdrawal of the troops from Sumter on the ground of military necessity", to which Baldwin objected; and JCR p. 105: he believed Lincoln had been understood "but, I am sure, misunderstood" by those who said an offer was made. RETRO-OFFICIAL (sworn, 4 years 10 months later).
- Corroborators of Baldwin's *report on return* (all 1866 letters printed in BALD; RETRO, unsworn, from men he reported to): Summers (p. 24, 18 May 1866): "I am very certain, that in your statement to us, you did not mention that the President had proposed or suggested to you that if the Convention would adjourn sine die, he would withdraw the United States troops from Forts Sumter and Pickens or either of them"; Janney (p. 25): "I have no recollection of having heard you say that any specific terms had been proposed by him, or that he would order the evacuation of Fort Sumter, if the Virginia Convention would adjourn sine die"; A. H. H. Stuart (pp. 25-26): "I am certain I heard from you no intimation of a proposition on the part of Mr. Lincoln to withdraw the United States troops from Fort Sumter, if the Convention would adjourn"; Price (p. 27): "you did not say to me nor to any one else whom I heard speak of it that Mr. Lincoln proposed evacuating Fort Sumter upon any terms"; Magruder (p. 23), who heard Baldwin the same afternoon: "the President asked him, 'Why don't you adjourn that Convention?' adding, 'I can take care of the Union'"; "Mr. Lincoln said to him more than once, 'You came too late.'" and (Magruder's recollection of Baldwin's own advice) "he would withdraw the troops from Fort Sumter, and issue a proclamation". Atlantic 1875, ATL pp. 440-441, repeats this.
- The earliest report of the interview: no contemporary letter or newspaper in PD sources I could read. Burlingame (via fn. 196, not read) gives a letter of R. Y. Conrad of 6 Apr 1861 published in 1994 (not PD): Lincoln "really does not know his own mind". This is consistent with hear-only but not a PD source.
- N&H vol. 3 p. 426-428 give Baldwin's version and reach a compromise (see below).
- Who advised meeting: Seward (the summoning; Magruder, BALD p. 22, has Seward present and silent in Lincoln's room on 2 April; ATL p. 439 "Mr. Seward ... had remained entirely silent"; the claim "Seward urged the president" is Burlingame, not read). Lincoln himself gave the instruction, so "who advised the meeting" is Lincoln and Seward; no one advises against.

`no-meeting`:
- NONE as advice. The only "no" is Summers's reluctance: BALD p. 24 (the Convention vote pending; "expressed some unwillingness that I should be absent"); ATL p. 440: "it turned out, as I had anticipated, that he could not come, owing to the business of the convention." Burlingame says Summers "suspected a plot" (cited to Crofts, not PD). Magruder's own suspicion: ATL p. 438 "Entertaining, I confess, a suspicious dislike of the Secretary of State." There is no contemporary source in which a person tells Lincoln not to meet. The spec's "Summers suspected a plot and sent a substitute" is sourced only to Burlingame/Crofts; the PD sources say Summers pleaded the Convention's business.

`offer-fort` ("offer to withdraw from Sumter if the convention adjourns sine die"):
- Botts, sworn, JCR p. 114-115, reporting Lincoln's words to him on Sunday 7 April: "'... if you will go back to Richmond and get that Union majority to adjourn and go home without passing the ordinance of secession, so anxious am I for the preservation of the peace of this country, and to save Virginia and the other border States from going out, that I will take the responsibility of evacuating Fort Sumter, and take the chance of negotiating with the cotton States which have already gone out.' ... 'Sir, he would not listen to it for a moment; he hardly treated me with civility. He asked me what I meant by an adjournment; did I mean an adjournment sine die. "Why, of course, Mr. Baldwin," said I, "I mean an adjournment sine die. I do not mean to assume such a responsibility as that of surrendering that fort to the people of Charleston upon your adjournment, and then for you to return in a week or ten days and pass your ordinance of secession after I have given up the fort."'" Botts adds that when he asked leave to put the proposal to the Union men Lincoln said "it is too late; the fleet has sailed" (JCR p. 115), and "Well, not just now, Botts; after awhile you may" (p. 115). RETRO-OFFICIAL, hearsay of Lincoln, no witness.
- Botts on Baldwin's admission, JCR p. 116: "'Mr. Baldwin, is it true that Mr. Lincoln did propose to you that if the convention would adjourn and go home without passing the ordinance of secession, he would evacuate Fort Sumter?' 'Yes,' said Mr. Baldwin, 'he did.'" J. F. Lewis, a hostile Baldwin friend, JCR p. 71 (testimony, retro-official): on 16 April 1861 Botts told him "that he had been to Washington a few days before ... Mr. Lincoln informed him that he had made this proposition to Colonel Baldwin: that if that convention would adjourn without passing an ordinance of secession, he (Mr. Lincoln) would take the responsibility of withdrawing the troops from Fort Sumter. Colonel Baldwin declined to accede to it". Next morning Lewis took Baldwin to Botts, "Colonel Baldwin did not deny it." This shows the claim was in circulation within days (16 April 1861) in Lewis's recollection; still given 1866.
- Pierpont (Botts, JCR p. 116) "said that Mr. Lincoln made the same statement to him, with this addition, that Mr. Baldwin also demanded the surrender of Fortress Monroe", Botts disclaims. Hearsay-of-hearsay.
- The one document of April 1861 origin that I read: Fox's memorandum "[Result of G. V. Fox's plan for reinforcing Fort Sumpter; in his own writing]", FOX vol. 1, p. 38 (written April 1861, since it speaks of events of "the 12th inst." and says "I cannot state the facts at this crisis of our affairs without injury to the Govt"): "The last card was to send for a Union man from the Virginia Convention and say to him that Sumpter would be evacuated at once if the Union people, who were in a majority in the Convention, would adjourn it. This Union man declined the proposition and made so many preposterous demands that the Pres^t decided the expedition should go forward. The order was given to me late the night of April 4th." This is the earliest-in-time evidence for the offer-fort version and is hostile to Seward ("the last card"), written by a man whose information came from Blair/Lincoln. Fox is not a participant in the interview (hearsay). STRONG for date and origin; hearsay as to content.
- Other versions via Burlingame (not read, MSS in LoC or later memoirs): G. P. Smith to Hay, 9 Jan 1863 (LoC Lincoln Papers) and Hay's reply of 10 Jan 1863 ("substantially correct"), Smith's memorandum of 1878; Garrett Davis speech, Congressional Globe, 40th Cong., 2nd sess., p. 1207 (17 Feb 1868); Hay diary 28 Oct 1861 ("most anxious to prevent bloodshed"). The Globe is a government publication and would be a citable RETRO-OFFICIAL source; I could not locate that volume on archive.org. Treat as unconfirmed until read.
- Baldwin's reply is JCR p. 105 (above): "persons have derived that impression from conversation with Mr. Lincoln. Whether Mr. Lincoln intended to convey that impression to them or not, of course I have no means of judging."
- N&H vol. 3 pp. 423-428, SECONDARY-PD, written with the MSS: gives both accounts and concludes "the truth probably lies midway between their extreme contradictory statements"; they see Lincoln as having intended "to have told Summers" an offer but find Baldwin's "dogmatic and dictatorial rejoinder" ended it. N&H says the messenger to Pickens "was not sent until the evening of April 6" (p. 424 n.), which exposes an error in Botts's account (Lincoln on 4-5 April could not say a messenger had already gone).
- Crawford 1887 (CRAW pp. 310-312, WEAK) prints the controversy and says Lincoln "repeated, in the presence of Geo. P. Smith and the members of the Committee, that he most positively assured Mr. Baldwin that if the Virginia Convention would adjourn without passing an Ordinance of Secession, he would abandon Fort Sumter, and ... 'would give a fort for a State' any time." It also misdates the 90-45 vote to 17 March.

### S16 `kentucky-neutrality`

#### (a) The events, dated

**A1. Legislature's neutrality resolutions, 16 May 1861.** STRONG. The House of Representatives (not both houses) adopted on 16 May the report of its Committee on Federal Relations (chair Geo. B. Hodge): "Resolved by the House of Representatives, That this State and the citizens thereof should take no part in the civil war now being waged except as mediators and friends to the belligerent parties; and that Kentucky should, during the contest, occupy the position of strict neu-trality" [ocr hyphen]. Second resolution: "the act of the Governor in refusing to furnish troops or military force upon the call of the executive authority of the United States, under existing circumstances is approved." Votes: first resolution 69 yeas, 26 nays; second 89 yeas, 4 nays (Clay, Tevis, Tye, Houghton). KY House J., 16 May, pp. 91-94 (report p. 91, nays p. 93). The Senate only received the same text as a Fisk resolution on 8 May (KY Senate J. p. 23); I found no Senate vote adopting it. So the spec's "legislature's resolutions" is accurate only for the House; the 4-nay House vote on the second resolution is the one near-unanimous act.

**A2. Magoffin's proclamation, Monday 20 May 1861.** STRONG (as printed at the time; RR1 prints a New York Times text with a summary preamble). "I solemnly forbid any movement upon Kentucky soil, or occupation of any post or place therein for any purpose whatever, until authorized by invitation or permission of the legislative and executive authorities." RR1 Documents, Doc. 181, pp. 264-265 (from N.Y. Times, 21 May). Earlier, 15 Apr, Magoffin's refusal of troops is at RR1 diary (line ~7072; not paged here).

**A3. McClellan-Buckner understanding, 8 June 1861 (the neutrality compact).** STRONG as a printed letter, but it is Buckner's account. Buckner to Magoffin, Louisville 10 June: McClellan "stipulates that the territory of Kentucky shall be respected on the part of the United States, even though the Southern States should occupy it; but in the latter case he will call upon the authorities of Kentucky to remove the Southern forces" (RR2 Doc. 30, pp. 163-164). McClellan to Scott, 26 June, disowns it: "I made no stipulations with Buckner, neither did I directly or by implication recognize the neutrality of Kentucky" (OR 52/1 p. 184). Treat the compact as contested.

**A4. Lincoln's public line, 4 July 1861.** STRONG. Message to Congress: "there are those who favor a policy which they call 'armed neutrality'; that is, an arming of those States to prevent the Union forces passing one way, or the disunion the other, over their soil. This would be disunion completed." NH-CW vol 2, p. 59 (also RR2 Documents pp. 225-226). Lincoln says in the same breath that "very many who have favored it are doubtless loyal citizens."

**A5. Lincoln's private promise, 10 July 1861.** STRONG. Memorandum handed to Buckner: "So far I have not sent an armed force into Kentucky, nor have I any present purpose to do so. I sincerely desire that no necessity for it may be presented; but I mean to say nothing which shall hereafter embarrass me in the performance of what may seem to be my duty." NH-CW vol 2, p. 66 (dated 10 July).

**A6. The "Lincoln guns", Camp Dick Robinson.** Evidence assembled, all STRONG unless marked.
- 7 May: Lincoln signed a commission empowering Col. Robert Anderson "to receive into the Army of the United States as many regiments of volunteer troops from the State of Kentucky and from the western part of the State of Virginia as shall be willing" (OR 52/1 pp. 140-141). Anderson's Department of Kentucky ("so much of the State ... as lies within 100 miles of the Ohio River") dates from General Orders No. 27, 28 May (OR 52/1 p. 147; OR 4 p. 254 n.), not 21 May as the brief says. It became the Department of the Cumberland on 15 Aug (G.O. 57, OR 4 p. 254).
- 9 June: Lt. William Nelson, USN, wrote Seward asking to keep a "Kentucky" brigade from Louisville; Lincoln's indorsement: "The suggestions within made are approved. Let directions be given accordingly. A. LINCOLN." (OR 52/1 p. 161). This is Lincoln's own order to stand off.
- 1 July: Adjutant-General to Nelson: muster five Tennessee and Kentucky regiments, Ordnance to send 10,000 stands of arms to Cincinnati "to be carried thence through Kentucky into East Tennessee" (OR 4 pp. 251-252). Nelson's 16 July report on the depot at Crab Orchard and 13,000 muskets: OR 4 pp. 252-253. Camp Dick Robinson is Nelson's work; Anderson's S.O. 3 (10 Sept) thanks Nelson for "providing and distributing arms to the Union men of Kentucky, and in collecting and organizing troops at Camp Dick Robinson" (OR 4 pp. 257-258).
- 24 Aug, Lincoln's own account: "I believe it is true that there is a military force in camp within Kentucky acting by authority of the United States ... I also believe that some arms have been furnished to this force by the United States ... In all I have done in the premises I have acted upon the urgent solicitation of many Kentuckians" (NH-CW vol 2 pp. 76-77; RR3 Doc. 13, pp. 79-80).
- Ordnance totals: "Since the 4th of May last there have been ordered to Kentucky for distribution 17,000 stand of arms, of which 13,000 were ordered to Cincinnati, 1,000 to Covington, and 3,000 to Louisville" (Ripley, 6 Sept, OR 52/1 p. 188).
- Speed's role: Wickliffe to Cameron, 29 Aug: "The arrangement made to send guns to Louisville, to be distributed by Mr. Joshua Speed upon the order of the members of Congress from this State, has been a failure"; Speed's own note: "No guns have come subject to your order. Jackson has about 1,700. I have no control over them. ... J. K. [sic] SPEED" (OR 52/1 pp. 187-188). So the Speed channel is attested, but as a late complaint of non-delivery, not as Lincoln's order.
- WEAK secondary (cites documents, not independent): NH-Hist vol 4 pp. 235-237 says Lincoln "sent [Nelson] to Kentucky without instructions", quotes Anderson to Lincoln ("your letter of the 14th instant introducing Mr. Joshua F. Speed, and giving me instructions about issuing arms to our friends in Kentucky") and a 28 May committee report that "five thousand muskets and bayonets" brought by Nelson had been distributed. Rousseau's name appears in OR as commander of the Louisville Home Guard (Scott to McClellan, 4 June, OR 52/1 p. 157), not tied to the guns in any primary I read. The underlying letters (Lincoln to Anderson 14 May; committee 28 May) were not located in a primary I could read. Needs OR Series III/1 or the Lincoln Papers.

**B1. Magoffin's protest, Frankfort 19 Aug 1861.** STRONG. RR3 Doc. 13, pp. 79-80 (also OR 4, ch. XII). "I urge the removal from the limits of Kentucky of the military force now organized and in camp within the State." Camp Dick Robinson is not named in the letter; Polk names "Camp Robinson, in the county of Garrard" (OR 4 p. 187).

**B2. Lincoln's reply, Washington 24 Aug 1861** (not the 26th; both NH-CW and RR3 date it 24 Aug). STRONG. "I do not believe it is the popular wish of Kentucky that this force shall be removed beyond her limits; and, with this impression, I must respectfully decline to so remove it." Also: "I do not remember that any one of them, or any other person, except your Excellency and the bearers of your Excellency's letter, has urged me to remove the military force ... One other very worthy citizen of Kentucky did solicit me to have the augmenting of the force suspended for a time." And: "I search, and cannot find, in your not very short letter any declaration or intimation that you entertain any desire for the preservation of the Federal Union." NH-CW vol 2 pp. 76-77; RR3 pp. 80.

**B3. Polk's seizure of Columbus, 3-4 Sept 1861.** STRONG. Polk's report (11 Sept) with enclosures: OR 4 pp. 179-187. Polk admits his own act: "The first and only instance in which the neutrality of Kentucky has been disregarded is that in which the troops under my command took possession of the place I now hold" (to Johnston's committee, 9 Sept, OR 4 p. 186). Harris (Tenn.) to Polk, 4 Sept: "this is unfortunate, as the President and myself are pledged to respect the neutrality of Kentucky" (OR 4 p. 180); Walker, 4 Sept: "Order their prompt withdrawal from Kentucky" (p. 180); Davis, 4 Sept: "The necessity justifies the action" (p. 181); Davis, 13 Sept: "it would conform to my declared policy of respect for the neutrality of Kentucky" (p. 190). Union side occupied Paducah on 6 Sept (Grant's report is in OR 4 ch. XII; I did not page it), which Polk and Buckner both cite. So "the Confederates broke neutrality first" is supported by Polk's own admission about Columbus, but the Confederates' 5 Sept Columbus petition and Buckner's 13 Sept telegram ("Our possession of Columbus is already neutralized by that of Paducah", OR 4 pp. 189-190) show the sides saw it as a reciprocal breach within days.

#### (b) Options and who advised them, in their own words

##### Stage A

**arm-quietly**

Second, 16 July (Cape Henry to Cape Romain, Hatteras, pp. 198-201; recommends the split of command at Cape Romain, p. 198). A "second memoir" on "Bulls Bay, St. Helena Sound, and Port Royal Bay" is referred to in the third report (p. 202: "possession of one or more of the three points") but is NOT printed in ORN 12 as far as I could find. So the "board preferred St. Helena Sound to Port Royal" is unverified here.

**4. Hatteras expedition, 26-29 Aug.** STRONG. Orders 13 Aug: expedition "against some batteries on Hatteras Inlet"; "The detachment will return to Fort Monroe after the expedition" (OR 4 p. 579); 21 Aug: "The expedition originated in the Navy Department, and is under its control" (p. 580). Forts surrendered 29 Aug (Butler, Stringham). Butler, 30 Aug (OR 4 pp. 581-585): "On consultation with Flag-Officer Stringham and Commander Stellwagen I determined to leave the troops and hold the fort" (p. 584). Wool, 2 Sept: told Butler to go to Washington "and ascertain whether it was to be held" and "I have received ... information that the Government intend to hold it" (p. 581). So the order of events is: raid planned, hold decided on the spot, ratified by Washington within days.

**5. The squadron split.** The formal act is Welles's order of **18 Sept 1861** dividing the Atlantic Blockading Squadron "at the junction of North and South Carolina", Goldsborough to the northern division, Du Pont to the southern (ORN 12 p. 208; ORN 6 pp. 233-234: "it is advisable there should be a subdivision of the squadron on the Atlantic coast"). The ORN editors date the two named squadrons (North and South Atlantic Blockading Squadron) from **29 Oct 1861** (ORN 6 preface; ORN 12 heading p. 193). The board had proposed Cape Romain; the Department chose the NC/SC line. Stringham asked to be relieved on **16 Sept** (ORN 6 pp. 216-217); Welles on 18 Sept: "The division of the Atlantic Squadron would probably have involved the retirement of Flag-Officer Stringham; but that gentleman having tendered his resignation in advance ..." (ORN 6 p. 234). The spec's "18 Sept" for Stringham is the date of Welles's order, not of the request.

**6. Port Royal chosen.** Welles to Du Pont, 12 Oct: "Bull's Bay, St. Helena, Port Royal, and Fernandina are each and all accessible and desirable points ... the Government had decided to take possession of at least two of them. Which of the two shall be thus occupied will be committed to your discretion, after obtaining the best information you can" (ORN 12 pp. 214-215). Du Pont to Fox, Wabash, Hampton Roads, 24 Oct: "A long & earnest conference last night with the Gen's was followed by another this morning on the practicability of the big place. ... we are about decided Sherman & I, to try" (Fox vol 1, c. pp. 57-58, from the OCR's bracketed page markers; the book prints a marginal note, hand unattributed: "Port Royal that I Insisted upon"). So Port Royal was decided by Du Pont and Gen. Sherman in the field under Welles's discretion, not by the Administration over the board. Du Pont 16 Dec on Fernandina: "Sherman thinks Fernandina wholly secondary now, and it must fall with Savannah" (Fox vol 1, c. p. 78). Lincoln's 18 Sept letter to Welles: the joint expedition "is in no wise to be abandoned, but must be ready to move by the 1st of, or very early in October" (ORN 12 p. 208).

### TASK 1: S21 `mcdowell-advance`

#### 1.1 Facts and dates

- Plan: McDowell was told verbally by Scott to submit a plan and force estimate. OR I-2 pp. 719-721 prints it as "Arlington, June --, 1861", with the editors' bracket "About June 24, 1861" (STRONG that it is undated). The document actually dated 24 June (OR I-2 pp. 718-719) is a different paper: McDowell's reply to Townsend's 21 June letter on a column to "sweep the enemy from Leesburg towards Alexandria". So "plan submitted 24 June" is an editor's estimate, not a record. N&H4 p. 324 footnote itself cites it as "McDowell to Townsend, June --, 1861, W. R. Vol. II., pp. 720, 721". - Plan content (OR I-2 p. 721): "Believing the chances are greatly in favor of the enemy's accepting battle between this and the Junction, and that the consequences of that battle will be of the greatest importance to the country, as establishing the prestige in this contest..." Plan assumes (p. 720): "If General J. E. Johnston's force is kept engaged by Major-General Patterson, and Major-General Butler occupies the force now in his vicinity, I think they will not be able to bring up more than ten thousand men."
- Council: Sandford (JCCW2 p. 55, testimony 31 Dec 1861): "On the 29th of June a council of war was held at the White House by the President and his cabinet, and all the senior officers on service here, to consider the propriety of an attack on the enemy's lines at Manassas." ##### drill-first (wait and drill)

- McDowell, JCCW2 p. 37-38 (STRONG, 26 Dec 1861): "I had begged of the Secretary of War and the Secretary of the Treasury, who at that time was connected with the Secretary of War in many of the plans and organizations going forward, that I should not be obliged to organize and discipline and march and fight all at the same time. I said that it was too much for any person to do. But they could not help it, or did not help it." And p. 38: "1 wanted very much a little time; all of us wanted it. We did not have a bit of it. The answer was: 'You are green, it is true; but they are green, also; you are all green alike.'"
- ATTRIBUTION OF THE REPLY: McDowell does not name the speaker of "You are green..." In the earlier passage (p. 37) it is "they said that the other side was equally green" (cabinet/Scott conversation about Patterson and Leesburg). I could not find the line assigned to Lincoln in any strong or PD source I read (Fry's Battles and Leaders article and N&H4 do not contain it). The Lincoln attribution is a later tradition. Do not put it in Lincoln's mouth on this evidence; use "the answer" or "Cabinet/Scott".
- Scott's Anaconda plan = drill first in the sense of camps of instruction: Weed letter RR2 p. 384-385 (above) and McDowell p. 37 (Mississippi plan). Scott's own words that he was "opposed to the movement at first" (JCCW2 p. 242). N&H4 p. 323 quotes the "war of large bodies" line from McDowell.
- McDowell's own plan (OR I-2 p. 721) asks that regiments be "organized into as many small fixed brigades as the number of regular colonels will admit... so that the men may have as fair a chance as the nature of things and the comparative inexperience of most will allow." Committee report (JCCW2 pp. 3-4): "There had been but little time devoted to disciplining the troops."
- Committee hindsight (JCCW2 p. 3): "In that respect the movement was made too late rather than too soon." This is a minority-opposite view to drill-first and is the Committee's, not a 1861 adviser's.

##### wait-for-patterson

- Gen. Charles W. Sandford (N.Y. militia), JCCW2 p. 55 (STRONG, 31 Dec 1861), speaking of the 29 June council: "I objected that no movement of that kind should be made until it was ascertained that General Patterson was in such a position as to prevent the junction between General Johnston's army and the troops at Manassas; that that ought to precede any advance against the enemy at Manassas, if it was made at all." Same, p. 62: "I deemed it my duty to say that I did not approve of the movement... if the movement was to be made, I objected to two points... no such movement should be made until it was ascertained that Patterson was between Johnston and Manassas." This is the clearest named on-record wait-for-Patterson adviser.
- McDowell: his plan was conditional on Patterson (OR I-2 p. 720) and he said the same to Scott (JCCW2 p. 36-37, "I could not undertake to meet all their forces together"). He did NOT advise waiting; he took Scott's assurance.
- Scott: gave the assurance (above) and, per Sandford (p. 55), on Seward's report of 6 July proposed sending Sandford to Patterson "for the purpose of pushing forward the army, and doing what I suggested was a necessary step prior to the battle of Manassas." Scott's own recorded wording is the telegram of 13 July (OR I-2 p. 166).
- The President, secondhand only: Blair, House 1 Aug 1861, Globe p. 387: "the President, after he had information that General Johnson had escaped through the hands of General Patterson and had joined General Beauregard on Friday evening [19 July], went to General Scott and suggested the propriety of waiting until Patterson's corps could come up and reinforce the army that was then before Manassas; but so firmly fixed was General Scott's determination to attack the enemy then and there, that the President's suggestion was disregarded." STRONG as a member's statement on the floor; secondhand and from a Blair family interest (Blair is defending the President and the Cabinet), uncorroborated in my reading. Richardson (Globe p. 246, 24 July): Scott said "I have fought this battle, sir, against my judgment; I think the President of the United States ought to remove me to-day for doing it; as God is my judge, after my superiors had determined to fight it, I did all in my power to make the Army efficient" (reported by Richardson from a conversation on 23 July with the President and Secretary of War present). Blair and Washburne say Scott did not blame the President (Globe pp. 386-387). Richardson on 24 July (p. 246): Scott "did not relieve the Cabinet from the imputation of having forced him to fight this battle", and Lincoln lacks "the will to stand up against the wily politicians who surround him"; on 1 Aug (pp. 387-388) he disclaims implying that the President forced Scott and names "that class of politicians headed by Mr. Greeley".

## Part F: Congress lag

### F. Congress lag, 1861: summons to a sitting quorum

Agent: gaps-F-congress-lag. All quotations are OCR read from archive.org full text (djvu.txt), exact apart from OCR doubts marked [OCR]. Labels: STRONG (contemporary primary/official), WEAK (memoir or retrospective), NONE.

#### Answer in one paragraph

Lincoln's proclamation is dated 15 April 1861 and sets Thursday 4 July 1861 at noon: **80 days** (15 Apr to 15 May = 30; to 15 Jun = 61; to 4 Jul = 80; 81 if counted from the 14 April Sunday on which N&H say it was actually signed). Both chambers had a quorum **on the set day itself, 4 July, at the first call**: the Senate had 40 Senators listed present at the opening plus 4 sworn in during the day (44; my count of the printed list); the House Clerk announced **157** answering the roll = quorum, and Grow was elected Speaker the same day (99 of 159 votes after changes). Each house told the other and the President the same day or next (5 July), and the message was delivered **5 July**. So the lag from summons to a sitting quorum is 80 days, and there was no slippage after the appointed day. The minimum *feasible* lag is **not on record**; see section 5 for the best bounds (Confederate comparison 17 days; the Kentucky election date, 20 June, i.e. 66 days after the summons, is the only documented hard floor for a full House, and it is WEAK as a floor).

#### 1. The proclamation

- Text read into the Senate record by the Acting Secretary on 4 July: Congressional Globe, 37th Cong., 1st sess. (archive.org `congressionalglo7380unit`), Senate, p. 1 (and again in the House, p. 2). STRONG. Key words (OCR):
  - "Deeming that the present condition of public affairs presents an extraordinary occasion, I do hereby, in virtue of the power in me vested by the Constitution, convene both Houses of Congress."
  - "Senators and Representatives are therefore summoned to assemble at their respective Chambers, at twelve o'clock, noon, on Thursday, the 4th day of July next, then and there to consider and determine such measures as, in their wisdom, the public safety and interest may seem to demand."
  - "Done at the city of Washington, this fifteenth day of April ... ABRAHAM LINCOLN. By the President: William H. Seward, Secretary of State."
- The same proclamation also called 75,000 militia and gave the combinations 20 days to disperse.
- Not read: Statutes at Large vol. 12 p. 1258 itself. archive.org `us_stat_12` (c37_djvu.txt) holds the session laws only; the proclamation appendix was not in the file I could reach. The Globe reading is the official text as read into the record, so the date and wording are not in doubt.
- Nicolay and Hay, *Abraham Lincoln: A History* vol. 4 (archive.org `abrahamlincolnhi04nico`), p. 77 (WEAK, secretaries' history): "The document bears date April 15 (Monday), but was made and signed on Sunday. This proclamation ... called into service seventy-five thousand militia for three months, and convened Congress in extra session on the coming 4th of July."
- Day count: 80 (from the date it bears). Weekday: 4 July 1861 was a Thursday (the proclamation says so).

#### 3. Why 4 July: what is on record

Lincoln's own words: **none found.** Checked: the 4 July message (Globe Appendix) and N&H vol. 4. The message does not explain the date. Its only reference: the habeas corpus passage, "it cannot be believed the framers of the instrument intended that in every case the danger should run its course until Congress could be called together; the very assembling of which might be prevented, as was intended in this case, by the rebellion" (Globe Appendix; the same passage from Lincoln's manuscript draft is printed in N&H vol. 4, ch. VIII, page not recorded). That shows he knew an interval existed; it does not give a reason for 80 days. Lincoln letters to Seward/Chase on the date and the cabinet discussion: not found in any source I could read (NONE). I did not reach Welles/Bates (memoirs; weak anyway).

What the record does give:
- The militia statute link (WEAK, N&H vol. 4 p. 77): the call was issued under the Act of 1795, whose limit was "until the expiration of thirty days after the commencement of the then next session of Congress"; "It was necessary to convene Congress". STRONG corroboration that the call ran past the assembly: Vallandigham in the House, 10 July (Globe pp. 57-58): the militia were called "for a period extending one month beyond the assembling of Congress". A secondary-only claim (Donald, via a blog snippet) that 4 July was fixed so the 3-month volunteers would expire about 4 August: counts as unread, no weight.
- Special elections that the date accommodated (N&H vol. 4, WEAK as to the reasoning, but they are dated facts):
  - Kentucky: "an election for Members of Congress, which the President's call for a special session on the 4th of July made necessary ... The election was held on the 20th of June, and nine out of the ten Congressmen chosen were outspoken loyalists" (p. 238). Eight Kentucky Representatives answered on 4 July (Globe p. 2). So 20 June to the seat: 14 days.
  - Maryland: "Governor Hicks issued a proclamation for the election of Representatives in Congress to attend the coming special session on the 4th of July" (p. 170). Election day not given in what I read. Five Maryland Representatives answered 4 July.
  - Virginia (loyal government): election on 23 May (Burnett in the House, Globe p. 5); two of the five claimants were elected that day; Whaley and Carlile answered the roll 4 July.
- Distant states: **not found.** Senators Latham and McDougall (California) and Nesmith (Oregon) were present 4 July (Globe p. 1), and Oregon's claimant Thayer was on the roll; the printed House roll has no California Representative. I found no contemporary statement about steamer or overland travel times, and no statement that an earlier date was impossible for the Pacific states. (Bioguide was 403-blocked.)
- Critics who said a faster summons was possible (STRONG as evidence the date was contested at the time, not as evidence about feasibility):
  - Vallandigham, House, 10 July (Globe p. 57, OCR): "why did he not forthwith assemble Congress ... instead of usurping powers which the Constitution has expressly conferred upon us?"; p. 58: "Congress was not assembled at once, as Congress should have been ... What if two or three States should not have been represented ... better this, a thousand times, than that the Constitution should be repeatedly and flagrantly violated".
  - Senator Powell of Kentucky, Senate, 11 July (Globe p. 68, OCR): "Why did not the present President ... convene Congress earlier? He could have convened Congress a month or two earlier than he did." Both are partisan opponents; neither gives a number of days or any evidence on travel.
  - Senator Lane of Indiana, Senate, 16 July (Globe, p. not recorded): reports a letter saying a Senator said "Lincoln's Congress should not be permitted to convene here upon the 4th of July, but should be confronted with Representatives of fifteen southern States" (Breckinridge asked whether he meant him). Unverified hearsay; shows July 4 was known to be a deadline by which the South might be seated.

#### 4. Constitution and notice

- Art. II, sec. 3 (text read on Wikisource, 1787 scan): he "may, on extraordinary Occasions, convene both Houses, or either of them". No minimum notice and no rule on the interval. STRONG.
- Art. I, sec. 5: "a Majority of each shall constitute a Quorum to do Business; but a smaller Number may adjourn from day to day". The quorum is a majority of members, not of seats. STRONG.
- I found no statute or House/Senate rule fixing a minimum notice.

#### 5. Comparisons and the minimum feasible lag

All STRONG unless marked; day counts are mine.
- **Confederate Provisional Congress**: Davis's proclamation "Given ... at Montgomery, this 12th day of April, A.D. 1861", convoking Congress "on the 29th day of April, at twelve o'clock noon" (Richardson, *Messages and Papers of the Confederacy*, vol. 1, Provisional Congress First Session (Continued), Wikisource transcription of the 1906 text; page ~60 per the editor's cross-references). **17 days.** Quorum the same day: "On the call of the States, it was ascertained that a quorum was present" (Journal of the Congress of the Confederate States, vol. 1, p. 159, archive.org `journalofcongres00conf`). No head-count printed. Davis said he convoked them "at an earlier day than that fixed by yourselves" (message, 29 April) and, on 8 May, that the proclamation was prompted by the 8 April message to South Carolina, not Lincoln's of 15 April. Caveat: the members were deputies of seven (then more) states, appointed by conventions or governors, already used to meeting at Montgomery. This is the fastest in-year precedent but not a like-for-like one.
- **Harrison/Tyler 1841**: proclamation 17 March 1841, Congress to meet 31 May: **75 days** (Richardson vol. 4, Harrison, "Proclamation": "convene the two Houses of Congress to meet in the Capitol ... on the last Monday, being the 31st day, of May next"). Ordinary spring session for a revenue purpose, not an emergency.
- **Cleveland 1893**: proclamation 30 June, meeting 7 August: **38 days** (Richardson vol. 9, Cleveland proclamation: "convening of both Houses of the Congress ... on the 7th day of August next ... Washington, on the 30th day of June, A. D. 1893"). A rail-age emergency figure, post-1861.
- **Van Buren 1837**: I could not locate the proclamation in the volumes I downloaded and do not rely on the 15 March / 13 May dates in the brief (the 1837 panic began in May, so 15 March looks wrong). NOT RETRIEVED.
- Notice speed in 1861: Lincoln's call reached the newspapers the next morning (N&H vol. 4 p. 77: "copied for publication in the next morning's newspapers"), so notice was not the constraint. The telegraph reached the Pacific coast only after 24 October 1861; I did not read a source for that and do not rely on it.

Best statement of the minimum feasible lag in 1861:
1. **Not on record.** No document of the period states what the earliest workable date was, and Lincoln gave no reason for 4 July.
2. Documented floor for a *complete* Union House: the Kentucky election of 20 June (N&H, WEAK on reasoning; the date itself is a fact also consistent with Globe p. 2, eight Kentucky members seated 4 July). 15 April to 20 June is 66 days, plus 14 days actually used. A summons that skipped Kentucky and Maryland could have met earlier; Powell and Vallandigham say so (as opponents). WEAK.
3. Documented *fastest actual* lag in that year, for a different legislature: 17 days (Confederate). STRONG for the fact; weak as a model for the Union, whose members ranged from Maine to Oregon.
4. For game use: a summons convening only the members already in or near Washington could in practice sit within about 2-3 weeks (the Confederate figure, and 40 Senators / 157 Representatives is a majority that did arrive by one date), but any lag shorter than about 66 days leaves out the border-state special-election members, and the Pacific members' travel is unsourced. If the game needs a number: 80 days is the historical lag; ~17 days is the only evidenced fast lag; anything between is the designer's inference, label it as such.
