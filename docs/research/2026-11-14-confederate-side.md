Status: first pass complete (2026-11-14), for decisions 0029 (the Confederacy runs on the same machinery) and 0026 (a playable Confederacy needs its own record under the gate). Nothing here contradicts 0009, 0015, 0021, 0026, 0028 or 0029. Several findings sharpen them; see "Design implications".

Conventions: citation keys in [brackets] resolve in the Sources table at the end. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result summary, page not read. **[I]** = our interpretation, never a source's claim. **UNVERIFIED** = asked for and no source found. ## 1. Davis as a principal

### 1.1 How he commanded

- **He treated himself as the source of military authority and worked at it.** He tried to rally stragglers at First Manassas, and a War Department official complained he spent time on "trash" that clerks should have handled [ECW-CinC]. McPherson shows him directing Johnston's withdrawal on the Peninsula in spring 1862 [HN-McP].
- **He refused posts that would stand between him and the armies.** He vetoed a bill creating a general-in-chief in 1862 and pocket-vetoed one creating a general staff, which the author reads as fear of losing power [ECW-CinC]. McPherson says the vetoed bill would have let a commanding general bypass presidential authority [HN-McP]. A DTIC study says Congress finally forced him to hand command of the armies to Lee in February 1865, too late to matter (snippet) [DTIC-Cmd].
- **He advised more than he ordered, and reacted badly to secrecy.** A May 1862 letter says he meant to suggest, not direct [HN-McP]. Johnston withheld his plans and withdrew without telling him, and Davis rebuked him sharply [HN-McP].
- **He backed friends and was slow to remove failures.** Woodworth's thesis is that Davis's personnel and strategy errors in the West helped lose the war, and that no commander in chief was more reluctant to remove failed generals (snippet) [TCU-Wood]. After Bragg lost the Army of Tennessee, Davis made him his military adviser [ECW-CinC].
- **He could not forgive a slight.** McPherson's contrast with Lincoln is that Davis "could never forget a slight" [EV-Davis]. Davis himself admitted that when aroused he lost control and became personal [EV-Davis]. Facial neuralgia and severe headaches are said to have worsened his temper [EV-Davis].
- **He overrode subordinates under political pressure, sometimes against his own staff.** Examples are in section 1.3.
- **Contested weight.** Reviewers say Woodworth is not wholly critical and finds a Davis less inflexible than his contemporaries claimed (snippet) [WEB-Wood]. Gallagher argues the Confederacy lost to superior force, not to a flawed strategy or a collapse of will [TCU-Gall]. A design that makes Davis the single cause of defeat inherits one side of that argument.

### 1.3 Documented insubordination and favouritism, as a pattern

- **Subordinate acts on his own, Davis ratifies after the fact:** Polk at Columbus, Sep 1861 [WTCW-Polk].
- **Subordinate leaves his post:** Beauregard at Tupelo, Jun 1862 [HN-Bragg], [CWM-Beau].
- **Subordinate withholds plans and retreats:** Johnston, 1862 [HN-McP], [EV-JEJ].
- **Subordinates petition over their commander's head:** Loring's officers against Jackson, Jan 1862; Bragg's generals against Bragg, 1863 [HN-SouthFeud].
- **Subordinate disobeys, the commander cannot discipline him because the president protects him:** Polk, 1862-63 [HN-Bragg], [HN-SouthFeud].
- **Davis reverses his own staff:** Randolph resigned as Secretary of War on 15 Nov 1862 (dates of 14, 15 and 17 Nov appear), after Davis overrode his order to Holmes to move troops east and objected that Randolph had not told him. Health (tuberculosis) and Davis's "Virginia-centric" micromanagement are also cited [EV-Rand], (snippet) [WEB-Rand].
- **Cabinet members who stayed despite it:** Judah Benjamin, censured by Congress after Roanoke Island but kept in office and moved between three posts [EV-Davis], [WIKI-Cab].

## 2. Confederate strategy

### 2.1 The strategic debate

- **Davis's stated approach was "offensive-defensive":** hold territory, concentrate in one theatre, and strike when the chance came [EV-Davis]. It reversed most Union gains from early 1862 and prolonged the war, and its Maryland (1862) and Gettysburg (1863) offensives ended in defeat [EV-Davis]. The Rossino article lists Woodworth, Nolan, Connelly and Keller among historians who see Davis and Lee as differing on offence and defence, and says Lee's role is underrated (snippet) [WEB-Strat].
- **Whether there was a strategy at all is itself disputed.** An NPS teaching document says nobody ever defined the offensive-defensive approach systematically (snippet) [NPS-Strat]. Connelly and Jones argue about whether a grand strategy existed (snippet) [WEB-Strat].
- **Gallagher defends the choice of decisive battles** as sensible and rejects defensive or guerrilla alternatives as unrealistic, citing civilian expectations, officer culture, heavier manpower losses under a defensive plan, fears of racial war and the need for foreign recognition [TCU-Gall]. The "fears of racial war" in that list is a gate point, see section 4.
- **Davis gave limited support to guerrilla war** [EV-Davis].

### 2.2 Departmental command and its failures

- The command system was departments with their own commanders. A Manitoba thesis on the "departmental system and Confederate strategy in the West" exists; only its abstract was seen (snippet) [WEB-Dept].
- **Examples of failure in the sources:** Albert Sidney Johnston's thin cordon (Jan to Feb 1862, snippet) [HN-ASJ]; Johnston's Nov 1862 Department of the West, where no one coordinated Bragg and Pemberton [EV-JEJ]; Pemberton following Davis's orders over Johnston's [EV-JEJ]; Kirby Smith's Trans-Mississippi Department as a nearly independent "Kirby-Smithdom," cut off from Richmond (snippet) [WEB-Dept].
- **Davis's change to the system:** a DTIC study says his reforms did not significantly alter it (snippet) [DTIC-Cmd].
- **Davis traded men between theatres reluctantly and politically.** Davis refused to send Longstreet west after Gettysburg and sent him to Georgia with two divisions [HN-SouthFeud]; Randolph's order to Holmes in Nov 1862 shows a theatre-shift order reversed [WEB-Rand].

### 2.3 Breaking Northern will

- **The strategic logic, as scholars put it:** the Confederacy did not have to conquer the North, only keep fighting until Northerners judged the cost too high (McPherson, "American Victory, American Defeat," in *Why the Confederacy Lost*) (snippet) [WEB-McP]. Archer Jones wrote that Confederate leaders and soldiers looked to the 1864 election as a referendum (snippet) [WEB-McP].
- **Lee's own letters show this reasoning in 1862.** On 3 Sep 1862 he told Davis it was "the most propitious time" to enter Maryland, that the two Union armies were weakened and demoralised, and that his army lacked much of the material of war and thousands of men lacked shoes [OR-XIX2-590]. On 8 Sep he proposed that the Confederate government offer the United States recognition of independence, and wrote that a rejection would show the responsibility for continuing the war did not rest on the Confederacy [OR-XIX2-600]. (Two blogs cite the 3 Sep letter under different Official Records volumes; the pages above are the primary text.)
- **Foreign recognition was bound to the same offensives.** Historians describe the Maryland and Kentucky invasions of Aug to Sep 1862 as aiming at the Northern midterms, foreign recognition and the border states (snippet) [WEB-1862]. Russell and Palmerston planned a mediation offer in Sep 1862; Antietam (17 Sep) and Lee's retreat made Palmerston wait; he decided by 22 Oct to remain observers; the Cabinet rejected Napoleon III's armistice proposal in Nov [ADAMS-XI].

## 3. Economy and diplomacy

### 3.1 King Cotton and the embargo

- Southern plantations supplied three quarters of the world's cotton in 1860; cotton was almost 60% of US exports [WIKI-KC].
- **The embargo was never a clean government act.** Planters held cotton back spontaneously in early 1861, and the Confederate Congress authorised burning cotton where Union armies threatened it in 1862; the page gives no date for a formal export ban [WIKI-KC]. Mississippi's history magazine says Davis backed King Cotton diplomacy after Britain declared neutrality in May 1861, and that the South burned about 2.5 million bales; the bale figure is not corroborated and looks high against a crop of about 5 million [MS-Cotton] (treat as unreliable).
- **It failed because Britain already had cotton.** British warehouses held a surplus (about a million bales, from the late 1850s and 1860) that delayed the "cotton famine" until late 1862; Britain turned to India, Egypt and Brazil; price rose from 10 cents a pound in 1860 to $1.89 in 1863-64 [MS-Cotton]. About a quarter of the British population depended on cotton textiles [WIKI-KC].
- **The blockade did the rest:** over 95% of Southern trade shut down by summer 1861 [WIKI-KC]. Historians: Owsley's *King Cotton Diplomacy* (1931) is the standard account; Surdam argues demand for American cotton would have stayed strong; Lebergott says the South clung too long to cotton instead of food [WIKI-KC].
- **Cotton as currency:** the 1863 Erlanger loan was tied to cotton [MS-Cotton]; the Confederacy used cotton to barter for weapons and ships [MS-Cotton]. The Erlanger bond yielded about GBP 3 million and was not repeated [WIKI-Fin].
- **Britain and slavery:** Palmerston cited sympathy for Lancashire, but the cotton shortage did not decide the matter; officials saw the Emancipation Proclamation as an incitement to servile war that hardened public opinion against future intervention [ADAMS-XI].

### 3.2 Blockade running

- **Cotton out, goods in:** early in the war Davis ordered cotton left on wharves to pressure Britain and France, wasting cotton that could have bought arms and medicine [EoA-Block]. The first Confederate vessel to slip out did so in Oct 1861 (snippet) [WEB-Wise].
- **Volume and success:** Wise counts about 1,300 attempts with over 1,000 successful, by just under 300 steamers (snippet) [DTIC-Wise]. Alabama's encyclopedia says up to two-thirds of ships got in or out in the first two years and under 25% in the last two [EoA-Block]. The two numbers describe different scopes (Wise counts steamers; the Alabama figure is a Gulf and Mobile picture) and should not be mixed.
- **Mobile:** 456,421 bales shipped in Sep 1860 to Aug 1861; about 32,000 to 35,000 bales between Feb 1862 and Aug 1864 [EoA-Block].
- **Government control came late:** in late 1863 owners had to set aside a third of cargo space for government cotton and munitions; in March 1864 the government's claim rose to a half (enforced at Wilmington and Charleston, largely ignored on the Gulf) [EoA-Block]. In Jan 1864 the government tried to claim a third of the space on North Carolina's *Don*, which Vance opposed [NC-War].
- **A state-owned runner:** North Carolina's *Advance* made eight successful runs between Bermuda and Wilmington, Jul 1863 to Aug 1864; NC troops were probably the best equipped in the army, with surplus going to other states [NC-War].

### 3.3 Inflation and the Confederate dollar

- **Money supply:** about twentyfold growth against about double in the North; over 60% of revenue from printing, 1862 to 1865 [WIKI-Fin].
- **Price index (Lerner):** 100 in Jan 1861, about 2,800 by Oct 1864, over 9,200 by Apr 1865; the dollar fell from 90 cents in gold to 1.7 cents [WIKI-Fin]. Inflation was about 12% a month by the end of 1861 [ECON-Inf]. The Lerner date is given as 1955 or 1956 in different places on the page.
- **First-year finance (McPherson, via the summary):** about three quarters from printing, nearly a quarter from bonds, under 2% from taxes [ECON-Inf].
- **Treasury note tranches:** $20m (May 1861), $100m (Aug 1861), $50m (Dec 1861), $50m (Apr 1862) [ECON-Inf].
- **Tax in kind (April 1863):** 10% of produce. One source says it raised about $62 million from 1863 to 1865 [EV-Imp]; another says it was about half of total revenue when converted to currency [WIKI-Fin]. These do not agree and need a primary or monograph check.
- **Currency Reform Act of 1864** cut the money supply by about a third, east of the Mississippi until Jan 1865; the gain was short-lived [WIKI-Fin].
- **Cause (Confederate, as the economists put it):** wealth was in land and enslaved people, which were illiquid, so taxing and borrowing raised little cash [ECON-Inf]. Do not let that sentence stand alone in the game: it states that enslaved people were counted as wealth, which is the point the gate says to refuse as a lever.
- Counterfeits made by Samuel Upham in the North worsened the problem [WIKI-Fin].

### 3.4 Conscription, impressment and exemptions

- **First conscription act, 16 Apr 1862** (one infobox says 26 Apr): white men 18 to 35 for three years; the first national draft in US history (snippet). Second act 27 Sep 1862 raised the age to 45; third act 17 Feb 1864 covered 17 to 50 [WIKI-Consc]. Substitution was abolished 28 Dec 1863 [WIKI-Consc]. Randolph helped write the first act, modelled on his Virginia law [EV-Rand].
- **The Twenty-Slave Law, 11 Oct 1862:** one white man per plantation of twenty or more enslaved people was exempt. Confederates saw the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (22 Sep 1862) as an attempt to incite revolt, and supporters said overseers kept the enslaved labouring and white communities safe. It was narrowed on 1 May 1863 and the threshold fell to fifteen on 17 Feb 1864 [EV-20S]. Small farmers called it a "rich man's war"; Escott argues it opened a breach between planters and non-slaveholders; others disagree on how much it mattered [EV-20S], (snippet) [WEB-Consc]. Davis clashed with Congress and the vice president over the conscription bill [EV-Davis].
- **Impressment:** informal from 1861, legal under the Act of 26 Mar 1863. Government prices ran about 50% below market. Georgia declared it unconstitutional [EV-Imp]. The Richmond bread riot of 2 Apr 1863 (about 5,000 people, mostly poor women) was the largest of a series (snippet) [WEB-Bread].
- **Governors:** see 3.5.
- **Desertion and resistance:** about 12,000 North Carolinians deserted, about 10% of the state's serving force; by 1864 the draft was close to unenforceable in places (David Williams) [WIKI-Consc].

## 4. The sensitive history the Confederate seat touches

All of this falls under 0021 and 0026. The test is "Is this a lever?"

### 4.1 Slavery was the cause, in the Confederacy's own words

- Mississippi's declaration: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery" [AVA-MS].
- Stephens, Savannah, 21 Mar 1861: the government's "corner-stone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man" [BF-Cornerstone].
- The Confederate constitution of 11 Mar 1861 protects "the right of property in negro slaves" in territories and states [AVA-CSA] (secession note).
- McCurry: the Confederacy was a slaveholders' project, a "white man's republic," whose slaveholding leaders defended slavery to non-slaveholders as a Christian and racial order [PENN-McC].

### 4.2 Enslaved labour and impressment

- Officials had long recruited enslaved people for fortifications, war industry and camp work; the March 1863 law formalised impressment. In 1864 the government sought 20,000 enslaved workers. Pay was slow or never came, workers were taken in the harvest season, kept past their terms, and many came back in poor health [EV-Imp].
- Enslaved and free Black men were a large and visible part of every Confederate army as coerced labourers and as body servants. Historians cited by Encyclopedia Virginia find no credible evidence that large numbers fought as soldiers [EV-BlackConf].
- A scholarly book on slave labour and the Confederate war effort has chapters on slave impressment (1863 to 1864) (snippet); its author was not confirmed [WEB-Impress]. It is the research lane's first target.
- **Impressed workers escaped, and that is agency.** Historians note many impressed enslaved people used the displacement of impressment to escape from fortifications and so freed themselves (snippet) [WEB-Impress].

### 4.3 Self-emancipation as it drained Confederate capacity

- Timeline of Union policy that enslaved people forced: Butler receives fugitives at Fortress Monroe and calls them contraband, 24 May 1861; First Confiscation Act 6 Aug 1861; Hunter's order 9 May 1862, nullified by Lincoln 19 May; Second Confiscation Act and Militia Act 17 Jul 1862; preliminary Emancipation Proclamation 22 Sep 1862; Emancipation Proclamation 1 Jan 1863 [UMD-Chron]. Butler wrote that the Confederacy was using enslaved workers on its batteries and that the number he held was "of very great moment" (snippet) [WEB-Butler].
- Du Bois called the flight a "general strike." Roediger's account says people came "by the thousands and, ultimately, the hundreds of thousands," that it pushed commanders toward the contraband policy and moved Lincoln toward liberating and arming, and that the loss of labour shook Confederate morale [VERSO-Roed]. One snippet gave about 500,000 withdrawing and about 200,000 reaching Union lines; it came from a newsletter and is UNVERIFIED [WEB-Strike].
- The Freedmen and Southern Society Project's documents show the active role of the enslaved in their own freedom and Confederate commanders ordering enslaved people moved from threatened areas [UMD-FAL]. McCurry argues that even those who never reached Union lines resisted [PENN-McC].
- The Twenty-Slave Law shows the Confederacy answering this as a control problem [EV-20S].
- **Gate reading [I]:** this is the Confederate economy's central vulnerability, and the game may show it as a derived loss with typed reasons. Neither seat may have a verb that manages or prevents it, because that makes enslaved people a resource.

### 4.4 Policy toward captured Black soldiers

- **Seddon, 30 Nov 1862:** captured enslaved men "in armed insurrection" should face death under state law; subordinates were told to execute the St. Catherines Island captives (not confirmed that they were) [CWM-Conc].
- **Davis's proclamation, 23 Dec 1862 (General Orders No. 111, 24 Dec; OR Series II vol. 5, pp. 795-97):** names Butler an outlaw to be executed if captured; commissioned officers serving with Butler's forces "robbers and criminals deserving death"; enslaved men captured in arms to be delivered to the states [UMD-POW].
- **Jan 1863:** Davis asked Congress to treat Union officers who enforced the Emancipation Proclamation as criminals; Congress rejected the broad form in secret sessions in Feb and Mar [CWM-Conc]; (snippet) [WEB-Davis63].
- **Joint resolution of 1 May 1863:** military courts may punish or execute U.S. officers commanding Black troops; Black men taken in arms, including those born free in the North, go to state authorities [CWM-Conc].
- **Aug 1863:** South Carolina's governor held the captured men of the 54th Massachusetts; the Charleston court ruled on 8 Sep 1863 that it lacked jurisdiction [CWM-Conc]. In exchange talks on 25 Aug 1863 Confederates would make exceptions for free Black men but not return recaptured people to slavery [CWM-Conc]. Lincoln threatened retaliation on 30 Jul 1863 [UMD-Chron].
- **Fort Pillow, 12 Apr 1864:** Forrest's troops massacred Black soldiers after the surrender [UMD-Chron]. (One search summary gave October; the University of Maryland chronology says 12 April, and that is the verified date.)
- **Concession:** Seddon told governors on 31 Aug 1864 to treat captured free Black men as prisoners of war; on 8 Feb 1865 Congress repealed the state-authority and officer-execution provisions [CWM-Conc]. Lee's 3 Oct 1864 offer to exchange soldiers "of whatever nation and color" excluded enslaved people [CWM-Conc].
- Confederate officials never formally accepted formerly enslaved Black soldiers as combatants; many were used as labourers, returned to owners or killed [CWM-Conc].
- **Arming enslaved men:** Cleburne's memorandum (Dec 1863, sent Jan 1864) was ordered silenced; the March 1865 law (Davis signed 13 Mar) allowed enlistment only with enslavers' permission and no enslaved man was accepted [EV-BlackConf].

### 4.5 Other gate topics the Confederate seat meets

- **Civil liberties and Unionists:** Davis's habeas suspensions were opposed by state leaders and some protested by mobs [EV-Davis]. North Carolina's courts issued habeas writs for draftees and the Secretary of War refused to accept them [WIKI-Consc]. The Shelton Laurel massacre of Union sympathisers in North Carolina (Jan or Feb 1863; the source says Feb) was investigated at Vance's order [NC-War]. The Confederate repression of East Tennessee Unionists was not researched here (UNVERIFIED).
- **Native nations:** the Confederate treaty with the Creek (10 Jul 1861), with the Choctaw-Chickasaw (12 Jul) and Seminole (1 Aug) is in the secession note [OHS-Pike] (listed in that note, not re-read).
- **Home front:** the 1863 bread riots show women acting politically and the government suppressing news of them (snippet) [WEB-Bread].

### 4.6 Lost Cause traps specific to a Davis seat

1. **"The feuds lost it."** The Davis-versus-generals story is dramatic and invites a blame narrative in which Richmond's dysfunction is the cause of defeat. It is a thesis (Woodworth), disputed (Gallagher, who says superior Union force decided it). The game should let the dysfunction have mechanical effects without telling the player it explains the outcome.
2. **"States' rights."** Governors' obstruction was real. The sources treat it as friction over centralisation inside a slaveholding republic and over the exemption of enslavers. The game's text must never name "states' rights" as the cause of secession or of the Confederacy's difficulties.
3. **"Black Confederates."** No credible evidence of significant numbers fighting; the March 1865 units never fought [EV-BlackConf]. The game must not seat Black men in Confederate ranks as willing soldiers.
4. **"Loyal slaves."** McCurry's "impossible problem of allegiance" [PENN-McC] and the self-emancipation record say the opposite.
5. **Lee-centrism.** Gallagher locates Confederate nationalism in Lee and the Army of Northern Virginia [TCU-Gall]. Davis-and-Lee is the best-documented pair; the game should not make Lee the Confederacy's moral centre.
6. **Memoir sources.** Johnston's 1874 *Narrative* and Davis's 1881 *Rise and Fall* are advocacy [ECB-Seniority] (the page says so; the page itself is a weak source).

## 5. Design implications

Recommendations, in the style of the project: one answer each.

### 5.1 Davis's seat: goals

The rule seat plays to break Union will (0028). Its standing goals, in priority order, drawn from the sources:

1. **Hold Richmond and the Virginia line** (Davis's stated "every inch" stance on the Peninsula) [HN-McP].
2. **Break Union will:** battlefield results, the 1862 midterms, the 1864 election, foreign pressure. The Lee letters of 3 and 8 Sep 1862 are the model: an offensive when Union armies look weakened, paired with a peace overture on recognition [OR-XIX2-590], [OR-XIX2-600].
3. **Keep Confederate will up:** territory, armies, supply, the blockade's bite, inflation, conscription burdens and class resentment (the Twenty-Slave Law, the bread riots).
4. **Win recognition** from Britain and France, which are pressure clocks driven by victories, the blockade and emancipation (FOUNDATION section 7) [ADAMS-XI].

### 5.2 Davis's seat: levers

The same verb table as the player's (FOUNDATION section 5), plus the home-front levers:

| Lever | Historical example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Appoint, relieve, promote | The Aug 1861 ranking; Bragg's retention; Polk's protection; Hood for Johnston | Relief modes cost differently. Davis favours friends and relieves slowly. |
| Transfer a force between departments | Longstreet sent to Georgia with two divisions after Gettysburg; the Holmes order reversed | The stripped commander resents it. |
| Set a goal, review a plan | "Every inch" of the Peninsula; the Maryland invasion | Davis "suggests, not directs" until he does. |
| Audience | Johnston, Beauregard, Lee | Lee is a trusted channel (adviser, Mar 1862). |
| Honour, pressure, address | Congress, governors, the Richmond press | Wigfall's faction, Stephens, Brown, Vance. |
| Cabinet appointments | Five war secretaries; Mallory and Reagan stable | A War secretary who is overridden resigns (Randolph). |
| Treasury | Print or tax (Memminger); tax in kind (Apr 1863) | Inflation is the cost; the rule seat will print. |
| Conscription, exemptions, impressment | Acts of 1862 to 1864; Twenty-Slave Law | Each has a bloc cost. Impressment of enslaved labour is not a verb (section 4.7). |
| State and cotton | Mason and Slidell; cotton embargo | Works through the Secretary of State's seat toward the British and French clocks. |
| Navy | Mallory's posts; blockade runners | Works through people; no ships moved (0028). |

**The missing lever matters.** Davis had no general-in-chief by choice. The Confederate seat should have no relay between Davis and the department commanders, so every report arrives directly and shaded by who sent it (0032), and Davis's attention is the scarce resource (0025). [I]

### 5.3 The generals who push back, and how

Map each documented pattern to the kernel's compliance outcomes (slow-walk, reinterpret, object, refuse, escalate):

| General | How he pushes back | Kernel analogue |
|---|---|---|
| J.E. Johnston | Withholds plans; withdraws instead of fighting; answers rank grievance with insolence; recuperates with Davis's enemies | Slow-walks and shades reports; high grievance, negative regard, caution value; politically allied with the opposition bloc |
| Beauregard | Leaves post unauthorised; disputes credit in reports; glory-seeking | Absence from post; grievance and public standing; press feud |
| Bragg | Lobbies for removal of subordinates; his subordinates petition Davis against him | Pressure from below; the principal's inaction becomes a cost |
| Polk | Acts on own initiative (Columbus); disobeys (Perryville); appeals to Davis over Bragg | Escalates to the principal around the chain; has a patron |
| Jackson (Romney) | Resigns when the War Department overrides him through his subordinates | Resignation threat as a rung of the ladder |
| Lee | Cooperates, proposes strategy, argues for offence | The trusted general: high regard, but his own plans can differ from Davis's |
| Governors (Brown, Vance) | Resist the draft, keep supply, deny impressment, stage habeas fights | Seats with loyalties of their own (0015, FOUNDATION border-state seats); not generals |

[I] The pattern to carry across is that obstruction often comes through channels that bypass the chain: petitions, patrons in Congress, press. The kernel already has a ladder and a patron concept; the Confederate pack should lean on those.

### 5.4 Calibration points (counts, not single cells, per 0028)

- **Cabinet churn:** three War secretaries in 1862 (Benjamin, Randolph, Seddon); five over the war; Navy and Post unchanged [WIKI-Cab].
- **Inflation:** price index 100 in Jan 1861, about 2,800 by Oct 1864, over 9,200 by Apr 1865; about 12% a month at the end of 1861; money stock about twentyfold [WIKI-Fin], [ECON-Inf].
- **Revenue mix:** printing about three quarters in the first year and over 60% from 1862 to 1865; taxes under 2% in the first year [ECON-Inf], [WIKI-Fin].
- **Cotton:** price 10 cents in 1860, $1.89 in 1863-64; Mobile exports from 456,421 bales to about 32,000-35,000 over 30 months [MS-Cotton], [EoA-Block].
- **Blockade running:** up to two-thirds success early and under a quarter late (Gulf picture); Wise's overall counts are separate [EoA-Block], [DTIC-Wise].
- **Conscription:** ages 18-35 (Apr 1862), 45 (Sep 1862), 17-50 (Feb 1864); Twenty-Slave Law from 11 Oct 1862; about 12,000 North Carolina deserters [WIKI-Consc], [EV-20S].
- **Foreign clocks:** the mediation scare of Sep to Oct 1862 should rise with Confederate victories and fall after Antietam; the Cabinet's refusal of the French armistice in Nov 1862 is the check [ADAMS-XI].
- **Command friction checks:** Johnston ranked fourth of five (31 Aug 1861); Davis's retention of Bragg across a year of petitions; Polk's charges dropped twice; Beauregard relieved within days of his unauthorised leave (Jun 1862) [HN-Feud], [HN-Bragg], [HN-SouthFeud], [CWM-Beau].
- **Turning points to test against:** Sep 1861 (Columbus ends Kentucky neutrality), Feb 1862 (Henry and Donelson), 31 May 1862 (Johnston wounded, Lee in command), Sep 1862 (Maryland and Kentucky, Antietam), Nov 1862 (Randolph out, French armistice refused, Johnston to the West).

### 5.5 Recommendations

1. **Build Davis's rule seat from the verb table, with no Confederate-specific kernel code.** The same machinery is the point (0009, 0029). A "no relay" flag on the Confederate pack's chain of command is pack data.
2. **Write the Confederate people data first** (section 7 lists the cast), citing sources in the pack as the validator requires.
3. **Do not open a playable Confederacy until the record in 4.7 exists.** Davis as a rule seat in a Union game needs only the narrated and modelled-with-locked-consequences items and the victory text.
4. **Draft the Confederate situations in this order:** the 9 Apr 1861 cabinet vote (scenario railroad); the Aug 1861 ranking; Columbus, Sep 1861; the departmental failure in the West, Feb 1862; the Peninsula; the Maryland offensive and peace overture, Sep 1862; the Twenty-Slave Law, Oct 1862; Randolph's resignation, Nov 1862.

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

## Design implications

Summary of section 5, for the lead:

1. Davis's rule seat plays to break Union will (0028), through the same verbs as the player. Goals: hold Richmond and the Virginia line, break Union will through offensives and the 1864 election, keep Confederate will up, win recognition.
2. The Confederate people data is the next bottleneck. Davis's pushback pattern (petitions, patrons, press, unauthorised acts, secrecy) maps to the kernel's ladder and patron concepts without new kernel code.
3. Give the Confederate pack no general-in-chief post. Davis refused one.
4. The gate record for a playable Confederacy must decide the verbs, the victory text and the treatment of enslaved people as agents before any build. Davis as a rule seat in the Union game is possible sooner, with items placed per 4.7.

## Findings outside my scope

1. **Search-tool and mirror hazards.** Two results from the `cavac.at` mirror site contained embedded instructions unrelated to the question (the search tool reported requests to output song lyrics and write about cheating); I ignored them and cited none of that site. One search summary also gave an October date for Fort Pillow (the verified date is 12 Apr 1864), and the tool misnamed Davis's house. Treat any source reached through search summaries as needing a read before it enters pack data.
2. **The Union dossier's Confederate cast leans on one weak source.** [ECB-Seniority] (a 2026 blog) is cited for the Johnston seniority episode and says its own key quotation is unverified. It should be replaced with Encyclopedia Virginia and the Official Records. "Needs citation" flags on Davis acting as his own war minister and on A.S. Johnston as Davis's favourite are partly resolved here (section 7), and "Davis's cabinet's individual votes" of 9 Apr 1861 remain unverified.
3. **Gate scope.** Four things the Confederate seat touches that 0021 and 0026 do not name yet: Confederate repression of Unionists (East Tennessee, the Shelton Laurel massacre in North Carolina), habeas corpus suspensions by Davis, the Confederate treaties with Native nations in Indian Territory (Jul to Aug 1861), and the bread riots as home-front politics. 0026 already covers civil liberties and the wars against Native nations; the gate checklist should list these by name.
4. **Phase 1 window and the gate.** The first slice runs 1861 to 1862. Davis's proclamation on captured Black soldiers (23 Dec 1862), the Twenty-Slave Law (11 Oct 1862) and the Impressment Act (26 Mar 1863, just outside) sit at or past its end; the lead should decide whether those are in the slice before authoring.
5. **A Union-side check.** Davis's 23 Dec 1862 proclamation named Butler's conduct in New Orleans as its stated reason. The Union pack's New Orleans situations should be checked against that (the Woman Order and the execution of Mumford are named in a search summary only).

6. **Official Records coverage is thin.** The brief preferred the Official Records, but only Lee's two Sep 1862 letters and Davis's 23 Dec 1862 proclamation were retrieved from primary text. Every command-friction episode in section 1.2 (the Aug to Sep 1861 ranking letters, Polk at Columbus, Beauregard at Tupelo, Randolph and Holmes, Romney) rests on secondary pages, some via loosely dated summaries.
