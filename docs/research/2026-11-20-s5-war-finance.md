## What the two treasuries could spend

Chase's December 1862 report distinguishes the authority of Congress, the President's direction of the armed services, and the Treasury's duty to meet authorized expenditure. He warns that changing army establishments and legislation overtook earlier estimates. An estimate is therefore evidence of what an official expected, not an observed expenditure. [T62 pp. 1-2]

Memminger likewise describes a government with assets and prospective subscriptions that could not all be turned into money. In March 1862 he puts produce-loan subscriptions at about $20 million, but says that closed ports left them chiefly a basis of credit, realized as crops could be sold. The subscription is not cash received and is not a cargo already exported. [M62 p. 3]

Chase reports that New York banks suspended specie payments on **30 December 1861**, followed by most other banks and the government for its circulating notes. In explaining the emergency he describes spending of nearly $1.25 million per non-Sunday day and ordinary revenue about one-tenth as large, requiring roughly $30 million of borrowing a month. These are his account of the crisis leading to the 1862 legislation, not daily constants for every month of the war. [T62 p. 7]

### What the Legal Tender Act actually authorized

The act approved **25 February 1862**, chapter 33, authorized $150 million of non-interest-bearing bearer notes, in denominations of at least $5. **$50 million was to replace earlier demand notes**, and the combined amount was capped at $150 million. Section 1 made the new notes legal tender with exceptions for import duties and public-debt interest, the latter payable in coin. It also allowed holders to exchange at least $50, or multiples thereof, into six-percent bonds redeemable after five and payable after twenty years. [LT62 pp. 345-346, §§1-2]

Section 2 authorized up to **$500 million** of those bonds. Section 4 initially permitted up to $25 million of temporary deposits, at five percent; withdrawal required the stated notice. Section 5 reserved customs coin receipts first for coin interest, then for the sinking-fund provisions, before any remainder went into the Treasury. [LT62 p. 346, §§2, 4-5]

The act of **11 July 1862** authorized another $150 million of notes. At most $35 million could be in denominations below $5, with no fractional-dollar notes. It raised the temporary-deposit ceiling to $100 million and reserved at least $50 million of the new notes for meeting deposits when needed. [LN62 pp. 532-533, §§1-3]

[I] An authorization is a ceiling and a set of conditions, not a payment on passage. The February replacement provision and July reserve make it particularly misleading to sum headline authorizations and call the result currency in public hands. [LT62 pp. 345-346; LN62 pp. 532-533]

## Tax law and collection time

The **5 August 1861** revenue act levied an annual **$20 million direct tax** apportioned among states. Its income-duty section imposed three percent on income above $800, with specified treatment of income from government securities and residents abroad. These are provisions in the enacted text, not evidence of collections. [TX61 pp. 294-295, §8; p. 309, §49]

The **1 July 1862** act created a Commissioner of Internal Revenue in the Treasury, appointed by the President with Senate consent. It required instructions, forms, stamps and collection machinery. [TX62 pp. 432-433, §1]

Its income provisions repealed the earlier sections specified in §89. Section 90 imposed three percent on income over $600 when total income did not exceed $10,000, and **five percent on the amount exceeding $600 when income exceeded $10,000**. This text is not the modern description of a five-percent marginal band applying only above $10,000. Sections 91-92 provided deductions and special treatment, assessment/collection beginning in May 1863 on the previous calendar year's income, and payment by 30 June 1863. [TX62 pp. 473-474, §§89-92]

Not all internal revenue waited until 1863. For example, the act provided three-percent withholding on federal salaries above $600 from August 1862, and taxes on manufactured products had their own commencement provisions. [TX62 p. 462, §75; p. 472, §86]

[I] For the 1862 midterm checkpoint, treat passage, creation of collection offices, early excise receipts and future individual income-duty payments separately. Crediting a full annual income-tax yield in July would conflict with the enacted collection schedule and the small actual internal-revenue receipt in the quarter ending September. [TX62 pp. 473-474; T62 p. 43]

Schwab records the subsequent expansion: the August 1861 non-interest note ceiling of $100m rose to $150m in December; April 1862 legislation provided further notes and bonds; and interest-bearing instruments also circulated. He discusses the difficulty of withdrawing money by making notes and bonds mutually convertible. His analytical judgement should remain attributed to him. [SCH pp. 11-12, 21-23]

### Confederate taxes were a collection and state-credit problem

The August 1861 war tax was **one-half percent** on the specified assessed property, with exemptions. States could anticipate their residents' assessments and receive a ten-percent rebate. Assessment and collection deadlines were subsequently extended; no revenue from this tax came in during the Confederacy's first fiscal year. Schwab reports approximately $10.5m in the five months ending 1 August 1862 and about $5m more during the final five months of 1862. These later figures are rounded and not a November observation. [SCH pp. 285-286]

Schwab's state examples show why a transfer to Richmond did not necessarily withdraw purchasing power from households: Alabama borrowed from banks, Georgia issued bonds, and Florida issued state notes. South Carolina collected from residents after a temporary advance. The federal receipt and the consolidated burden on state credit were different transactions. [SCH pp. 287-289]

[I] Tax-assessment descriptions include the enslavers' legally recognized claims over people. Those are evidence of the slaveholding state's tax structure, never a proposed stock, valuation tool or lever for managing people. This research does not revise the sensitive-history register or its protected-population rules.

The general tax-in-kind regime belongs to the **24 April 1863** act in Schwab's account, not the 1862 midterm economy. [SCH pp. 290-292] [I] Keep that later response visible in an extension note but do not backdate it to make the 1862 budget balance.

## Prices, exchange and household pressure

### Union observations at the endpoint

Chase quotes wheat at **$1.38-1.45 per bushel on 1 November 1861**, and **$1.45-1.50 on 1 November 1862**; prime mess pork at **$15-15.50 per barrel** and **$12.50-13** respectively; corn at **62-63 cents** and **71-73 cents per bushel**. These selected market quotations support neither a complete cost-of-living index nor a claim that all prices rose together. Chase uses them to defend his currency policy, so the argument is an interested official's argument. [T62 pp. 13-14]

He quotes the gold premium at **37 3/8 percent on 15 October 1862**, and **29-30 percent on 29 November**. [T62 pp. 13-14; scan checked] [I] The latter means $1 gold cost about $1.29-1.30 in notes; it is not a 29-30 percent fall in the note's gold value. Its reciprocal is about 76.9-77.5 gold cents per paper dollar. A midterm run must not use the later November observation as information known before the election.

## Adjacent external finance: do not spend the 1863 loan in 1862

Schwab reports foreign purchasing through Fraser, Trenholm & Co. and agents including Huse, with remittances relying on specie, cotton shipments and cotton credit. He credits Huse with $4m of supplies bought and shipped during 1861-62, about half small arms, with more than $1m still on hand. This is an agent-specific, two-year account, not all Confederate imports or an exact November balance. [SCH pp. 28-29]

The Erlanger contract belongs to **January 1863**, with the public loan offered in **March 1863**. Its terms included £3m face value, seven-percent interest, an issue to the public at 90, and a banking purchase/guarantee at 77 with commissions; bonds could be exchanged for cotton on specified terms. Face amount, subscription price, net usable proceeds and cotton actually delivered are distinct. [SCH pp. 30-33]
