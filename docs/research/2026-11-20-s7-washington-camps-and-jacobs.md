## Source, page and date

Harriet Jacobs's **“Life Among the Contrabands,” signed “Linda,”** appeared in *The Liberator*, **5 September 1862**. UNC's *Documenting the American South* identifies it as Jacobs's report and labels its source **p. 3**. The retained newspaper image containing the letter visibly bears **printed page 144**, and the article occupies its second through fourth columns. Citations in this note use **p. 144**, with that discrepancy recorded rather than silently repeating the transcription's locator. The text was read in the UNC transcription and checked against the original newspaper OCR and scan. [J, p. 144.]

The **publication date is not the date of every visit**. Jacobs describes early June within her narrative and visits before publication. Where no visit date is given, this note does not supply one. It does not date the conditions to 5 September merely because that is the issue date. Jacobs writes as an abolitionist and aid worker who had herself escaped enslavement. Her report is a named observer's account, with conversations mediated through her writing, not a comprehensive register of residents or diagnoses. [J, p. 144.]

## Duff Green's Row: overcrowding, illness, hiring and aid

Jacobs describes men, women and children crowded together at **Duff Green's Row**, some on bare floors or filthy rags, with people arriving at all hours. She names measles, diphtheria, scarlet fever and typhoid among the illnesses she encountered. She says that **on some days in early June as many as ten deaths were reported in twenty-four hours**. That qualified statement is not an average, a daily series, or a total death count; no denominator in the letter permits a mortality rate. [J, p. 144.]

Her hiring figures distinguish family circumstances: **$4 monthly for single women; $2.50–$3 for a woman with one child; $10 for men**. These are the reported local hiring terms, not a federal army pay scale, a national wage schedule or proof of actual receipt. The different terms are evidence of the circumstances women with children faced, not a measure of their productivity. [J, p. 144.]

She describes a new superintendent, printed as **Mr. Nichol**, removing partitions for separate hospital rooms; Wadsworth's order for cots; and clothing and bedding obtained through aid networks. She names Hannah Stevenson, Miss Kendall, William H. Channing and the Freedman's Association, as well as women supplying goods from northern cities. Her praise for particular helpers belongs beside the suffering, without treating the arrival of clean beds as an end to illness or death. She says people died after the improvements. [J, p. 144.]

## Alexandria: several locations and unequal circumstances

At the **old foundry**, Jacobs describes inadequate protection from weather, sick men lying on boards, and insufficient clothing. The men she spoke with said they had enough food but had **not been paid for their work** and asked what would happen to them afterward. Some feared wages might be paid to their enslavers. This is testimony about the people she encountered; it does not identify a completed payroll audit or the date payment was eventually made. [J, p. 144.]

At the **old schoolhouse**, she describes women and children whose husbands worked for the government, with food and shelter but little opportunity to earn independently. She reports that people coming from Maryland were sent there to protect them from the Fugitive Slave Law. That is Jacobs's explanation of the administrative movement; the letter does not reproduce the official transfer order. She names Rufus Leighton and superintendent **Mr. Clarke** among those assisting residents. [J, p. 144.]

Other people lived in the former **Birch slave pen**, a house on **Cameron Street**, and other accommodation. Jacobs contrasts the condition of these places, describes people furnishing and cleaning homes themselves, and gives a more favorable account of conditions at **Arlington Heights** and **Fairfax Seminary**. Her letter therefore does not support one undifferentiated description of all Washington-area camps. Her judgments about apparent happiness are observations and interpretations, not a survey of consent. [J, p. 144.]

## Agency and decisions in the residents' accounts

Jacobs records family reunions, escape to protect children, a mother offering to care for an orphan despite already supporting a large family, and eagerness to read. These are people's own actions and relationships as she reports them. They are not an output of a federal labor program. [J, p. 144.]

At Fairfax Seminary, she recounts Clarke urging men to go to **Haiti** and their refusal when he said he would not accompany them. This is one reported exchange and a concrete objection to leaving; it establishes neither a general Black opinion nor a signed refusal on a particular date. It is stronger evidence of residents' responses than an inference drawn solely from a statutory consent clause. [J, p. 144.]

She also reports an unsuccessful effort to bring orphaned children to a Philadelphia institution, including captains' refusal of passage on the ground that it would create hostile feeling. That is Jacobs's account of discrimination obstructing aid. It supplies no count of all children refused transport or of all carriers' conduct. [J, p. 144.]

Her language about civilization, dependence and instruction is part of her reform argument and should not be treated as a neutral description of everyone she visited. Her descriptions of suffering and residents' choices can be retained without adopting those judgments. [J, p. 144.]

## Camp Barker: an October newspaper report

The **24 October 1862 Evening Star**, p. 3, carries an unsigned **“Camp Barker”** report in its first column. The original full-resolution page is retained, with inspection crops. Its badly damaged OCR drops or changes several numbers: the following figures were checked on the scan. This is a local newspaper's report, favorably disposed toward the camp administration, not a register independently audited by this lane. [B, p. 3, col. 1.]

The report places the camp near **Twelfth and Q Streets** and describes it as an asylum and hospital. It reports **675 people present**, including **about 100 in hospital**; much of the remaining population is described as women, children and older or disabled people. It identifies **D. B. Nichols** as superintendent and **Amos Pettijohn** as surgeon. The present population is a stock on the reporting occasion, not a total number ever resident. [B, p. 3, col. 1.]

The correspondent describes reused barracks, additions and families fitting up rooms themselves, including buying stoves. Three tents served as hospital wards; the planned conversion of the chapel to hospital use was **prospective**, not already complete. The writer argues that the high number sick does not by itself prove the site unhealthy because sick workers were also sent from army units, and reports a recent arrival of twenty-five teamsters for treatment. That is the writer's explanation; there is no medical series here that settles the source of disease. [B, p. 3, col. 1.]

The article reports a day school attended by **about 75 children** and an evening school by **about 100 adults**, with preaching and meetings led at different times by teachers, the superintendent and residents themselves. Its disparaging comparison of the learners with people in South Carolina is an interested judgment, not a finding about ability adopted by S7. [B, p. 3, col. 1.]

It distinguishes people in government employment, described as drawing rations and clothing while employed, from sick residents unable to work, described as receiving rations and depending on charity for clothing. It reports women sewing for new arrivals, aid parcels from the North and the National Freedman's Relief Association's distribution work. These are described arrangements and activities; the article gives no complete payroll or household provisioning record. [B, p. 3, col. 1.]

The report says **400 people were under Nichols's charge when he took over**, and **3,500 had been registered since 16 June**. Those are different figures from the 675 present. The cumulative death numeral in the same paragraph is not used here pending comparison with another copy: the scan and damaged OCR should be reviewed together before transcribing it. No mortality rate is derived from registration and current-residence totals. [B, p. 3, col. 1; retained `numbers.png` inspection crop.]

Finally, the reporter says offers to move beyond Maryland were rarely accepted despite recruitment of household servants from Wisconsin, Iowa and Pennsylvania. It is evidence of the correspondent's account of choices and employment demand, not a count of everyone offered work or proof of each person's reasons for declining. [B, p. 3, col. 1.]

This reaches a contemporary Camp Barker source rather than borrowing another camp's history. Jacobs's earlier account and this October report concern **different places, times and observers**. Their different judgments must be preserved; neither supplies a safe composite count for “Washington camps.”

## What these sources leave open

- **Camp Barker is not named in Jacobs's letter.** Its separate October report does not move Jacobs's observations from Duff Green's Row to Camp Barker.
- The letter does not establish camp-wide populations, cumulative deaths, complete wage payments or the subsequent fate of every person she describes.
- The location and timing of each visit need corroboration if a future author requires a day-specific event rather than a dated published report.
- The quoted conversations are Jacobs's representations, not independent transcripts. No unnamed speaker is assigned a fabricated identity.
