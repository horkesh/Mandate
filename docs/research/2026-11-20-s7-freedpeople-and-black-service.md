## 1. Port Royal: people remaining, escaping and making choices

**Pierce's observation, not a census.** Edward L. Pierce's report to Treasury Secretary Chase, dated 3 February 1862 with a continuation through 10 February, describes visits to seventeen plantations. He expressly warns that his brief investigation makes his conclusions uncertain. He estimates roughly 200 accessible plantations and 8,000 people on them; after allowing for camp residents, refugees from outside the occupied district and the North Edisto settlement, he estimates **10,000–12,000 people** within the protection he describes. His estimate of the Edisto settlement changes from 1,000 to a reported 2,300. These are nested, changing estimates, not additive counts. [P1, pp. 3–6, 35.]

Pierce reports that many residents of Ladies' and St. Helena islands remained on their plantations, while movement to Beaufort and Hilton Head was greater from Port Royal and Hilton Head islands. He describes **48 people escaping together from a plantation near Grahamville**, travelling by boat at night and hiding by day before reaching Hilton Head. He also records people evading both Confederate and Union pickets. Their presence did not all begin with a Union invitation or removal: many were already living on the occupied land. [P1, pp. 5–7.]

The report records demands to learn to read and write, people defending themselves with available weapons on Ladies' Island and at North Edisto, and distrust of what Union occupation would mean. Pierce says people feared removal and family separation; he reports enslavers telling them that Union forces would carry them to Cuba. The claims about what enslavers said are Pierce's reports, not a verified statement that every enslaver used that threat. His initial inference that people lacked a strong desire for freedom changed as he encountered further evidence. [P1, pp. 18–20.]

**Source limit.** Pierce was a white federal agent advocating a particular system of supervised free labor. His report repeatedly assesses people through racial, paternalist and productivity assumptions. The conversations are mediated by him, often without names; they are not transcripts of an independently heard Black consensus. His qualification and change of mind belong with his evidence. [P1, pp. 3–4, 18–20, 24–28.]

## 2. Camps and plantations were different settings

### Hilton Head and Beaufort, winter 1861–62

At Hilton Head, Pierce places the camp under the Quartermaster's Department, with civilian superintendent Barnard K. Lee Jr., appointed by Rufus Saxton. He gives roughly **600 residents**, **472 registered**, and **137 on the payroll**, with the last two counting different things. Of the registered residents, 279 came from the mainland or places still held by Confederate forces. Forty-eight later arrivals are reported separately. [P1, p. 30.]

His wage chronology is specific. Lee's instructions of 10 November 1861 promised reasonable wages without fixing the amount. Around 1 December, rates were set at $8 monthly for carpenters and $5 for other laborers. A formal order of 18 January 1862 set ranges of $8–$12 for mechanics and $4–$8 for other laborers, plus a ration, with deductions for family rations and clothing. Pierce distinguishes amounts **paid**, **owed**, and **on the payroll**; payment delays, attributed to a shortage of small currency, made people uneasy and affected their willingness to work. Do not call every payroll entry a payment. [P1, pp. 30–31.]

Pierce praises Lee's management, but the same passage describes punishments: men made to stand on a barrel and women confined in a dark room. At Beaufort he found less reliable enrollment information, approximately 600 people, a recently appointed superintendent, and a school of sixty children with three Black teachers, one named John Milton. Some residents had already returned to plantations within Union-held territory. [P1, pp. 31–32.]

**Food insecurity was partly made by occupation.** Pierce says Union removals of corn for forage had left several plantations with insufficient food to last until the next harvest; some had only weeks remaining. He also reports the removal of draft animals and slaughter of cattle, including unauthorized foraging. His report says further taking of corn had been forbidden, and asks government provision for the shortage it helped create. This is evidence of a local problem and an official's warning, not a measured regional famine death toll. [P1, p. 34.]

### The June report: health, payment and compulsion

Pierce's second report, dated **2 June 1862**, was written as supervision passed from Treasury to the War Department. The retained 1863 printing reproduces the reports with *Rebellion Record* pagination. Its **1863 introductory survey is later evidence**, distinct from the dated 1862 reports. [P2, pp. 302, 315.]

The June report describes **9,050 people** on the supervised plantations. It explicitly excludes the Quartermaster camps at Beaufort, Hilton Head, Bay Point and Otter Island, estimated with families at **2,000 or more**. Its classifications and later payroll counts were made at different times; Pierce explains migration between plantations and camps in the interval. [P2, p. 316; scan, not the damaged OCR total.]

Pierce reports unsuitable bedding and sleeping rooms, prevalent smallpox on several plantations, vaccination, and a hospital on Port Royal Island. Six association-funded physicians were, in his judgment, insufficient for the territory, especially without adequate transport. He gives **no defensible camp-wide mortality rate** in this passage. His speculative explanations of illness through race, diet and character are his opinions, not medical findings adopted here. [P2, p. 316.]

The report describes unpaid earlier cotton work, inadequate clothing, and the late arrival of Treasury funds. An initial payment on account began on **28 April** at $1 per acre of cotton planted by 23 April; another payment followed for later planting. Pierce reports $5,479.65 distributed among **4,030 recipients** for the cotton work and another $10 for a vegetable garden. These are his administrative totals, not proof that everyone was paid what their work was worth. People worked on their own food plots and sought paid camp work while government plantation work remained unpaid. [P2, p. 320.]

Pierce also reports Hunter's **12 May order compelling able-bodied men to Hilton Head**, some still held against their will when he wrote. He protested the order, its execution and the retention of unwilling recruits. He says women and children over twelve consequently bore much of the remaining plantation work. The same page records military enforcement of plantation discipline and workers doing extra work on Friday to retain Saturday for themselves. [P2, p. 320.]

**[I] Research consequence:** these sources require separate questions about freedom, mobility, family security, conditions, promised wages, actual payment and forced service. A report praising a free-labor experiment does not settle all of them. It also does not authorize any change to the gate's existing restrictions.

## 3. Hunter: the government's denial and his competing claim

The House asked on 9 June 1862 whether Hunter had organized and armed a Black regiment, appointed officers and obtained War Department authority. Stanton's **14 June reply**, printed on 17 June, said the department then lacked official information establishing the organization and had requested a report. He expressly denied that Hunter had departmental authority to organize and muster fugitive or captive enslaved people. Arms and clothing had been sent on Hunter's general requisitions, without instructions for this use; the President withheld further orders and correspondence on public-welfare grounds. [H133, pp. 1–2.]

Hunter's **23 June reply**, transmitted to the House on **2 July**, acknowledges an armed regiment while rejecting the House's description of its members. He claims Cameron's general instructions, inherited from T. W. Sherman, authorized employing loyal persons without a color restriction, and argues that this implied authority to equip them. He acknowledges **no specific authority** for clothing, uniforms, arms and equipment for this regiment. His report of success and hope of organizing **48,000–50,000 by the end of the following fall** are an advocate's assessment and forecast, **not a return of men actually enlisted**. [H143, pp. 1–3.]

These documents establish disagreement about authority. They do not establish that Stanton approved Hunter's interpretation, or that all the men entered voluntarily. Pierce's report of compulsion predates Hunter's reply and supplies contrary evidence about the manner of recruitment. [H133, pp. 1–2; H143, pp. 2–3; P2, p. 320.]

## 4. Later 1862 authority and service

### Saxton's two authorizations must not be added together as soldiers

Stanton's **25 August 1862 instructions** distinguish two authorizations, each capped at 5,000: enrolled volunteer **laborers** under the Quartermaster's Department, with stated wage limits and service rules; and armed, uniformed **volunteers** for the protection of occupied plantations and settlements. The latter were to receive pay and rations as allowed by law to volunteers, with officers detailed to instruct and command them. Further clauses concern naval service and freedom of servicemen and family members. These are authorities and ceilings, not evidence of 10,000 enlisted combat soldiers. [O14, pp. 377–378.]

The letter's coercive and resource language is evidence of the War Department's policy and rhetoric. It is not this note's description of people, and no mechanic is proposed from it. Its broad description of family freedom must be read alongside the actual qualifications in the **17 July Militia Act, section 13**, including the status of the person claiming the family members' labor. [O14, p. 378; M62, p. 599.]

Saxton's **12 November report** identifies Company A of the First South Carolina Volunteers in the 3–10 November coastal expedition. He reports **550 enrolled** in the regiment and explains that more than 1,000 men were employed in the Engineer and Quartermaster departments, whose work he had avoided disrupting. Beard's accompanying **10 November report** gives **62 men** in Trowbridge's company at departure. Those three figures describe a regiment, civilian or departmental employment, and an expedition detachment respectively. [O14, pp. 189–191.]

Saxton praises the men's conduct and proposes further operations, but also says obtaining the vessel had been difficult and that transportation, ordnance and medical supplies depended on the department commander. His proposal is not an accomplished expansion. [O14, pp. 190–191.]

### Louisiana: Butler's order and its limit

Butler's **General Orders No. 63, 22 August 1862**, calls for the enlistment of free Black citizens in the Native Guards, subject to presidential approval. His **27 August covering letter** asks for that approval and says uncertainty about presidential support discouraged enlistment. The order reproduces Louisiana militia documents to justify his action. Those recitals alone establish neither combat for the Confederacy nor an individual recruit's motive or prior service. [O15, pp. 555–557.]

On **20 November**, Halleck answers that the matter had been submitted to Stanton, who said no instructions were needed because it was left to the department commander's judgment and discretion. That is the documented reply; it should not be silently replaced by a separate presidential approval this note has not found. [O15, p. 601.]

## 5. Pay: the statutory distinction and a family's recollection

The **6 August 1861 act**, chapter 63, set regular and volunteer privates' pay at **$13 a month**. The **17 July 1862 Militia Act**, section 15, provides $10 monthly and one ration for persons of African descent employed under that act, of which **$3 may be in clothing**. The statute says *may*, not that each payroll necessarily deducted exactly $3. It also does not demonstrate when a particular regiment received cash. [PAY61, p. 326; M62, p. 599.]

Writing in **1902**, Susie King Taylor recalls joining the South Carolina regiment at Camp Saxton in October 1862 as a laundress, relatives in its ranks, long nonpayment, and women supporting families by washing and selling food. Her account extends into the pay dispute of 1863–64. It is a participant's retrospective testimony, not a monthly 1862 payroll. The later dispute must not be dated to the day of the July statute. Her precise recollection of the regiment's earlier organization also needs comparison with contemporary formation records. [T, pp. 15–16.]

Taylor recalls teaching roughly forty children and adults at night on St. Simon's Island in 1862, and the fear of raids and kidnapping there. Her account distinguishes a supposed settlement and removal to Liberia, which she herself qualifies as possibly a rumor. It supplies evidence of fear and what she remembered hearing, **not proof of an adopted settlement**. [T, pp. 11–15.]

## 6. Captured Black men: November evidence, with the outcome unresolved

Mercer's **14 November 1862 letter** reports six armed Black men in federal uniforms encountered on St. Catherines Island: two killed and four captured. He names **Manuel**, described as a boy, as having been placed with Savannah brokers for sale; Mercer says he ordered him into jail pending a decision. His description of their legal status and his demand for exemplary punishment are the captor's claims. Beauregard requested instructions on **17 November**. [OII4, pp. 945–946.]

Seddon's **30 November reply**, after consultation with Davis, denied such captives soldier status and directed summary execution in the described circumstances, with the decision entrusted to the local commanding general. It is evidence of a policy and a threat. These pages do **not** establish that Manuel or the other three captives were executed, nor do they identify their regiment. The December copy to Forney is later than S7's core window. [OII4, p. 954.]

This is already a capture-policy problem in November. It must not be described as originating only in Davis's December proclamation. Neither the four captives' individual fate nor a quantified effect on Black enlistment is established here. [OII4, pp. 945–946, 954.]

## 7. North Carolina: Colyer's account and a named escape narrative

Vincent Colyer's **1864** report describes his work under Burnside in **1862**. It reproduces Burnside's **30 March** appointment of him as superintendent of the poor and says an instruction to obtain 5,000 laborers at $8 monthly, with rations and clothing, was **never filled**. Colyer reports no more than 2,500 able-bodied men under his charge by his departure on **6 July**, within approximately 10,000 people distributed among New Bern, Roanoke Island and other posts. These are his retrospectively published estimates; an authorization for 5,000 is not an actual recruitment total. [COL, pp. 5–6.]

He describes fortification work, bridge and wharf work, guides and scouts. The reproduced **24 April order** directs outposts to respect his passes and send people asking for him without examination. His account of two scouts initially arrested by Union pickets shows that service did not guarantee recognition by every Union officer. It does not establish a standard treatment of every scout. [COL, pp. 9–10, 13.]

Colyer prints **William Kinnegy's account**, which he says he took from Kinnegy before employing him as a scout. Kinnegy describes sale and inspection in Richmond, separation from his family, escape from a moving train, years hiding in the woods, assistance from his wife and other people, and traders' language of human “stock.” The publication conceals some names. This is a named man's testimony mediated by Colyer in an abolitionist publication, not a verbatim independently recorded interview. It gives family attachment and self-directed escape a place alongside the command's account of useful service. [COL, pp. 18–21.]

Colyer reports evening schools, including approximately 800 attendees in two schools, and says he stopped them following the opposition of Military Governor Edward Stanly. He reproduces a newspaper account dated **31 May 1862**, including its claim that Stanly required enforcement of North Carolina law against teaching Black people to read. Colyer then recalls a **5 June** visit with Sumner to Lincoln and Lincoln's rejection of that asserted instruction. These are two layers of reproduced and retrospective testimony. The original newspaper, Stanly's own instructions and an independent contemporary record of the conversation were not read here. Do not present the quoted conversation as a cabinet minute or a settled legal adjudication. [COL, pp. 43–44, 47, 51.]

## 8. Naval entry: a different administrative path

Stringham's **18 July 1861** report describes people who fled to the *Mount Vernon*, their fear of being killed if returned, and his request for instructions and rations. Welles's **22 July reply** says return would be cruel and impolitic and authorizes employment aboard storeships, while saying the government did not invite the escapes. These are earlier context for the August–November window, not an army enlistment order. [N6, pp. 8, 10.]

Goldsborough's **27 September 1861 acknowledgment** records a departmental letter of **25 September**, directing that people described as “contrabands” could enlist at the rating of **“boys,” $10 monthly and one daily ration**. The retained text is Goldsborough's acknowledgment and summary, not the full original Welles letter. The rating is an administrative category in that communication, not proof that the enlistees were children. [N6, pp. 256–257.]

This establishes a naval entry/pay rule for that described class. It does not show every Black sailor's rating, erase the distinction between previously free and newly escaped men, prove timely payment, or demonstrate equal promotion and treatment. Army private pay and the Militia Act's clothing provision cannot be transferred to this naval rule. The commander's **23 August** report of receiving sixteen men and acknowledging clothing instructions likewise supplies a dated movement, not a full naval service return. [N6, p. 110; PAY61, p. 326; M62, p. 599.]

## 9. Limits and next source questions

- Camp Barker is **not** established by the Port Royal evidence. A separately reached October newspaper report and Jacobs's testimony from other Washington-area sites are in the [Washington camps note](2026-11-20-s7-washington-camps-and-jacobs.md). The register, cumulative deaths and disputes still require further records.
- No national total of self-emancipation, a November total of Black soldiers, general enlistment response rate, camp mortality curve or causal coefficient is supplied by these local reports.
- Hunter's disbandment and the full transition to Saxton's regiment need muster and discharge evidence; a report of organization is not a service return.
- Officer policy is established here only where the orders provide for appointment or detailing. The racial composition and service histories of all officers need a separate roster check.
- The naval acknowledgment now establishes the stated entry rating and pay for its described class. Individual ratings, promotion, discipline and actual payment still require orders, muster rolls and pay records.
- Island Mound and the First Kansas Colored are not documented by this selection. A contents heading alone is not a report of formation or service.
