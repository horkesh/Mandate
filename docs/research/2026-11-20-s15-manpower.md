# Manpower calls, exemptions and service terms in 1862

S15 research, 20 November 2026. These are data drafts for later levy design, not loaded world data or accepted canon. [Source register](2026-11-20-s15-sources.md); [catalogue and CSV contract](../../packs/union-1861/source/drafts/calls/1862/README.md). Citations refer to pages read. **[I]** identifies interpretation, arithmetic or a proposed ruling.

## Confederate retention and conscription

The act approved **16 April 1862**, ch.31, empowered the President to call white male residents between eighteen and thirty-five, except those legally exempt, for three years unless the war ended sooner. Men within that description already serving on shorter terms were continued for **three years from original enlistment**. Twelve-month organizations could elect officers within forty days; eligible retained men could receive furloughs up to sixty days, timed and limited by the Secretary, or transport-value commutation. Those not already paid the fifty-dollar bounty were entitled to it. Men already serving under eighteen or over thirty-five were retained for ninety days unless replaced sooner. [CS1 pp.29-32, §§1,7]

Enrollment could use state officers with governors' consent, otherwise Confederate officers. The Secretary assigned enrolled men first to same-state companies already serving, up to their maximum; excess men could remain at home in an unpaid reserve, with subsequent details by lot at intervals no greater than three months. The President could call that reserve into actual service. Persons subject to enrollment could volunteer into existing companies beforehand. The law prescribed infantry companies of 125 rank and file, artillery 150 and cavalry 80; these are establishments, not measured strengths of every actual company. [CS1 pp.30-32, §§3-6,12-13]

**[I]** This combines retention, replacements and a reserve obligation. Adding every retained man as a fresh recruit double-counts him. A blanket discharge on the anniversary of a twelve-month contract misses the legal extension, but a blanket new three-year term from 16 April also misdates its expiry. Original enlistment dates and each unit's muster evidence are still required.

The ninety-day transition was disputed. On **5 August**, Randolph described Attorney-General Watts's construction: those whose terms expired within ninety days after enactment would remain through those ninety days; the others in the outside-age class would leave at original expiry. Randolph objected and maintained the Department's rule of ninety days beyond original terms unless the President directed otherwise. This note reads Randolph's explanation, not Watts's original opinion. [OR42 pp.33-34] **[I]** Preserve competing attributed constructions; do not invent one national release day for that cohort.

The **27 September** amendment authorized calling white male residents aged thirty-five to forty-five for three years unless the war ended sooner. It also maintained authority over those reaching eighteen; once enrolled, men were to serve their full time. A partial call had to begin at the younger end of the additional ages. Men first filled same-state organizations existing on 16 April; surplus could go to later/new organizations. The President could suspend execution in localities where it was impracticable and accept troops under earlier laws there. [CS2 pp.61-62, ch.15]

Authority did not mean immediate enrollment of all forty-five-year-olds. On **20 October**, Randolph recommended not going beyond forty, using estimated population and equipment/supply constraints. Davis endorsed a current call limited to men **under forty**, and Special Orders 245, paragraph XXIX, extended enrollment to men between eighteen and forty not exempt. Randolph's projected population and field-force calculations are his estimates, not returns of men actually under arms. [OR42 pp.132-133]

## Exemptions changed during the year

The **21 April** act exempted specified government employment/offices, medical unfitness, transport and communication work, regular ministers, iron workers, newspaper printers, college/academy leadership, teachers with at least twenty pupils, specified care institutions, one practical apothecary per existing store, and textile personnel whom the Secretary might exempt. [CS1 pp.51-52, ch.74] **[I]** Do not backdate that act to the 16th or assume its classes count the number actually excused.

The **11 October** law expressly repealed April's exemption act and substituted a more conditional list. It specified railroad occupations and **two expert track workers per eight-mile section**, rather than all railway labor; telegraph local superintendents/operators were limited to four per locality except the seat of government. Existing religious objectors in four named denominations had to provide substitutes or pay **$500**. Physicians needed five years of actual practice; education personnel needed two years of regular engagement. Occupational/manufacturing exemptions carried production-price/profit conditions, in the specified cases capped at seventy-five percent above cost or the Secretary's maximum; evasion ended the employment-based exemption. Government and contractor munitions establishments required certification or approval of needed employees. [CS2 pp.77-79, ch.45]

The same law exempted specified stock raisers and salt/mining personnel and allowed presidential exceptions for justice, equity or necessity. Its exemptions continued only while the specified occupation continued. [CS2 p.79] **[I]** These clauses expose competition between recruiting, skilled production and transport, but measure no percentage loss of output or gain in recruits.

The plantation clause protected white men engaged in maintaining slavery. It was not simply an exemption for anyone who enslaved twenty people. It distinguished plantations where state law required a white person, plantations of twenty enslaved people in states without that law, and an additional policing provision for combined smaller plantations within five miles. The absence of an adult white man already not liable to duty was a condition; in the combined clause, the oldest owner or overseer was designated. [CS2 p.79] **[I]** The draft records discriminatory eligibility and coercive purpose. It creates no stock, valuation or management of enslaved people. Wording and any eventual situation/effect remain for the separate sensitive-history gate.

Actual implementation could be contested. Vance's **10 October** letter said his promise of a choice of regiments increased arrivals and threatened to withdraw his militia officers when Confederate orders contradicted it; Randolph replied on the 11th that volunteering before enrollment remained legal, while enrolled men were assigned by the Department. These are attributed reports and instructions, not a measured national recruitment multiplier. [OR42 pp.114-116] Randolph reported **2,718** conscripts enrolled and brought into service in Georgia on **26 September**, while replacing the camp's commander and acknowledging missing administrative information. [OR42 pp.95-96] **[I]** That observation is not every Georgian volunteer who enlisted in response to the law's threat.

## The Union's two 300,000 calls

Lincoln's **1 July** reply to the governors announced an additional **300,000** men, chiefly infantry, with quotas promised the next day. Later regulations refer to the **2 July** call. Stanton's 1 July bounty instruction refers to recruits for three years or during the war, with $25 of the existing bounty advanced on regimental muster. [OR32 pp.187-188, 333,335] **[I]** The catalogue uses July 2 as the call convention while preserving July 1 as the document date. It supplies no imaginary immediate full army.

The **17 July Militia Act**, ch.201, allowed a President's specified militia term up to nine months, enrollment of able-bodied male citizens eighteen to forty-five, apportionment by representative population, and federal rules where state law was defective. Section 3 separately authorized up to **100,000** nine-month infantry volunteers, and §4 twelve-month volunteers for existing infantry regiments. The former received first-month pay and $25 bounty at muster; the latter a $50 bounty split between joining and expiry. These are different authorities, not three actual completed levies to add together. [USM62 pp.597-598]

Sections 12-13 authorized service by people of African descent and specified freedom for the man serving and certain family members under the act's conditions. Section 15 prescribed $10 monthly pay, with $3 potentially in clothing, for people employed under its racial pay clause. These discriminatory provisions and the conditional freedom language are recorded as enacted law, not as proof that all authorized service was immediately organized or paid. [USM62 p.599] The draft adds no Black enlistment yield, population-management action or gate wording; S7 and the gate own those applications.

**4 August**, General Orders 94, ordered **300,000 militia for nine months unless sooner discharged**. A further special draft was to supply any July volunteer deficiency by **15 August**. The circular dated 5 August instead prints **18 August**; General Orders 99 of 9 August repeats the 15th, with “unless otherwise ordered.” [OR32 pp.291-292, 295-296,333-335] **[I]** Preserve document variants and subsequent instructions; this is a draft threat and administrative process, not a single compulsory arrival day.

General Orders 99 used state enrollment, county commissioners, medical certification, public drawing, service of notices, rendezvous and federal muster. It removed those already serving and specified public-service/transport classes plus state-law exemptions. A drafted man could offer an eligible consenting substitute. [OR32 pp.333-335] Correspondence allowed excess three-year volunteers to offset the militia draft; governors apportioned county quotas, the War Department state quotas. [OR32 pp.331-332] On **26 August**, instructions to several governors used volunteers mustered from **2 July to 1 September** above the July quota as credits against militia, allowing a draft after **3 September** when necessary. Delaware received changed figures; New York's telegram printed 59,755 rather than the earlier 59,705. [OR32 pp.462-464,466-468,470]

The quota CSV preserves all twenty-three original state/district rows for both calls. Their sum is **334,835 [I]**, not the headline 300,000. The editors' furnished columns total **421,465 [I]** and **87,588 [I]**, including Nebraska's separately stated credits, and are kept in a separate retrospective CSV. The footnotes do not establish when each credited man arrived. [OR32 pp.188,291] **[I]** Do not silently rescale the quotas or load retrospective credits as knowledge available on the call date.

## Expiring short terms and renewed contracts

The earlier April militia requisition specified **three months unless sooner discharged**, followed by rendezvous and federal muster. On **11 July 1861**, Cameron warned Curtin that Pennsylvania's three-month volunteers would expire that month and asked when fifteen replacement regiments could march. The same page records New York's conflicting two-year/three-month muster paperwork. [OR31 pp.68-69,325] **[I]** There is no basis for keeping every April 1861 militiaman in the November 1862 army, nor for discharging all on one day obtained by adding ninety to April 15.

Short-service questions recurred in 1862. On **27 May**, Maine was instructed to discharge three-month recruits and fill its three-year regiment; Massachusetts asked for three-month acceptance, and Stanton directed release of its militia and long-term enlistment. Named other governors were told to accept no more short-term men without special orders. [OR32 pp.85-86] On **9 August**, Indiana still reported three-month, thirty-day and seventy-day units in service, while the current general instruction refused new three-month volunteers. [OR32 pp.333,338] **[I]** Contract expiry, policy on new enlistments, early discharge and special exceptions need separate records.

## Proposed rulings and open evidence

1. **[I]** Preserve legal authority, executive call, quota, enrollment, muster, retention, expiry and field strength as different measurements. Reject adding every authority to army strength or counting retained men twice.
2. **[I]** Keep the original quota tables and a dated correction/credit ledger. Reject silently forcing their sum to 300,000 or replacing earlier figures with a later telegram without a validity date. The unexplained aggregate discrepancy and New York/Delaware revisions need lead review before adoption.
3. **[I]** Retain calendar months/years and original enlistment dates. Reject assumed 30-day months, one universal expiration date and the notion that passing an exemption act automatically discharged everyone already mustered.
4. **[I]** Treat the exemption CSV as a research classification requiring occupational facts and gate review. Reject a blanket enslaver exemption, universal skilled-worker percentages, or managed stocks of protected people.

Missing: original July 2 circular quota sheet as a contemporaneous whole, a reconciled final state credit ledger and rules for converting excess three-year service into nine-month credit, regiment-specific expiration/muster-out and reenlistment dates, complete Confederate enrollment/exemption returns, and judicial/local application of exemption categories. No tolerance band or uptake coefficient is derived from absent evidence.
