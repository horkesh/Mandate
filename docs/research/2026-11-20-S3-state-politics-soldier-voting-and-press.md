## 1. State government has its own dates and institutions

The almanac's tables of loyal governments change between its 1862 and 1863 editions. The earlier table names Morgan in New York, Olden in New Jersey, Burton in Delaware, Washburn in Maine and Whiteaker in Oregon; the later table names Seymour, Parker, Cannon, Coburn and Gibbs respectively. Both tables give terms and ordinary election and legislative calendars. They are publication-time reference tables, not daily officeholding histories. [TA62, p. 64; TA63, p. 64.]

[I] An election winner needs a separate record from the incumbent. Do not replace every governor on 4 November or infer an inauguration day from a printed term-expiration month. This matters especially when a newly elected opposition governor has not yet taken office. The tables support identifying the transition; exact inauguration and succession dates remain a separate source check for production data.

### Governors organize a policy intervention

McPherson prints an address to Lincoln from governors endorsing the emancipation notice, pledging support for prosecution of the war, and recommending a reserve of at least **100,000 one-year volunteers**, after the existing quotas have been filled. Its signatories include Curtin, Andrew, Yates, Washburn, Salomon (printed “Solomon”), Kirkwood, Sprague, Pierpont, Tod, Berry and Blair, with Indiana represented by D. G. Rose on Morton's behalf. [MP, pp. 232–233.]

The dating is unresolved in these witnesses: McPherson heads the Altoona address **22 September 1862**, whereas the almanac's September chronology places the governors' meeting at Altoona on **24 September**. The preliminary proclamation itself is dated the 22nd. [MP, pp. 227–228, 232–233; TA63, p. 48.] [I] Retain this conflict and use the address as an attributed September intervention. Do not silently turn a printed heading into a verified convention, presentation or delivery date. An original dated address or governor's correspondence is required for an exact-day event.

[I] The requested reserve is a policy demand, not a new force already mustered. An address signed by these governors does not prove unanimity among all loyal governors, compliance by every state or an extra 100,000 soldiers actually available that day. S6 owns the cast's dated values; S4 owns resulting forces.

## 2. Voting from military service is a state-specific institution

### Minnesota: the original 1862 act

The extra-session law's chapter I is approved **27 September 1862**, effective on passage. Its title addresses the November 1862 and subsequent general elections during the war. [MN62, pp. 13, 18.] The provisions actually read are:

| Provision | What the law says | Printed pages |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | Covers the specified military/naval and state-service voters, with residence, age, citizenship or declared intention, and registry qualifications. It includes residents who attain twenty-one after enlistment. | 13–14, 16 |
| Voter's act | The voter seals the folded ballot, addresses the envelope to the home district's election judges, identifies himself and his service, and acknowledges it as his voluntary vote before a commissioner. | 14 |
| Oath and certificate | The act supplies prescribed forms. The commissioner certifies identity, service and voluntary acknowledgment. | 14–15 |
| Return and counting | Commissioners mail with postage prepaid or deliver the envelope. Judges retain it unopened until polls open, check the registry and canvass the ballot with ordinary votes. Envelopes and certificates are preserved. | 15–16 |
| Registration safeguard | Section 5 specifies challenges and proof, including an election-day insertion provision for a ballot accompanied by the section 3 certificate. It is more detailed than a blanket statement that any absent soldier may vote. | 16 |
| Administration | Governor with Senate advice and consent appoints six commissioners, allocated to three jurisdictions and selected equally from the two recognized parties. Commissioners swear not to influence a soldier's vote. They take candidate ballots and supplies, with ballot availability qualifications, and the voter's choice is to remain private. | 16–17 |
| Penalties and expenditure | Section 8 penalizes the specified unlawful ballots, interference and official failures. The law appropriates **$1,200**, or the necessary portion, for administration, with per diem and mileage specified. | 17–18 |

[I] This is evidence of legal machinery and intended safeguards, not evidence that every eligible soldier was reached, every envelope arrived or every safeguard was observed. A model must distinguish access, participation, delivery, admission and counting. A military block's strength is not its number of qualified Minnesota voters. No universal race or citizenship franchise is inferred from this one act; the broader state constitution and election law still need examination.

Walter N. Trenerry's later account describes the 1862 machinery and finds no official separate count establishing Minnesota soldiers' contribution to the result. His p. 170 contrasts partisan newspaper reports of their preferences. His later speculation about manipulation in 1864 is explicitly speculation and is not accepted here as an 1862 fact. [TRE, pp. 168–170, 171–172.] [I] Use the statute for procedure and preserve uncertainty about effects; do not fit a partisan soldier-vote probability from competing newspaper claims.

### The almanac's soldier returns are a compilation, not a national election statistic

TA63 p. 63 labels separate returns for Pennsylvania **1861**, Pennsylvania **1862**, Wisconsin **November 1862**, Iowa **1862**, Colorado Territory **1862**, and Missouri **1862**. Pennsylvania's 1862 table explicitly says it includes only a few regiments then remaining **in the state**. Missouri's Democratic total is approximate. The recapitulation mixes these entries, including Pennsylvania's different years. [TA63, p. 63.]

| Entry | Published figures | Usable interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Pennsylvania 1861 | Union **11,351**, Democratic **3,173** | Earlier-year reference only. |
| Pennsylvania 1862 | Union **1,867**, Democratic **251** | The limited in-state regiments described by the source, not every Pennsylvanian in the army. |
| Iowa 1862 | Republican **14,874**, Democratic **4,115** | Published state-ticket soldier subtotal; not a turnout denominator. |
| Wisconsin 1862 | Republican **6,229**, Democratic **2,000** | Published compilation, subject to unit/return coverage. |
| Missouri 1862 | Republican and Emancipation **1,732**, Democratic **about 75** | Preserve both the combined category and approximation. |

All five rows: TA63 p. 63. [I] Do not add them into a “national 1862 soldier vote.” An observed subtotal does not establish a state's law or all soldiers' political views. Pennsylvania's constitutional litigation is a remaining primary-report check; these numbers alone cannot establish its holding or the exact legal boundary of voting outside the state.

Iowa's district returns permit a narrower arithmetic case. In district 4 the almanac gives Grinnell **12,900** and Martin **11,529**, including soldiers **3,366** and **1,136** respectively. [TA63, p. 61.] Subtracting those soldier subtotals leaves **9,534 to 10,393** among the remaining votes; Martin leads that subtraction by **859**, while Grinnell leads the combined return by **1,371**. [I] The included soldiers change the winner in this published return. This is a transparent subtraction, not a claim that soldiers were the sole historical cause: turnout, nominations and civilian behavior need not remain fixed in a counterfactual without the law.

## 3. Congress's oversight is distinct from a party's general support

In the Senate proceedings of **9 December 1861**, Grimes proposes a joint committee of **three senators and four representatives**, with power to send for persons and papers and sit during either House's sessions. The Senate adopts the modified resolution **33–3**. Hale wants Ball's Bluff especially identified; Wilson favors a general inquiry and cautions that responsibility cannot yet be assigned from the information available. [GCW, p. 32.] This page establishes the Senate action, not the House's concurrence or a final committee membership list.

The committee's **1863** report says it was appointed in December 1861. In its opening argument it praises Congress's provision of men and money, directs scrutiny toward those applying those means, and says it communicated information and recommendations to the President and cabinet. It describes its emphasis on the Army of the Potomac and says it heard nearly **200 witnesses**, including about **100 generals**. These are the committee's own retrospective descriptions, not neutral findings that Congress had supplied everything needed or that its selected military explanation was correct. [JCW, pp. 3–4; title and filing material, unnumbered preliminary leaves.]

[I] This supplies two useful distinctions. A legislator can support the war while criticizing its management; and information reaches political authorities through testimony, requests and competing explanations. Do not turn an inquiry into an automatic dismissal order or equate a committee report's later publication with what a player knew in December 1861. This packet leaves detailed battle adjudication to S4.

The Cameron episode offers a second channel: McPherson's note records the House's **30 April 1862** censure vote **79–45**; Lincoln's subsequent explanatory message describes emergency purchases and accepts shared executive responsibility. [MP, pp. 333–334.] [I] A relationship model should permit a president to defend a colleague while the House investigates expenditure. Neither the censure tally nor Lincoln's defense alone proves the underlying procurement allegations.

## 4. Press arguments: named publications and changing responses

The following is a small documentary sample, **not a circulation-weighted survey**. Editorial accusation is attributed, even when the accusation appears beside accurate legal text. No number of persuaded voters is inferred.

### Douglass's newspaper criticizes policy, then welcomes a conditional change

In its September number, *Douglass' Monthly* attacks Lincoln's address to free Black men and his colonization policy. The editorial argues that the administration has been firm in protecting slavery and hesitant in enforcing antislavery measures. On the same printed page, “The Peninsula Abandoned” attacks McClellan's leadership and identifies his protection of slavery as part of its criticism. [DM-S, pp. 707–708.] These are the newspaper's judgments. Its charges about motive, troop numbers, treachery or every military failure are not independently established by their appearance in print.

The October issue welcomes the preliminary proclamation and urges continued antislavery organizing. The article and the address to readers in Great Britain and Ireland stress that the promise remains contingent and the work unfinished; supporters should continue writing, speaking, raising funds and helping people escaping slavery. The address describes providing food, shelter, advice and help finding employment. [DM-O, pp. 721–723.]

The same issue explicitly endorses **James S. Wadsworth for governor** on its first page. [DM-O, p. 721.] [I] This connects a named newspaper's policy argument to an actual electoral choice; Seymour's victory in the almanac does not measure that paper's readership or persuasion effect. A published endorsement is an observable act, while its electoral reach remains unsourced.

[I] This sequence supports a Black abolitionist political voice whose response changes with a policy act while retaining independent aims and demands. It does not support a permanent “pro-Lincoln” or “anti-Lincoln” switch, or the claim that an October editorial speaks for every Black community. People seeking freedom and those helping them act for themselves; they are not rewards for presidential policy.

### Lincoln's reply to Greeley identifies a public argument

McPherson reproduces Lincoln's **22 August 1862** reply to Greeley's letter of the 19th, published in the *New York Tribune*. Lincoln presents saving the Union as his official object, discusses possible courses concerning slavery, and distinguishes his personal wish for universal freedom. [MP, p. 334.] This is an actual public presidential statement in the research record. Greeley's complete original letter has not been independently read in a retained issue here; do not invent its full demands or infer the Tribune's entire readership from Lincoln's reply.

[I] The political sequence needs the dates: the August reply and September preliminary proclamation are distinct public acts. Quoting the August statement as proof that the September policy cannot occur would replace a dated record with a fixed characterization. Likewise, later policy does not erase what critics could read in August.

### Distribution and coercion belong beside editorial content

McPherson prints the **16 August 1861** New York grand-jury presentment criticizing named newspapers, followed by the Post Office Department's **22 August** direction not to forward the New York papers described in it. He separately describes seizure of *Daily News* bundles in Philadelphia. [MP, p. 188.] The presentment asks about punishment; it is not itself a conviction. An exclusion from the mails, seizure of bundles, closure of a press and arrest of an editor are different actions.

The **24 September 1862** presidential proclamation includes persons discouraging enlistments or resisting military drafts among those made subject to military trial under its stated terms, and suspends habeas corpus for the described military detentions. [MP, p. 177.] This is the text's asserted authority. It does not establish the legality of each arrest, a particular editor's guilt, the number detained or the electoral effect of suppression. The later 1863 postal inquiry and 1864 newspaper cases in the same compilation are outside the November cutoff.

[I] A press system needs evidence about both arguments and their means of distribution. It must not assume every published voice reaches everyone, or reward suppression as a cost-free way to make opposition disappear. Specific situations concerning coercion, racism, slavery or persecution require S7's research and separate gate review. This note signs no consequences or player wording.

## Proposed rulings for the lead

1. [I] Date officeholding separately from election outcomes, using exact inauguration sources before implementation. Reject immediate election-night replacement of every governor or a publication table treated as a daily roster.
2. [I] Keep governor, state legislative chambers and federal delegation separate. Reject assigning every office from one statewide party result.
3. [I] Model soldier access only from jurisdiction-specific authority and delivery evidence. Reject treating every soldier as eligible, every ballot as received, or all soldiers as a fixed partisan bloc.
4. [I] Attribute press and committee claims and date when information exists. Reject using an editorial accusation or a retrospective committee conclusion as an omniscient fact.
5. [I] HOLD the Altoona exact-day event and quantified press/election effects. Reject filling gaps with the almanac's conflicting heading or a coefficient fitted to national seats.
