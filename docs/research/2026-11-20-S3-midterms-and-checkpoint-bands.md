Read with the [Congress note](2026-11-20-S3-congress-and-politics-1861-62.md), [state and press note](2026-11-20-S3-state-politics-soldier-voting-and-press.md) and [source register](2026-11-20-S3-source-register.md).

## 1. Define what November knows

The 37th Congress's second session closes on 17 July 1862 and its third begins on 1 December. The 38th's first regular session begins on 7 December **1863**. [MP, pp. 121, 140.] [I] November 1862 is therefore an election checkpoint, not the day a newly elected House begins legislating.

Three different comparisons belong in the research:

1. **State returns for elections held in 1862**, with the local ticket names and the source's completeness qualifications.
2. **The eventual election series for the 38th Congress**, including states that did not elect in the autumn 1862 group and the historian's party classification.
3. **The House that actually organizes in December 1863**, including replacements and disputed credentials. It has a Speaker vote, not simply a sum of November party labels.

[I] The proposed primary checkpoint is **30 November 1862**, closing the November month without importing December or 1863 elections. A game's report can show an estimate earlier, but it should not pretend the almanac's compiled, sometimes incomplete figures were a certified nationwide return available on 5 November. This is a proposed reporting convention, not a sourced delivery-lag estimate.

### Apportionment and district rules are different denominators

The **4 March 1862** act fixes the House at **241** from after 3 March 1863, assigning eight additional members, one each to Pennsylvania, Ohio, Kentucky, Illinois, Iowa, Minnesota, Vermont and Rhode Island. [ST12, p. 353, ch. 36.] This is the statutory allocation, not the count elected in the sixteen-state group or a count of seated members.

The **14 July 1862** act requires single-member districts of contiguous territory in states entitled to more than one representative. It expressly exempts California's election to the 38th and permits Illinois's additional representative to be elected at large, with the other thirteen elected from the existing districts unless the legislature otherwise provides. [ST12, p. 572, ch. 170.] [I] Illinois's at-large seat is therefore not a duplicate district accidentally added to the table. Do not compare the 241-seat statutory allocation, a partially represented wartime House, a roster of members-elect and the Directory's retrospective election series as though they measured the same thing.

### The almanac's roster summary is not a substitute for its district evidence

TA63 pp. 18–19 prints **76 Republicans/Unionists and 74 Democrats** for the House “as far as chosen.” The same list omits Vermont's later-elected successor members and leaves Wisconsin district 6 vacant. Its typography, omitted names and totals should not be silently reconciled by assigning all unknowns to a preferred party. The reconstruction of original election winners above has **151** seats; the printed roster footer has **150**, including a different political split. Subtracting the vacant Wisconsin seat is not enough to reconcile the party categories. [TA63, pp. 18–19, 50–62; TA64, p. 55; MP, pp. 123, 140–141.]

[I] Use the state rows as the auditable input and retain a roster-summary conflict. The footer is evidence of a contemporary published claim; it is not accepted here as a certified national seat total. A member-by-member modern party crosswalk is still required before these local labels can be compared directly with 85/72/27.

## 4. House composition: two reference series and an actual organizing vote

The **2024 Congressional Directory, printed p. 1355 (PDF p. 1366)**, supplies the following historian series. It explicitly says the figures reflect **initial election results** and exclude subsequent deaths, resignations, contests, special elections and party changes. Do **not** label these rows “final House membership.” [CD24, p. 1355.]

| Congress | Democratic | Republican | Other | House total | Definition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 37th, 1861–63 | 44 | 108 | 31 | 183 | Directory's retrospective initial-election series. |
| 38th, 1863–65 | 72 | 85 | 27 | 184 | Same definition; includes the wider election cycle, not just November 1862's known returns. |
| Difference [I] | +28 | −23 | −4 | +1 | Arithmetic between the two rows, not a same-district swing or a national popular-vote change. |

[I] The Directory's “Other” category must remain visible. An alternative grouping that gives **87** Republicans would require explicitly moving **two** members from another category; it cannot be treated as the same measurement as the Directory's **85**. The individual-member crosswalk remains HOLD. Do not average incompatible classifications or take their difference as random electoral uncertainty. Contemporary “Union” and “Emancipation” labels are retained in the state table rather than assigned to a modern category without that crosswalk.

TA64 p. 40 prints the **7 December 1863 Speaker vote**: **Colfax 101**, **Cox 42**, **Dawson 12**, **Mallory 10**, **Stebbins 8**, **King 6**, **Blair 2**, **Stiles 1**. These add to **182 votes cast [I]**, but the list includes two Louisiana claimants whose credentials MP says were later referred and rejected. It is evidence of an organizing majority, not a clean 182-member uncontested House. [TA64, p. 40; MP, p. 141.]

[I] Colfax's 101 is a useful later check on a governing coalition. It is not interchangeable with 85 Republicans, 87 Republicans plus independents, or 112 Republicans plus all “Other” members. A member's willingness to organize a House does not establish support on every policy.

## 6. Proposed bands for the 1B checkpoint

These are **new proposals**, not recenterings of existing acceptance criteria. They are [I] design envelopes around the page-backed references above. They are not historical uncertainty intervals. They should remain informative until the election owner has a causal mechanism, state franchise rules, apportionment, district representation and a modern-party crosswalk. A failure of a subsequently adopted criterion remains a failure until the lead changes it.

### Primary proposal: the November subset, with a fixed denominator

|---|---:|---:|---|
| Original election contests represented in the sixteen-state subset | **151 seats** | **151 exactly** for a historical-schedule reference | Structural denominator, not a calibration target to relax. ## 7. What the returns can explain

The election references show a closely divided New York governor's race, Democratic statewide victories in Ohio, Indiana and Illinois, and continued Republican success in Iowa and Massachusetts. They show different outcomes for different offices in Delaware and a divided Missouri political field. [TA63, pp. 50–62.]

[I] They are consistent with several simultaneous pressures, but election totals alone cannot identify causal shares for military disappointment, emancipation, racist campaigning, arrests, turnout, recruitment or local disputes. Sources documenting an argument establish that someone made it; they do not establish how many marginal votes it moved. A calibration model should expose its assumptions rather than fit an unsourced nationwide emancipation coefficient to the seat totals.

## Proposed rulings for the lead

1. [I] Adopt the November subset as the primary checkpoint definition, keeping later results pending. Reject scoring November against the entire later House.
2. [I] Retain local election labels alongside a future explicit party/coalition crosswalk. Reject treating “Union,” Republican and antislavery as synonyms.
3. [I] Use the proposed envelopes provisionally and retain the named state checks. Reject a single national total that can pass with the wrong regional distribution.
4. [I] Keep structural counts, vote-share estimates, exact historical winners and causal-mechanism checks distinct. Reject changing a denominator or a binding winner requirement merely to obtain a pass.

## Findings outside my scope

1. The election owner needs House-specific election laws, district/apportionment treatment and Wisconsin vacancy handling before historical-schedule data can be production inputs. Vermont belongs to the 1863 group even though it held a gubernatorial election in 1862.
2. An individual-member crosswalk must reconcile contemporary Missouri, Pennsylvania and New York labels with the official historian series. This research does not invent that mapping.
3. A national opinion model cannot be sourced from aggregate House seats alone. Soldier voting, eligibility and returns arriving after election day need independent owners and evidence.
4. The existing politics note's “final” label and 22–30 Democratic-gain band should not be reused unchanged: the directory's stated initial-election series gives **+28 D and −23 R** under one definition, not a range of interchangeable measurements.
