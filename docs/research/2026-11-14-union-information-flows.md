# How information about the Union's own forces reached Washington, 1861–65 (Mandate report-model research)

Status: first pass complete (2026-11-14). Written by a history research lane for the lead. It sources the report model the engine needs: who wrote a report about the Union's forces, what it claimed, how long it took to reach Lincoln, which way it was biased, and how often it came. It does not repeat the observer, correspondent and Joint Committee material already in `2026-11-14-union-command-and-promotion.md` (section 3); where that note covers a point this one only points to it.

Conventions:
- Every claim ends with one URL. **(fetched)** means a page this lane read; the reading tool summarises long pages, so quotations are short and figures were checked against the page's own table where it printed one. **(snippet)** means seen only in a search-result summary; treat it as a lead.
- Official Records (OR) material is read through civilwar.com page transcriptions, blog transcriptions of OR pages, and the beyondthecrater.com transcription. Where a blog transcribed the OR, the OR volume and page are given and the URL is the blog's.
- Two CIA "Studies in Intelligence" PDFs by Edwin Fishel (the scholar behind *The Secret War for the Union*) and the SUVCW copy of the Army Regulations were downloaded and read as text (marked "fetched, PDF read locally").
- Bates, "Lincoln in the Telegraph Office" (1907), is read from the archive.org full text. It is a memoir by a War Department cipher operator written 40 years later; page numbers below are the book's own, as the reading tool reported them (treat the pagination as approximate). Wheeler, "Mr. Lincoln's T-Mails", was not read; only summaries of it were seen (snippet).
- **[I]** marks our own inference, not a sourced fact.
- A search tool misattributed one figure during this pass (it called 88,665 Lincoln's; it is McClellan's, see 1.4). Anything not confirmed on a page is marked.

## 1. Strength returns

### 1.1 What the regulations required
- Company morning reports, signed by captain and first sergeant, went to the regimental adjutant before 8 a.m. and were consolidated within the next hour "for the information of the Colonel"; a consolidation sent upward was signed by colonel and adjutant (para. 236). https://suvcw.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/1861-us-army-regs.pdf (fetched, PDF read locally; this copy carries changes to June 1863)
- Monthly returns: captains to regiment, regimental commanders to the Adjutant-General's Office in Washington. All forwarded on the 1st of the next month, regimental returns as soon as the company returns were in (para. 458). In campaign, divisions, detached brigades and other detached commands also made monthly returns, "transmitted through the intermediate commanders" (para. 459). After any action a return of killed, wounded and missing, with each man's name, rank, regiment and wound, was required (para. 465). Same source.
- The regulations name no ten-day return for field armies (their only "tri-monthly" reports are the recruiting service's, para. 962). Field armies nonetheless filed "tri-monthly returns": the Army of the Potomac's abstract is dated 1862-07-10 (fetched below); the 1865 returns of Grant's armies are dated Jan 31, Feb 28, Mar 20, Mar 31 and Apr 10. https://www.beyondthecrater.com/resources/ors/vol-xlvi/part-1-sn-95/siege-of-petersburg-reports-95/or-xlvi-p1-002-abstract-tri-monthly-returns-pfde-jan-feb-mar-apr-1865/ (fetched). Other abstracts dated Aug 10 and Nov 10 1862 and Aug 10 1863, and a Defenses of Washington return of Sept 20 1862, fit a 10th/20th/last-day rhythm **[I]**. https://civilwarintheeast.com/?p=13382 (snippet)

### 1.2 The categories, with a real return
- Army of the Potomac, tri-monthly return of 1862-07-10 (McClellan): present for duty 4,327 officers and 95,449 men (99,776, our sum); "present for duty equipped" 98,631; aggregate present 117,714; aggregate present and absent 157,038. https://civilwar.com/official-record/582-peninsular-campaign-part-III/162587-312-series-i-volume-xi-iii-serial-14-peninsular-campaign-part-iii.html (fetched; OR I/11/3 p. 312)
- "Present for duty equipped" is the abstracts' own gloss for the "effective strength" of the armies. https://www.beyondthecrater.com/resources/ors/vol-xlvi/part-1-sn-95/siege-of-petersburg-reports-95/or-xlvi-p1-002-abstract-tri-monthly-returns-pfde-jan-feb-mar-apr-1865/ (fetched)
- Whole Union army on 1865-03-01, from tri-monthly returns: 602,598 "aggregate present available for duty" against 965,591 present and absent, so about 62 per cent of the paper army **[I]**. The footnote says the available-for-duty column includes men on extra or daily duty and men in arrest or confinement. Compiled by AAG Thomas M. Vincent, dated 1865-11-18. https://civilwar.com/official-record/990-union-letters-orders-reports/273209-137-series-iii-volume-v-serial-126-union-letters-orders-reports.html (fetched; OR III/5 p. 137)
- Forum glosses agree that "present for duty" is a subset of "aggregate present" and that "present and absent" counts everyone on the rolls. Community interpretation, not regulation text. https://civilwartalk.com/goto/post?id=2231376 (snippet)

### 1.3 Who compiled them, how often, how late
- Compilers: the company first sergeant and captain, the regimental adjutant, then each higher headquarters' adjutant general (1.1). Chain: company, regiment, brigade, division, corps, army, Washington **[I]**; the regulations name only the endpoints and "intermediate commanders."
- Frequency: morning reports daily; monthly returns; armies' tri-monthly abstracts (1.1).
- Delay to Washington: **not established.** No page read gives the date a return reached the Adjutant-General's Office. What is documented is that Washington often rebuilt strength afterwards from the morning reports. A statement of the Army of the Potomac on April 1 and June 20 1862 (present for duty 136,444 then 115,102; grand total 158,419 then 156,838) was certified by AAG E. D. Townsend on 1862-12-31, "compiled from the morning reports." https://civilwar.com/official-record/582-peninsular-campaign-part-III/162514-239-series-i-volume-xi-iii-serial-14-peninsular-campaign-part-iii.html (fetched; OR I/11/3 p. 239; the table's column labels are garbled in the source and were inferred by the reader). A strength statement for Sept 20 1862 (present for duty 164,359 across the army and the Defenses of Washington) was certified by Adjutant-General Thomas on 1863-02-27 from McClellan's signed morning report. https://civilwar.com/official-record/587-antietam-part-ii/175481-336-series-i-volume-xix-ii-serial-28-antietam-part-ii.html (fetched; OR I/19/2 p. 336; labels garbled). Both are retrospective certifications, months after the date.
- Commanders also quoted figures by wire when asked. One of Meade's two dispatches of 1863-07-05 (sent 8:30 a.m. and 6:00 p.m.; the page does not say which) gave "about 55, 000 effective in the ranks," excluding cavalry, baggage guards, ambulances and attendants: his own category, not a return column. https://civilwar.com/official-record/930-gettysburg-campaign-part-i/188701-79-series-i-volume-xxvii-i-serial-43-gettysburg-campaign-part-i.html (fetched; OR I/27/1 p. 79)

### 1.4 How figures were inflated or deflated, with named disputes
The pattern is that no single number was a lie. The categories differed, and the commander chose which to cite.
- **McClellan, July 1862.** Lincoln wrote on 1862-07-13 that he was told over 160,000 men had gone into the army on the Peninsula, that about 86,500 remained, and that perhaps half to two-thirds of the roughly 45,000 "still alive and not with it" were fit for duty. McClellan replied on 07-15: "All commanders find the actual strength less than the strength represented on paper"; present for duty 88,665 (3,215 officers, 85,450 men), absent by authority 34,472, absent without authority 3,778, present and absent 144,407; he conceded more than half the absentees were probably fit but said many of those absent by authority were sick or wounded "or under pretence" of it. https://rebellionrecord61-65.blogspot.com/2012/07/july-13-1862-sunday-lincoln-mcclellan.html (fetched; OR I/11/3 pp. 319, 321, blog transcription)
  - **Unreconciled:** his own return five days earlier (above) shows 99,776 present for duty and 157,038 present and absent, about 11,000 and 12,600 higher. I could not find what explains it.
- **Defenses of Washington, April 1862.** McClellan's statement of 1862-04-01 listed 7,780 at Warrenton, 10,859 at Manassas (projected), 35,467 in the Shenandoah Valley and 1,350 on the lower Potomac (55,456) and left about 18,000 in the Washington garrisons under Wadsworth (OR I/5/1 p. 61). https://rebellionrecord61-65.blogspot.com/2012/04/april-1-1862-tuesday-force-left-to.html (fetched). Wadsworth reported slightly more than 19,000 on 04-02. https://www.jfepperson.org/peninsula.htm (fetched; a chronology, not the OR). The dispute was what counted as "defense": the Valley and Manassas troops. A board of Stanton, Thomas and Hitchcock held it was too few, and McDowell's corps was held back about 04-04. https://civilwartalk.com/goto/post?id=2953041 (snippet; a forum summary, weak). Total implied by McClellan's statement, 73,456, is our sum **[I]**.
- **Rosecrans, 1863-02-20.** He wrote Halleck that before Stones River only about 63 to 67 per cent of each corps was present, that "the inspection system detects the illegal absentees," and that the present return forms did not show true strength. https://rebellionrecord61-65.blogspot.com/2013/02/february-20-1863-friday-combat.html (fetched; OR I/23/2 pp. 77-78, blog transcription). The commander himself called the aggregate misleading.
- **Antietam.** Figures of 87,164 (McClellan's report) and about 72,000 (a historian's fighting strength) circulate; sources disagree on which is present for duty and which is aggregate. https://civilwartalk.com/goto/post?id=941401 (snippet). Unresolved.
- **Livermore's "effectives"** equal present for duty in at least one account, and he corrected OR totals for detached men and absentees. https://dupuyinstitute.org/tag/american-civil-war/ (snippet)
- Direction of bias **[I]**: a commander asking for more men cited the smaller category (McClellan 1862, Rosecrans's complaint about the forms); a commander wanting to look ready or promising cited the larger (aggregate, "on paper"). The structural error, not forgery, dominates: in the returns above, aggregate present exceeds present for duty by about 18 per cent (July 1862: 117,714 against 99,776), and present-and-absent exceeds it by 16 per cent in April 1862 (158,419 against 136,444, the army before the Peninsula losses and sickness) but by 57 to 60 per cent in July 1862 (157,038) and March 1865 (965,591 against 602,598), all our arithmetic **[I]**.

## 2. Dispatches and the telegraph

### 2.1 Reach, volume, control
- The Military Telegraph Corps built about 12,201 miles of land line, 147.5 of submarine cable and about 1,000 of temporary field line; about 1,500 operators at peak; about 3 million telegrams from July 1862 to June 1864 (about 4,110 a day); 71 operators captured. The country had about 50,000 miles of wire before the war, a tenth in Confederate territory. https://www.civilwarmonitor.com/article/in-the-line-of-duty/ (fetched). Another popular page says "about 15,000 miles"; the sources differ. https://www.historyisnowmagazine.com/blog/2020/2/2/the-importance-of-the-telegraph-in-the-us-civil-war-part-3-the-unions-use-of-the-telegraph (fetched)
- McClellan had his own telegraph service at headquarters in 1861-62. In March 1862 Stanton centralised war telegraphy in the War Department's old library beside his office. https://www.mrlincolnswhitehouse.org/washington/the-war-effort/war-effort-telegraph-office/ (fetched). An executive order of 1862-02-25 put the lines under military control, with Edward S. Sanford as military supervisor of telegraphic messages and Anson Stager as superintendent. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwards_S._Sanford (snippet, Wikipedia fallback)
- The reach was ragged where armies moved. In the West the line ended at the river: Grant "sent Captain Wm. M. Dunn of my staff to Cairo, the nearest point where the telegraph could be reached" with the Vicksburg surrender. https://grantrevealed.com/?p=4722 (fetched). In June 1863 Banks warned that the Memphis–Cairo line was unprotected and "dispatches will have to be sent by river to Cairo." https://civilwar.com/official-record/993-vicksburg-part-iii/184374-386-series-i-volume-xxiv-iii-serial-38-vicksburg-part-iii.html (snippet; one of several OR Vicksburg pages the search returned, the exact page not confirmed). With a line connected, Grant and Stanton "would hold a conversation for an hour or two." https://www.historyofwar.org/sources/acw/grant/chapter40.html (fetched)

### 2.2 How Lincoln used it
- He came "morning, afternoon, and evening," often for hours, sometimes all night; read the incoming drawer from the top down to the last telegram he had seen ("down to raisins"); and wrote at Eckert's cipher desk. Bates, Introduction and ch. III, pp. 3-4, 40-42. https://archive.org/stream/lincolnintelegra00bates/lincolnintelegra00bates_djvu.txt (fetched). The office's manager and Wheeler both say he spent more time there than anywhere but the White House. https://www.historynet.com/?p=13739125 (snippet)
- In the last six months of 1861 he sent only 13 telegrams; from 1862 he wired field commanders directly. https://www.historyisnowmagazine.com/blog/2020/2/2/the-importance-of-the-telegraph-in-the-us-civil-war-part-3-the-unions-use-of-the-telegraph (fetched, popular source)
- He used the wire to ask for what he lacked, including about other armies: to Rosecrans on 1863-05-27, "Have you anything from Grant? Where is Forrest's headquarters?" https://civilwar.com/official-record/989-tullahoma-campaign-part-ii/181859-365-series-i-volume-xxiii-ii-serial-35-tullahoma-campaign-part-ii.html (fetched; OR I/23/2 p. 365). To Couch at Harrisburg, 1863-06-24: "Have you any reports of the enemy moving into Pennsylvania?" https://abook.qq.com/read/1000804604/443 (snippet; a secondary compilation of Lincoln's telegrams, unverified)
- He drew on non-commanders as direct sources: during Second Bull Run he depended on Haupt, the railroad chief, "far more than upon Pope or McClellan" (Bates ch. VIII, p. 120, fetched archive.org text above), and Navy dispatches reached him through Welles. Welles's diary has Porter's dispatch on Vicksburg arriving at the Navy Department on 1863-07-07 and Welles carrying it to Lincoln. https://civilwarnotebook.blogspot.com/2017/06/diary-of-gideon-welles-tuesday-july-7.html (fetched). Grant's recollection, as quoted on one page, that Vicksburg and Gettysburg news came "the same day" does not fit Welles's date or Lincoln's own telegram to Halleck of 07-07 ("We have certain information that Vicksburg surrendered … on the 4th"). https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2007/fall/telegram-lincoln-to-halleck (fetched)

Sources: https://civilwar.com/official-record/930-gettysburg-campaign-part-i/188697-75-series-i-volume-xxvii-i-serial-43-gettysburg-campaign-part-i.html ; https://civilwar.com/official-record/930-gettysburg-campaign-part-i/188700-78-series-i-volume-xxvii-i-serial-43-gettysburg-campaign-part-i.html ; https://civilwar.com/official-record/930-gettysburg-campaign-part-i/188701-79-series-i-volume-xxvii-i-serial-43-gettysburg-campaign-part-i.html ; https://civilwar.com/official-record/930-gettysburg-campaign-part-i/188702-80-series-i-volume-xxvii-i-serial-43-gettysburg-campaign-part-i.html (the July 4 10 p.m. entry on p. 78 is listed as received on the 6th; that is probably a transcription slip for the 5th).
- Why: Bates says "all the news we received dribbled over a single line of wire via Hagerstown," and when Meade moved beyond it, contact was lost until the Hanover Junction route opened a day or two later (pp. 155-157). Meade's chief of staff complained that the cipher operator Caldwell had left headquarters, so Meade could not send or read cipher (OR p. 78). Lincoln left the office on July 6 "a good deal dissatisfied" (Bates p. 156).
- A telegram could take half a day on a working line during a crisis; it could take over a day (27 h 50 m) when a single line was jammed or the cipher operator was missing. The one fast case (5 h 30 m) came late in the day, after the army had stopped moving **[I]**.

Courier, boat, rail:
- Vicksburg: Porter's gunboat took the dispatch up river to Cairo's telegraph, and it reached Welles on the 7th: three days from the event (Welles diary above; https://www.clevelandcivilwarroundtable.com/vicksburg-falls-pemberton-surrenders-to-grant-july-2-to-july-4-1863/ , snippet, for the gunboat to Cairo).
- Red River: Banks was beaten at Mansfield on April 8 1864. The first word the War Department forwarded to Grant, on April 19 at 10:30 p.m., was a Cairo telegram built from private letters written at Grand Ecore (April 10) and Alexandria (April 12); the War Department said these were "all the intelligence we have had from General Banks." Eleven days, second-hand and with the date wrong in the first telegram. https://civilwar.com/official-record/963-red-river-campaign-part-iii/207708-220-series-i-volume-xxxiv-iii-serial-63-red-river-campaign-part-iii.html (fetched; OR I/34/3 p. 220)
- Sheridan's dispatches to Grant went by scouts carrying tissue copies in tin foil (Bates ch. IV, pp. 64-66). A captured Johnston cipher dispatch from Vicksburg reached Washington "nearly a week" after its date (ch. V, p. 69).
- Rail: Halleck told the cabinet that moving two corps from Virginia to Chattanooga would take nearly three months; Eckert's written estimate was 15 days; Stanton ordered it at 2:30 a.m. on 1863-09-24; the first train left Bristow on 09-25 and the last arrived at Chattanooga on 10-06, about 11.5 days over 1,233 rail miles for some 23,000 men (Bates pp. 172-179, fetched archive.org text above). The staff's estimates were 5 to 8 times too slow, a bias in what Lincoln was told is possible **[I]**. Richmond had word within about a day through agents in Washington (Bates pp. 181-182).
- Mail: no transit time found; a letter's wait was "days or weeks." https://content.lib.washington.edu/civilwarweb/essay.html (snippet). It carried returns and letters, not the news Lincoln acted on **[I]**.

### 2.4 Out of contact, and commanders who controlled what Washington heard
- **First Bull Run, 1861-07-21:** the news stopped, then came one line: "Our army is retreating" (Bates ch. VI, pp. 91-92).
- **Ball's Bluff, 1861-10:** the message came to army headquarters, and Eckert held it from Lincoln, who found it under the blotter; his written orders were to deliver military telegrams to the commanding general first (Bates ch. VI, pp. 94-96). Stanton later barred even the President from seeing front telegrams early in 1862 (mrlincolnswhitehouse page above).
- **Seven Days:** McClellan's 12:20 a.m. telegram of 1862-06-28 ends "You have done your best to sacrifice this army." https://civilwar.com/official-record/580-peninsular-campaign-part-I/160265-61-series-i-volume-xi-i-serial-12-peninsular-campaign-part-i.html (fetched; OR I/11/1 p. 61). Bates says Sanford, the telegraph censor, cut the last paragraph before it reached Stanton (ch. VII, p. 109); the mrlincolnswhitehouse page dates a Sanford cut of a McClellan dispatch to July 1862. Date disagreement, kept open.
- **Fredericksburg, 1862-12:** Burnside forbade anyone at the front to wire or board boats; Stanton barred telegrams in Washington describing the battle. https://historynet.com/inconvenient-truth/ (fetched)
- **Rosecrans, 1863-09-22:** Lincoln wired that no word had come in 36 hours and asked him to relieve his anxiety (Bates p. 163).
- **Grant, Wilderness, 1864:** Lincoln wrote on April 30, "The particulars of your plans I neither know nor seek to know"; the telegraph did not follow the army across the Rapidan; Tinker's diary for May 6 reads "No news direct from Grant" (Bates ch. XVII, pp. 244-245).
- **Sherman, Nov–Dec 1864:** Lincoln said, in T. Harry Williams's retelling, "I know what hole he went in at, but I can't tell what hole he will come out of"; he wrote on Dec 26 that he had been "anxious, if not fearful." The army left the wire about Nov 15; Sherman's telegram offering Savannah arrived Dec 22. https://housedivided.dickinson.edu/sites/lincoln/letter-to-william-sherman-december-26-1864/ (fetched)
- Commanders who fed Washington selectively are in the earlier note, section 3.2 and 3.4 (Villard, Hooker's order against correspondents, Meade and Crapsey).

## 4. Enemy-strength estimates

- Fishel, writing for the CIA, calls the Union's service "a lower-case secret service," "improvised pretty much according to the taste of the individual commander," usually under a provost marshal; the largest organisation peaked at 70 people; there was no central bureau in Washington. https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Military-Intelligence-1861-63-II.pdf (fetched, PDF read locally)
- **Pinkerton under McClellan.** His order-of-battle list was good: about 220 regiment-sized units against 178 real ones at the Seven Days, with all 178 included. His totals were not: he struck a "medium" or "general estimate" by averaging gross guesses from prisoners (camp gossip), refugees (rumour), deserters (sometimes coached) and spies, reached 200,000 when Lee's top strength was 88,000, and argued that unidentified forces must lie beyond his count. Fishel concludes that to McClellan intelligence was "a lever against his superiors," not a weapon against the enemy, and that the Quartermaster General saw through it. Same author, Part I: https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Military-Intelligence-1861-63-I.pdf (fetched, PDF read locally). A footnote works the Yorktown number: 100,000–120,000 for about 50 identified regiments is 2,000–2,400 a regiment, double the table-of-organisation strength and four to six times the real one (same Part I).
- McClellan's own wires: 1862-04-07 "probably not less than 100,000 men, and probably more"; 05-03 Pinkerton's report of 100,000–120,000 at Yorktown against a page author's estimate of about 56,000; 06-25 "stated at 200,000." https://www.jfepperson.org/peninsula.htm (fetched; a chronology, with that page author's 56,000)
- **Contested.** A reviewer's summary of Fishel's 1988 article says "McClellan was not deceived"; commenters on that page say Pinkerton's early reports were accurate and he padded later to suit McClellan. https://behind.aotw.org/2006/04/18/fishel-secret-war-for-the-union/ (fetched; blog commentary, weak). Colonel John Clark's interrogation of 250 prisoners put Lee's strength at the start of the Maryland campaign at 97,175 against a roll-based 75,528 on Sept 2 (https://behind.aotw.org/?p=23 and https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/maryland-campaign/ , both snippet).
- **Bureau of Military Information (Hooker, 1863).** Founded under Hooker, headed by George Sharpe with Babcock and McEntee, reporting to the chief of staff Butterfield. Sources: prisoner, deserter and refugee interrogation as routine, its own scouts and about a dozen spies, cavalry, balloons, Signal Corps observers and cryptanalysts, newspapers, and dispatches from Washington; it produced "digested, semi-evaluative reports." Babcock's order of battle put Lee at 61,500 plus artillery before Chancellorsville, "within 1/4 of 1%" of the accepted figure, though two division errors and one cavalry error cancelled by accident. At Gettysburg on July 2 a tabulation from prisoner interrogations showed all of Lee's infantry present except Pickett's division. Fishel Part II above. Another summary gives 55,300 for late April, which disagrees with Fishel's 61,500 (snippet: https://olli.gmu.edu/docstore/300docs/1009-311-4OLLI%20IN%20Course%20Lecture%20%234%20Outline.pdf). Kept open.
- Fishel's generalisation: Union commanders usually wanted tactical information from cavalry, not strategic information from spies; Burnside "misplaced an enemy concentration" a mile from his pickets at Fredericksburg (Part I).
- The enemy had the same problem and the same leaks: Lee estimated Hooker's strength 25 per cent too low although a full and correct figure had been printed in a Washington newspaper as a medical return (Part II). Richmond learned of the Sept 1863 corps move within about a day from agents in Washington (Bates pp. 181-182).

## 5. Other channels

The earlier note covers observers (Dana, Cameron, Thomas), correspondents and censorship (Villard, Reid, Sickles, Hooker's order, Meade and Crapsey), Washburne and Wilson as informal sources, subordinates writing to Lincoln (Franklin, Smith, Newton, Cochrane), and the Joint Committee (section 3 and 2.8). New points here:
- **Lincoln read the newspapers, including Southern ones.** Hay's diary has him reading the Richmond Examiner (snippet); Noah Brooks said he liked "to skirmish" with the papers. https://friendsofthelincolncollection.org/lore-archive/ll_1955-10-24_01 (snippet). At Chancellorsville a secondary page says he "relied mostly on newspaper accounts from both sides" (fetched, weak). https://civilwarmonths.com/2023/05/07/the-chancellorsville-campaign-ends/
- **Governors as a wire.** Curtin asked Washington on June 12-13 1863 whether he should call out the militia (snippet: https://hd.housedivided.dickinson.edu/view/docs?page=163), and confirmed Villard's account to Lincoln on the night of Fredericksburg (https://historynet.com/inconvenient-truth/ , fetched). Lincoln asked Couch for "reports of the enemy" (2.2).
- **The Navy was a second channel into the War Department,** through Porter and the Cairo fleet captain Pennock (2.3), and its news reached Welles first.
- **Sanitary Commission inspectors** reported on conditions in more than 400 regimental camps by late October 1861 to the Commission and the War Department. https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Sanitary_Commission (snippet, Wikipedia fallback). Not read: what the reports said or how they were routed.
- **Joint Committee, extra:** hearings were private, witnesses were told not to speak to the press, and it met 272 times (snippet; same page as the earlier note's, plus https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congressional_Joint_Committee_on_the_Conduct_of_the_War).

## Design implications

**Does the evidence contradict a settled decision? No.** 0008, 0013 (ink, pencil, red; fading) and 0020 fit it. Two refinements to FOUNDATION section 5 ("reports go back through the commander, shaded by who he is") and section 8:
1. **Not every report came through the commander.** Lincoln read raw telegrams from the cipher drawer, wired subordinates directly (Couch, Curtin, Haupt), got Navy dispatches through Welles, and learned of Fredericksburg and the Wilderness from a reporter. Let the commander's report be one of several authors; give each report an author and a route, and allow a "direct line" from a corps commander, a governor or the Navy.
2. **Gatekeepers sit on the wire.** Stanton, Eckert and Sanford held, trimmed or delayed what Lincoln saw (2.4). A Secretary of War seat that filters is sourced; model it as a route with a delay and an edit chance, not as a given.

**Report types to model** (author, claim, delay, bias, frequency). Delays are in kernel days, with the hourly stamping FOUNDATION section 9 already allows for battle days.

| Type | Author | Claims | Typical delay | Typical bias | How often |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wire dispatch | the commander, chief of staff, or a subordinate | position, action, status, requests | same day on a working line (5-12 h at Gettysburg); up to 1.5 days when a line is jammed (2.3) | optimism early; vague; omits losses; cut by a censor in 1862 | many a day in a crisis; none when off the wire |
| Strength return, tri-monthly or monthly | adjutant general of each command, signed by the commander | present for duty, equipped, aggregate present, present and absent | not established; model as courier/mail, several days to two weeks **[I]**; as-of date is the report's date | structural: aggregate counts absentees; PFD includes extra duty and arrest; commander picks the category | 3 a month for armies; monthly below |
| Commander's quoted figure | the commander | "effective," his own category | wire delay | smaller when he wants men (McClellan 1862); excludes cavalry, trains | when pressed, as Lincoln did 07-13-1862 |
| Casualty return | regimental colonels via commander | named killed, wounded, missing | days to weeks; first wire figure is a round guess | low by about 20 per cent in the one case (Fredericksburg) | once per action |
| Enemy estimate | the intelligence officer or the commander | enemy strength, order of battle | 1-3 days after interrogation | about 1.0x under a good bureau (Fishel: within a fraction of a per cent at Chancellorsville by luck; a snippet gives 2 to 7 per cent elsewhere); 2x-4x under the McClellan method; a commander's own number can exceed his bureau's | on request; before and after battles |
| Observer or inspector | a sent man | character, morale, truth of the commander | by wire or by hand; see earlier note | answers to his sender; Dana's Chickamauga wire was too dark | at the sender's wish |
| Press account | correspondent | what he saw | about 1 day (Fredericksburg, Wilderness) | the paper's and the commander's friends' politics; stopped by Stanton's ban | per battle |
| Direct informal source | governor, congressman, wounded, Navy, railroad chief | local fact, rumour | 0-2 days | partisan; sometimes the best source | any time |
| Retrospective certified statement | Adjutant-General's Office | strength on a past date | 5 to 11 months (Townsend, Thomas) | best figure available | once, for the reckoning |

**What Lincoln could and could not plausibly know** **[I]**, built on the above:
- **Could, in 1862 onward:** that an army's headquarters had wired within the day, what its commander chose to say, aggregate strength about a week to ten days old, a first casualty guess, and the enemy's order of battle if a bureau existed (1863 on). During a battle on a working line, hourly fragments; at Gettysburg, a lag of 5 to 28 hours with a gap when the army outran the line.
- **Could not:** the true present-for-duty of an army on a given day (the paper army was 18 per cent bigger than the fighting one by the aggregate-present column and 16 to 60 per cent bigger by the present-and-absent column); the location of divisions; casualties in the first day or two; anything from an army off the wire (Grant from the Rapidan, Sherman for five weeks); the truth about a commander without an observer or a rival; his own commander's category choice.
- **From the West and Trans-Mississippi,** the lag was the relay: three days for Vicksburg, eleven days of silence then a second-hand, wrongly-dated letter for the Red River. A pack can set edge latency by the last point on the wire (Cairo, Memphis, Fort Monroe).
- **A posture that cuts reports.** Grant preferred to be cut off (Bates p. 244) and Sherman chose silence; model "latitude" as a lowering of report frequency the player buys with trust. It also lowers what the staff map can show.
- **Scale for bias:** first-report understatement of own losses about 20 per cent; commander's category choice 16 to 60 per cent between PFD and the paper totals; enemy overestimate 1.0x to 4x depending on who ran the bureau.
- **The reckoning (FOUNDATION section 6):** the AG's retrospective statements are the real figures; show them against the wires, as Townsend's 1862-12-31 statement does for April and June of that year.

## Findings outside my scope

- **A Secretary of War seat that filters the wire** (Stanton, Eckert, Sanford, 2.4) is sourced and is a candidate seat for the people systems. It also puts a second "gate" next to the commander's.
- **The enemy's side of the same model (0009).** Richmond received Washington's order of battle within about a day through agents, and a Washington newspaper printed a Union medical return that gave Hooker's strength to Lee (Bates pp. 181-182; Fishel Part II). A symmetric opponent should have the same leaks, and Union press discipline (Hooker's exchange and newspaper bans) belongs to the press system.
- **Staff estimates of what was possible were badly biased.** Halleck said the corps transfer would take about three months; it took 11.5 days (2.3). The staff's view of rail capacity, and its "assessment" of what a commander can do, should carry the same kind of error. Relevant to the rail-capacity resource in FOUNDATION section 7.
- **Lincoln's time at the telegraph office was itself the cost of information** (hours, sometimes all night, 2.2). It bears on the attention rules of 0025.
- **Security as a lever.** Hooker cancelled newspaper exchanges with the enemy, curtailed flags of truce, punished press conjecture about movements and stopped outgoing soldier mail on the eve of his 1863 march (Fishel Part II, note 13). It cost him nothing in the sources read but made Lee's picture of him worse; a pack could offer such orders at a cost to the press and morale blocs. Related press points: Greeley rewrote Villard's Fredericksburg account as too shocking (historynet page above), and Stanton cut a paper's telegraph access in April 1862 (snippet: https://civilwarnotebook.blogspot.com/2013/02/after-philadelphia-inquirer.html), so the editor and the censor are filters after the author.
- **Wheeler and Bates are read second-hand:** Wheeler was not read at all, and Bates is a 1907 memoir. A lane with the books could check the Gettysburg line story and the Sanford cut.

## Gaps and weak links (do not rely on these without more work)

- Not found: when any return reached Washington (the central number for the strength-return delay); mail transit times; the date and size of Meade's first full casualty return; Shiloh's first reports (the earlier note has the press fight over them).
- Unreconciled: McClellan's July 10 return against his July 15 wire (1.4); the Antietam strength (87,164 against about 72,000); Babcock's Chancellorsville figure (61,500 against 55,300); the July 1862 versus June 28 date of the Sanford cut; the Gettysburg 10 p.m. receipt entry (2.3); Grant's "same day" remark against Welles's 07-07.
- Snippet-only: the Sept 1861 camp inspections, the Feb 1862 order's wording, the Richmond Examiner anecdote, the Wheeler claims, the 23,049 Gettysburg figure, and Hooker's Chancellorsville 55,300.
- Not read at all: Livermore's tables, Fishel's book, Bates beyond ch. XVII (the last 195,000 characters), Tap's "Over Lincoln's Shoulder," Hartwig's appendix (403), the Medical Department's returns, and any state adjutant-general's reports.

## R92: the 1861 mail and wire offices (20 November 2026)

**Finding for the 5 March–31 July window:** the passages read below do not establish a holder who screened the president's incoming military telegrams during this window. Leave the wire-filter role unfilled. This is a limit of the evidence read, not a claim that no screening occurred. Do not turn the private secretary's caller or mail work into the kernel's wire-filter role. Decision 0105 permits leaving that role vacant when its holder cannot be established.

**The mail.** John Hay's own account, written to Herndon in 1866 and reproduced in Herndon and Weik, *Abraham Lincoln: The True Story of a Great Life*, vol. II, printed pp. 225–226, assigns receiving congressional and other visitors, communicating presidential messages, and general supervision to Nicolay. Hay assigns opening, reading and answering letters, reviewing newspapers and supervising record clerks to himself. He describes Lincoln gradually handing correspondence over to him. The account covers the administration generally: it does not date that handover to March 1861, or give either secretary control of all incoming military wires. Read the [book's OCR, including those page markers](https://archive.org/stream/abrahamlincolntr02hernuoft/abrahamlincolntr02hernuoft_djvu.txt), rather than treating the quotation on the White House website as a newly read primary document. Extract: `runs/r92/hay-read-extract.txt`. The website was a discovery aid; Gutenberg returned a maintenance page.

**The early wire office.** David Homer Bates, *Lincoln in the Telegraph Office* (1907), printed pp. 25–29, describes the operators reaching the War Department in April, its instruments in chief clerk Sanderson's room, and the corps taking orders directly from the Secretary of War. He names David Strouse as first superintendent, with an official appointment dated 15 May 1861 (p. 27, note 2), then Gilmore and Eckert as successors. The passage does not attribute withholding or trimming Lincoln's telegrams to Strouse or date those successions. Bates says that on 24 May he personally recorded and delivered the Ellsworth telegram to Lincoln (p. 29). A superintendent's title and an operator's delivery are insufficient evidence to populate a role whose effect is to hold or trim the president's incoming wires [I].

**The dated withholding case is later.** Bates, printed pp. 93–96, places Eckert at McClellan's headquarters during the September period. Cameron's written instructions required military telegrams to go first to the commanding general. Bates describes Eckert withholding Stone's dispatch from Lincoln on **21 October 1861** under those instructions (pp. 94–96). This establishes a named, later case of withholding, not a March–July appointment to a general presidential filter office. Its route through McClellan's headquarters also matters: do not move it to the White House mail desk [I]. Bates, pp. 38–40, dates Stanton's centralization and supervision of military news to 1862. That later arrangement cannot fill the July gap [I]. Read the [Bates OCR](https://archive.org/stream/lincolnintelegra00bates/lincolnintelegra00bates_djvu.txt); extracts with printed page markers: `runs/r92/bates-read-extracts.txt`. These are retrospective witnesses, not contemporary appointment orders.

**Data consequence:** no person, holder, post kind, trait, value or situation is added or relabelled. The existing Nicolay post retains its null role. Filling the wire role merely to make the filter-hold band pass would invent the missing evidence. A later-window implementation can use the October case after establishing its dated appointment and route; R92 does not extend the history run beyond July.
