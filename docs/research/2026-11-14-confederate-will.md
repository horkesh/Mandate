# Confederate national will, 1861–65: what raised it, what wore it down, and what could measure it (Mandate research)

Status: first pass complete (2026-11-14), for decision 0028 (two national wills decide the war) and 0029 (the Confederacy runs on the same machinery). It builds on `2026-11-14-confederate-side.md` (cited as CS, with that note's keys) and does not repeat its cast, cabinet or command material. **The evidence does not contradict 0028's two-wills premise, but it changes what the Confederate will has to be:** it is not one number. The record shows a will that split by class and region, that the army and the home front carried differently, and that outside observers (bond markets) judged lost by the end of 1863 while many Confederates kept fighting into 1865. Historians still disagree whether it broke before the armies did (section 14). Enslaved people, about 39 percent of the Confederacy's population (3.52 million of 9.10 million in 1860, per the secession-winter note, section 6.2), were not part of this will at all; their actions belong in the model as a separate force that drained Confederate capacity (section 11). See "Design implications".

Conventions: citation keys in [brackets] resolve in the Sources table at the end. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result summary, page not read. **(agent)** = read by a fetch agent whose report summarised the page. **[I]** = our interpretation or arithmetic, never a source's claim. **UNVERIFIED** = asked for and no source found. Confidence: **high** = a primary text read in full, or two independent sources that agree; **medium** = one reputable secondary page, usually Wikipedia; **low** = snippet only, or sources that conflict.

## 2. How Confederate will can be measured at all

There were no polls. What survives, and what each can and cannot show:

|---|---|---|---|

Schwab says monthly averages reflect "deliberate public opinion", daily moves the battle news [SCHWAB] p. 169 | High for the series, medium for the reading |
| Commodity prices | Lerner's index (100 in 1861, about 2,800 in Oct 1864, over 9,200 in Apr 1865) [W-FIN]; Jones's Richmond flour prices (section 6) | Hardship, the bread riots' cause; feeds desertion through letters home | High |

## 3. Battlefield results and how the news travelled

- **Victories carried the will in 1861–63, and the record shows it.** Schwab reads Confederate credit as strongest in the summer of 1862 and early 1863 (the Peninsula, Second Bull Run, Lee's and Bragg's offensives) and weakest after the reverses of spring 1862, July 1863, late 1863 and early 1865 [SCHWAB] pp. 169–71. **High** for the pattern in the gold series; **medium** for the causal reading, which is Schwab's.
- **The news came late, wrong and through the enemy's papers.** Gettysburg was fought 1–3 July 1863. Jones heard of "a great battle" on 5 July from Northern papers brought by flag-of-truce boat to City Point; read the governor of Pennsylvania's word "indecisive" as a Union defeat on 6 July; on 7 July recorded a telegram, "credited" in the War Department, that Longstreet and Ewell had captured 40,000 prisoners; and on 9 July wrote "Instead of 40,000 we have only 4000 prisoners" and called the telegraph operators "great liars" [JONES-1] pp. 369–75. Vicksburg surrendered on 4 July; Johnston's telegram of 7 July reached Richmond on 8 July [JONES-1] p. 375. **High.** [I] For the model: a Confederate defeat far from the capital took four days to arrive and a rumour of victory could run for two days first.
- **Atlanta, by contrast, arrived within two days.** The city fell on 1–2 September 1864; Jones noted an "ugly rumor" on 3 September that he blamed on the telegraph operators, and Hood's dispatch the same night [JONES-2] (4 Sep). **High.**
- **The government suppressed bad news at home.** No account of the Richmond bread riot appeared in the papers the next day "for obvious reasons" [JONES-1] p. 286; Wikipedia says the government suppressed most reports to protect soldiers' morale and that later papers gave the wrong date [W-BREAD]. **High.**
- **The markets saw July 1863 as the break.** Brown and Burdekin find two turning points in the London price of the Confederate cotton bond that were not reversed: Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863, and the fall of Atlanta in September 1864 (snippet) [S-BB]. Weidenmier and Oosterlinck put the implied chance of Confederate victory at about 42 percent before Gettysburg and Vicksburg and about 15 percent by the end of 1863, and conclude that McClellan's possible election and Confederate victories in 1864 did little to change the market's view (NBER abstract, read in full by the PR-fixes lane) [S-WO]. A later Amsterdam series, cited second-hand, falls from under 20 percent at the start of 1864 to under 3 percent by its end (snippet) [S-WO]. Weidenmier also built a weekly "grayback" series whose turning points differ from the Union greenback market's (catalogue record only) [S-WEID]. **Low to medium**: the Weidenmier and Oosterlinck figures are from the NBER abstract (read in full), but no paper is read and Brown and Burdekin are a snippet; the direction agrees with Schwab's gold series.
- **Failed peace talks rallied the capital.** After Hampton Roads (3 February 1865), where Lincoln required reunion, Jones expected that "the South will soon be fired up again", and on 6 February Davis told a crowd at the African Church that Lincoln and Seward might find "they had been speaking to their masters" [JONES-2]. Historians dispute whether Davis entered the talks in bad faith to discredit the "croakers" (Sanders, via Wikipedia) [W-HR]. **High** for the rally, **medium** for the motive.

## 4. Conscription, its exemptions and resistance to it

- **The acts.** 16 April 1862 (18–35), 27 September 1862 (to 45), 17 February 1864 (17–50); substitution abolished 28 December 1863 [CS:WIKI-Consc]. **Medium.**
- **What the draft yielded against what it exempted.** The Bureau of Conscription's report of February 1865 counts 81,993 conscripts and 67,054 exempts east of the Mississippi from 16 April 1862, an exemption rate of about 45 percent; the Official Records print the exempt total as 66,586 and note that the parts add to 67,054 [MOORE] p. 107, [OR-IV-3] p. 1101. Moore thinks the true number exempted was "much larger": the Bureau's own registration office counted 96,578 exempts in six states in January 1864, Lee (citing Kemper) claimed 40,000 in Virginia alone, and Moore estimates more than 100,000 exempted outright plus 25,000–40,000 detailed to civil work [MOORE] pp. 107–8. Of men examined, 39.6 percent were exempted as physically unfit [MOORE] p. 108. **Medium** (the table's OCR is damaged; Moore's summary is clean).
- **Governors shielded men as "state officers".** Late in 1864 the Bureau counted 25,892 men on governors' certified lists: North Carolina 14,675, Georgia 8,229, Virginia 1,422, Alabama 1,223, South Carolina 233, Mississippi 110, Florida 109 (OCR reads "tog"). Davis rejected the report as inaccurate; an inspector put Mississippi's real figure at 4,000 or more; by February 1865 North Carolina's figure had fallen to 5,589 [MOORE] p. 95, citing [OR-IV-3] pp. 735, 866–68, 1102. **Medium.**
- **Class resentment had a statute behind it.** The Twenty Negro Law exempted "one person, either as agent, owner or overseer" per plantation of twenty enslaved people "to secure the proper police of the country", 19 days after the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation [W-20]; small farmers called it a "rich man's war" [CS:EV-20S]. Even Jones, a loyal War Department clerk, wrote after Atlanta that "some 40,000 landowners, and the owners of slaves, are at their comfortable homes ... while the poor and ignorant are relied upon to achieve independence" [JONES-2] (4 Sep 1864). In April 1863 General D. H. Hill wrote that conscription in North Carolina had become "a machine for making money and putting pets in office" [JONES-1] p. 286. **High** for the law's text and the quotations.
- **Resistance became organised where the draft met Unionism.** The Gainesville hangings (October 1862) followed opposition to conscription in North Texas [W-GAINES]; the North Carolina peace meetings of March 1862 were against the draft [W-RED]; Red Strings members may have been "motivated by opposition to conscription as much as, or more than," Unionism [W-RED]. **Medium.**

## 5. Impressment and the tax in kind

- **Impressment paid below market.** Jones recorded the government impressing flour at $12 a barrel while it sold at $24 in November 1862 [JONES-1]; Encyclopedia Virginia (via the CS note) puts government prices at about 50 percent below market and says Georgia declared impressment unconstitutional [CS:EV-Imp]. **High.**
- **The tithe was resented because it took produce, not money.** The April 1863 act levied one-tenth of the 1863 crop, delivered to post quartermasters by 1 March 1864 [SCHWAB] p. 292. North Carolina meetings resolved that they were "opposed to the tithe system ... discriminating against and taxing the labor and industry of the agricultural classes"; farmers were "willing to pay any reasonable tax in money" [SCHWAB] pp. 296–97. Collection was wasteful: food left exposed and ruined, unauthorised collectors [SCHWAB] p. 297. **High** (Schwab cites the Standard and other papers).
- **What it raised.** By the end of 1863, a little over $5 million in produce, "less than $350,000 in specie"; in the six months after 1 April 1864, over $140 million, "about $7,000,000 in specie", by Schwab's reckoning enough to feed an army of a million men for a month; the agitation forced Congress to relax it [SCHWAB] pp. 297–98. The CS note's two incompatible figures ($62 million over the war against about half of all revenue) remain unresolved [CS:EV-Imp], [CS:WIKI-Fin]. **Medium.**
- **Money taxes, by state.** $82,262,349.83 collected in currency by April 1864: Georgia 22 million, Virginia 21.5, South Carolina 12.5, North Carolina 10, Alabama 9.5, Texas about 3, Mississippi 2, Florida 1, Louisiana $200,000, Tennessee $140,000, Arkansas presumably nothing [SCHWAB] p. 293. [I] The occupied states' near-zero yields are an occupation proxy in their own right. **High** for the figures as Schwab gives them.
- **Impressment fell on enslaved people too, and the gate governs that.** On 5 September 1864 Lee called for 2,000 enslaved men "to be impressed" for the Petersburg works [JONES-2]; the CS note covers the 1863 law and the 1864 requisition of 20,000 [CS:EV-Imp]. This belongs to section 11, not to white taxpayers' grievances.

In the Shenandoah Valley in September 1864 it still sold for $8 in gold [JONES-2]. Food in the capital grew dearer even in gold, which fits the transport and impressment story more than money alone.

## 7. Food shortages and the bread riots

- **Spring 1863 was the crisis.** Riots, mostly by women, in Atlanta (16 Mar), Salisbury (18 Mar), Mobile and High Point (25 Mar), possibly Raleigh (25 Mar), Petersburg (1 Apr) and Richmond (2 Apr) [W-BREAD]. Causes listed: inflation outrunning wages, refugees (Richmond grew from 38,000 in 1860 to over 100,000 by 1863), foraging armies, salt shortages, army seizures that made farmers withhold supplies, and hoarding [W-BREAD]. **Medium.**
- **Richmond, 2 April 1863, from an eyewitness.** Jones saw "a few hundred women and boys" gather in Capitol Square, "more than a thousand" by the time they marched; a young woman told him they were "going to find something to eat"; they emptied speculators' stores, then broke windows on Main Street for "silks, jewelry"; Governor Letcher had the Riot Act read and gave five minutes; Davis spoke from a dray, asking them to go home "so that the bayonets there menacing them might be sent against the common enemy"; Seddon refused to call troops, calling it "a municipal or State duty"; the government then issued rice [JONES-1] pp. 284–86. Wikipedia gives about 5,500 participants [W-BREAD]; the CS note's snippet gave about 5,000. **High** for Jones's account; the crowd size is **low** (one eyewitness at 1,000-plus against later estimates of 5,000–5,500).
- **How the government and press explained it away.** Within two days some in Richmond believed the riot "stimulated from the North, and executed through the instrumentality of emissaries" [JONES-1] pp. 286–87; Jones blamed foreign residents with exemptions; later reports cast rioters as "Yankees" or the "unworthy poor" [W-BREAD]. [I] A pack should not adopt either story: the record is of soldiers' families who could not buy food.
- **The response was relief plus food policy.** Richmond set up markets for the "worthy poor" [W-BREAD]; McCurry argues the riots pushed the Confederacy into welfare measures aimed at soldiers' wives (via Wikipedia) [W-BREAD]; Congress (4 Apr) and Davis (10 Apr) urged food crops over cotton and tobacco [RICH-1] pp. 331–33. **Medium.**
- **Hunger reached the army.** Lee, January 1864, on short rations and desertion to the enemy (quoted on Wikipedia) [W-BREAD]. **Medium**; the Official Records page was not checked.

## 8. Desertion over time

- **The trend, in the Confederacy's own words:**
  - Early 1862: "probably not less than thirty thousand" absent soldiers (Davis, veto message) [RICH-1] p. 157.
  - 1 August 1863: after Gettysburg and Vicksburg, Davis declares that the absent "would, if present in the field, suffice to create numerical equality" (p. 330), offers amnesty to all who return within 20 days (except men twice convicted of desertion), and asks "the wives, mothers, sisters, and daughters of the Confederacy" to send them back [RICH-1] pp. 329–31. Schwab notes desertions "particularly frequent" that summer [SCHWAB] p. 170.
  - Late September 1864: "two-thirds of our men are absent—some sick, some wounded, but most of them absent without leave" (Davis at Macon, from the Daily Richmond Enquirer of 29 Sep 1864) [ROWLAND-6] p. 343.
  - 24 February 1865: "several hundred" deserted from Hill's corps in two weeks, mostly North Carolinians from the west of the state; "letters written to the soldiers by their friends at home" tell them the cause is "hopeless"; "the number of deserters is so large in the several counties that there is no danger to be apprehended from the home guards"; "The deserters generally take their arms with them" (Lee to Vance) [OR-I-47-2] pp. 1270–71.
  - 3 March 1865: "over 100,000 deserters scattered over the Confederacy"; desertion has "lost the stigma"; deserters are "shielded by their families and by the sympathies of many communities" and "form the numerical majorities in many localities"; since the reserves took over the task in September 1864, fewer deserters have been returned "not only in proportion to the number, but absolutely" (Preston, Bureau of Conscription, to Breckinridge) [OR-IV-3] pp. 1119–20.
  - **High** for each quotation. The page for Preston's letter is [I] inferred from the volume's gathering marks (signature 71 begins p. 1121).
- **The totals are disputed.** An "official" count of 103,400 Confederate deserters (Ella Lonn's 1928 figure) and Weitz's argument that it is too low were seen only in an agent's summary of Wikipedia; the Confederate States Army article's lead says "more than 100,000" and that some estimates reach a third of all Confederate soldiers (agent) [W-CSA]. Lonn's own derivation was not reached (her book is lending-only on archive.org). **Low.**
- **Why men went: home, not the enemy.** Lee's and Preston's letters both put the cause in the counties: despondent letters, families who hid deserters, communities where deserters outnumbered the home guard. Bearman's study of North Carolina regiments ("desertion as localism") and Weitz's argument that soldiers fought for their families rather than a nation reached us only in an agent's summary (agent) [W-CSA]. Lee in January 1864 blamed hunger [W-BREAD]. **Medium.** [I] Desertion is a regional term (where the soldier's home is, how close the enemy is, how hungry his family is), not a national one.
- **The fall of the Army of Northern Virginia, for scale:** about 28,231 paroled at Appomattox (agent, citing OR Ser. I vol. 46 pt. 1 p. 1277) [W-ANV]; 89,270 at Bennett Place [W-BENNETT]; 174,223 surrendered in all, from a weak source (agent) [W-CSA]. **Low** to **medium.**

## 9. Governors who resisted Richmond, and the 1863 elections and peace movements

- **Brown and Vance.** The CS note covers both (Brown's challenges to the draft and impressment; Vance's broad exemptions, habeas fights and state blockade running) [CS:GA-Brown], [CS:NC-War]. This note adds the state-officer counts above (section 4). [I] Both governors also kept their states in the war: Vance crushed the peace candidate in 1864, and Beringer and colleagues cite Brown and Vance against the "states' rights" explanation of defeat [W-WSL]. **Medium.**
- **The 1863 congressional elections.** Held from May to November 1863, over six months because of occupation and refugee voting. Anti-administration members rose from 26 to 41 of 106 in the House and from 11 to 12 of 26 in the Senate; Davis kept majorities in both. Nine of Georgia's ten members were defeated. Alabama, Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Texas sent 32 of the 41 opponents. Grievances: shortages, inflation, financial mismanagement, conscription, taxation, habeas suspension and martial law. Most opponents still favoured secession and the war; in North Carolina none of the new members had voted for secession and five ran on peace platforms (a search summary says six of ten); W. R. W. Cobb of Alabama was so openly for peace that he was not seated. The administration's majority "would not have been possible without the near unified support from the occupied districts", whose members were chosen by refugees and soldiers [W-1863], [S-NCPEACE]. **Medium** (Wikipedia; the underlying work is Alexander and Beringer, Yearns and Martis, not read).
- [I] The 1863 result is an inverted signal: districts the Union occupied voted for Davis through their refugees and soldiers, while districts still under Confederate control turned against him. A model that lowers support for the government wherever the enemy is near would get 1863 backwards.
- **North Carolina's peace movement.** William W. Holden's Raleigh *Standard* reported and backed more than 100 public peace meetings in 1863; Confederate troops sacked the *Standard* office on 8 September 1863 and Vance issued a proclamation against the meetings the next day (snippets from NCpedia and an NC State exhibit) [S-NCPEACE]. Vance beat Holden on 4 August 1864, 58,070 to 14,491 (80.0 to 20.0 percent), a margin of 43,579 [W-NC64], [S-NC64]; an older account gives 54,323 to 20,448 [S-NC64]. NCpedia (snippet) calls Holden's defeat, and the military repression in the central Piedmont that followed, the end of the peace movement [S-NC64]. In February 1865 a Raleigh correspondent warned that after Hampton Roads the *Standard* and *Progress* still preached submission, and "Desertions will increase; people will withhold their supplies" (D. K. McRae) [OR-I-47-2] p. 1251. **Medium**; the 1864 totals conflict.
- **Peace societies.** The Red Strings (Heroes of America) in the North Carolina Piedmont, south-west Virginia and East Tennessee protected deserters, helped escaped prisoners and passed information to Union forces; "as many as 10,000" members by the war's end is an estimate [W-RED]. **Medium.**
- **Stephens.** The vice president was an advocate of ending the war by 1863, and on 6 January 1865 asked the Senate to approve formal negotiations [W-HR]. **Medium.**

## 10. Unionism in the mountains and the inner civil war

- **Where it was.** East Tennessee, western North Carolina, north Alabama, north Georgia, the Texas Hill Country, north Texas, north Mississippi, the Arkansas Ozarks, and Loudoun County, Virginia [W-UNION]. About 100,000 white Southern Unionists served in the Union army, and every Confederate state but South Carolina raised white Union units [W-UNION]. **Medium.**
- **How the Confederacy answered it.** Martial law and hangings in East Tennessee after the bridge burnings (1861) [W-ETBB]; the Nueces killings (August 1862) [W-NUECES]; the Gainesville hangings (October 1862), by a "Citizens' Court" with no standing in law, seven of whose twelve jurors were slaveholders in a county where 11 percent of households were [W-GAINES]; Shelton Laurel (January or February 1863) [CS:NC-War]. **Medium.** All of this is in the gate (civil liberties, treatment of civilians).
- **Networks that drained the army.** A Shenandoah Valley network of Mennonites, Brethren and Unionists hid draft resisters and deserters and guided them to Union lines (more than 30 "depots" in Rockingham County); the South Carolina Upstate sheltered deserters; in North Carolina "Buffaloes" mixed deserters, draft dodgers, Unionists, escaped enslaved people and outlaws [W-UNION], [W-RED]. **Medium.**

## 11. The collapse of slavery behind the lines

This section is under the gate (0021, 0026). Enslaved people are agents here, and the Confederate measures are recorded as what they were: attempts to control people who were freeing themselves.

- **Scale.** At least 500,000 enslaved people are estimated to have fled to Union lines (Amy Murrell Taylor; a History News Network essay; the Civil War Monitor says about one-eighth of the enslaved population); a review of Chandra Manning's work gives about 400,000 from the Confederate states (snippets) [S-500K]. The CS note's 500,000 figure came from a newsletter and was unverified [CS:WEB-Strike]; these snippets lift it to "the usual estimate", still **low** until a monograph is read. Many more left their enslavers without reaching the army, or were turned away [S-500K].
- **Self-emancipation forced Union policy, and the Confederacy knew it.** The CS note's chronology (Butler's "contraband" ruling, the Confiscation Acts, the Proclamation) and Du Bois's "general strike" apply [CS:UMD-Chron], [CS:VERSO-Roed]. Roediger's account says the loss of labour shook Confederate morale [CS:VERSO-Roed]. **Medium.**
- **Black enlistment drew on the Confederacy's own population.** Black enlistments by state of enlistment: Louisiana 24,052, Tennessee 20,133, Mississippi 17,869, Virginia 5,919 (and the border states Kentucky 23,703, Maryland 8,718, Missouri 8,344), from a page that gives no source (snippet) [S-USCT]. The Official Records table (Series III, vol. 5) was downloaded but its OCR is unreadable. **Low.** Per 0026, these are men's choices, not a pool.
- **The Confederate response was a control problem dressed as defence.** The Twenty Negro Law's stated purpose was "the proper police of the country" [W-20]. Davis's proclamation of 1 August 1863 told soldiers the enemy meant to "incite servile insurrection" [RICH-1] p. 329, the government's way of naming emancipation; quote it only as the Confederate government's words, never in the game's own voice. At Macon in September 1864 Davis said Butler had published that "if we would consent to the exchange of negroes, all difficulties might be removed", and dismissed it [ROWLAND-6] p. 343; [I] the Confederate refusal to exchange Black soldiers on equal terms was the sticking point (CS 4.4). **High** for the texts.
- **Arming enslaved men and offering emancipation for recognition.** The March 1865 law enlisted men only with their enslavers' consent and none was accepted [CS:EV-BlackConf]. Davis sent Duncan Kenner to Britain and France in 1864 to offer emancipation in exchange for recognition [W-KENNER]. [I] Both came when the war was lost and both kept slavery's terms in planters' hands; neither shows a Confederacy turning against slavery (see the gate note on Beringer, section 14).

## 12. Union occupation, emancipation and foreign hopes

- **Occupation had three different faces.** Stephen Ash's synthesis divides the occupied South into "Three Worlds": garrisoned towns, the Confederate frontier, and a no-man's-land between them; southerners' experience depended on their distance from a garrison (snippets of the book's contents and publisher's description) [S-ASH]. **Low** (book not read). [I] This maps onto the regional loyalty and control model in 0019: a region's will term should depend on whether it is garrisoned, contested or behind the lines, not only on the control share.
- **Occupation emptied the treasury's map.** Louisiana paid $200,000 and Tennessee $140,000 of $82 million in taxes by April 1864 [SCHWAB] p. 293; the 1863 elections took six months because of occupied districts [W-1863]. **High** for the figures.
- **Emancipation hardened as well as weakened.** The Twenty Negro Law answered the preliminary Proclamation within 19 days [W-20]; on 6 February 1865, recording both the peace commissioners' return and the House's passage of the Thirteenth Amendment, Jones expected that "WAR will rage with greater fury than ever" [JONES-2]. [I] So emancipation's effect on Confederate will has two signs: it hardened the slaveholding core in the short term and drained labour and men from below over the long term. **Medium.**
- **Foreign recognition.** The British mediation plan of September–October 1862 died after Antietam, and the Cabinet rejected France's armistice proposal in November 1862 [CS:ADAMS-XI]. The bond markets put the Confederacy's odds at about 42 percent before July 1863 and falling after (NBER abstract) [S-WO]. British customs seized the two Laird ironclads building for the Confederacy at Liverpool on 9 October 1863 [W-LAIRD]. The Kenner mission of 1864 was the last bid [W-KENNER]. James Mason's withdrawal from London (1863) was asked for and not verified here: UNVERIFIED. **Medium** for 1862 and the rams, **low** after.

## 13. Confederate will in 1864–65

- **A partial recovery in the first half of 1864.** The gold dollar eased from 23 (February) to 17 (June) under the Funding Act; Schwab also credits the North Carolina "war party" victory, Grant's and Sherman's slow progress and McClellan's nomination [SCHWAB] pp. 167, 171. In August Vance beat Holden about four to one [W-NC64]. Markets abroad did not share the hope: the later Amsterdam series falls from under 20 percent to under 3 percent across 1864 (snippet) [S-WO]. **Medium.**
- **Atlanta and the election broke it a second time.** Atlanta was "a stunning blow" [JONES-2] (5 Sep 1864); by 11 November Jones judged that "Lincoln has been re-elected" and that "All our physical power must be exerted, else all is lost" [JONES-2]; after the re-election the gold premium rose "more rapidly than at any other time during the war" [SCHWAB] p. 171. Davis's Macon admission that two-thirds of the army was absent came between the two [ROWLAND-6] p. 343. **High.**
- **Sherman aimed at civilian morale.** The march ran from Atlanta on 15 November to Savannah on 21 December 1864; Sherman expected foraging to have "a destructive effect on the morale of the civilian population" [W-SHERMAN]. That it drove Georgia and Carolina soldiers to desert is widely said and was not verified here: UNVERIFIED. **Medium** for the dates and intent.
- **Winter 1865: resolve flickers, cohesion goes.** Richmond flour went from $500 to $1,250 in January [JONES-2]; Stephens asked the Senate for negotiations on 6 January [W-HR]; Hampton Roads failed on 3 February and Davis rallied a crowd on the 6th [W-HR], [JONES-2]; within three weeks Lee was reporting hundreds of desertions a fortnight from one corps, driven by letters from home, and the Bureau of Conscription counted over 100,000 deserters [OR-I-47-2] pp. 1270–71, [OR-IV-3] pp. 1119–20. North Carolina's peace press kept urging submission after Hampton Roads [OR-I-47-2] p. 1251. **High.**
- **The end came through the armies.** Lee's army surrendered at Appomattox on 9 April and Johnston's at Bennett Place on 26 April (agent) [W-ANV], [W-BENNETT]. [I] For 0028 this is the key fact: the Confederate government did not sue for peace; its armies were destroyed, cornered or dissolved while their men went home.

**Lost Cause framings to flag (gate).**
1. **"Overwhelming numbers and resources."** Lee's farewell order of 10 April 1865 told his army it had been "compelled to yield to overwhelming numbers and resources" (snippet; Wikipedia's Lost Cause article gives the phrase as "overwhelming resources and numbers") [S-GO9], [W-LOST]. Jubal Early's Southern Historical Society articles of the 1870s and Davis's *Rise and Fall* (1881) built the Lost Cause on it [W-LOST]. Union superiority in men and matériel is a fact; the trap is using it to say the cause was noble and could only be beaten by brute force. The game may show the numbers; it must not use them as a eulogy.
2. **"Died of state rights."** Owsley's thesis is a scholar's argument, but it borrows the Lost Cause's vocabulary (his own phrase is "lost cause") and invites the claim that states' rights, not slavery, defined the Confederacy. Pollard's *The Lost Cause* (1866) made states' rights the cause of the war [W-LOST]. The governors' obstruction was real and is modelled; its framing in text stays as friction inside a slaveholding republic (CS 3.5).
3. **"The Confederacy was moving to end slavery."** Beringer and colleagues read the March 1865 law as a step toward emancipation; Carlton's review calls this wrong, mistaking pious penitence over being bad masters for "repentance that they had been masters at all" [W-WSL]. No enslaved man was accepted under that law [CS:EV-BlackConf]. The game must not imply the Confederacy would have freed anyone.
4. **"Loyal slaves" and the women's sacrifice story.** The "faithful slave" story is Lost Cause propaganda (a first monument in South Carolina in 1896, then the United Daughters of the Confederacy's campaign from 1904) [W-LOST]; Faust's essay attacks the matching myth of Confederate women's unflinching loyalty, which began as wartime propaganda [S-FAUST]. The bread riots and Lee's letter on letters from home are the record.
5. **Rally-round-Lee.** Gallagher's own emphasis on Lee's army as the nation's centre is a historian's finding; the CS note already warns against making Lee the Confederacy's moral centre (CS 4.6).

## Design implications

Everything here is [I] unless it carries a citation.

### What the Confederate will is, for the game

1. **The will of the Confederacy's white political community and its army, not of everyone inside its borders.** Enslaved people were not part of it; their actions are a separate force that lowers Confederate capacity (labour, supply, men) and raises Union strength. Counting them inside the will would make their freedom a Confederate morale problem, which is the Confederate government's own framing ("servile insurrection") and fails the gate.
2. **Terms, not one number** (as the Union note proposed for the Union will). The Confederate will is derived in `politics`, recomputed from terms owned elsewhere, never saved (hard rule 5), and each term carries a typed reason (hard rule 6).
3. **Two layers that historians keep apart: resolve and cohesion.** Resolve is the home front's willingness (blocs, prices, food, elections, peace activity). Cohesion is the army's presence (desertion and absence). Gallagher's and Beringer's camps disagree about what failed first, and the game should let either happen: a run where both hold until the armies are beaten in the field (Gallagher's reading) and a run where letters from home empty the regiments first (Lee's February 1865 letter; the internal-collapse reading) should both be possible.
4. **Outside expectations are a third thing.** The bond markets wrote the Confederacy off after July 1863 (NBER abstract) [S-WO] while Richmond rallied in February 1865 [JONES-2]. Keep the foreign view in the foreign-pressure clocks, not in the will.
5. **How the Confederate will breaks.** Historically the war ended with armies surrendered or dispersed while desertion was emptying them. So 0028's "Confederate will breaks" should resolve when either (a) cohesion in the main armies falls below a threshold, or (b) resolve falls below a threshold after the government has lost its capital or its main army; the verdict reports which terms did it. This keeps 0028 and avoids taking a side in section 14.

### What the Union player can plausibly move

Through people, as hard rule 1 requires: generals who win, take cities and garrison regions; a navy whose blockade raises prices and closes ports; policy on emancipation and Black enlistment (as policy replies, under 0026); terms offered at peace talks; support for Unionist regions; amnesty and reconstruction terms (Lincoln's December 1863 proclamation was not researched here). Not levers: Confederate prices directly (counterfeiting was Samuel Upham's private trade [CS:WIKI-Fin], and a Union counterfeiting verb is not recommended), desertion directly, riots.

5. **Union amnesty and reconstruction policy** (the December 1863 proclamation, loyalty oaths in occupied regions) is the Union player's main direct handle on Confederate will and has no research note yet.
6. **The situations catalogue (`docs/design/SITUATIONS-1861-62.md`)** has East Tennessee only as a Union goal (S54). The bridge burnings of 8 November 1861 and the Confederate hangings that followed fit its format as a narrated, gate-placed situation inside the first slice.
