# Research: the war's scale, 1861–65 (organisation, division size, named people, territory, order-of-battle sources)

Date: 2026-11-14. Status: research note (not canon). Written by a research lane for the lead, to size data, performance and the user interface for the settled design (divisions are the smallest unit that moves between commanders; corps are posts holding people; named people plus an officer pool; a 40 ms day tick).

**Conventions.**
- Every claim ends with a citation key in [brackets] that resolves to a URL in the Sources list, or with an inline URL. **(fetched)** = this lane read the page or file. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result summary; treat it as a lead, not a fact.
- **[I]** = our inference or arithmetic from the cited numbers. **[gk]** = general knowledge, unverified.
- "Aggregate" means present plus absent (the muster rolls). "Present for duty" (pfd) excludes the sick and the arrested but includes teamsters, musicians and hospital staff. "Effectives" are the men who could fight: Livermore found them to be 89–93% of pfd in infantry and 83–86% in cavalry [LIV p68].
- The Official Records were read only through transcriptions of single pages (civilwar.com) and through Livermore's and Dyer's digests. The printed OR organisation tables were not read directly.

**Not reachable by this lane.** The Dyer pages on generalstaff.org, americancivilwarhighcommand.com, civilwartalk.com and the armyheritage.org marching-rate PDF all returned 403, 404 or 410. Dyer and Livermore were read instead from the archive.org scans of the 1908 and 1900 printings (public domain). Fox, Phisterer, Heitman and the Quartermaster General's 1887 *Commanders of Army Corps, Divisions and Brigades* were not read. Eicher and Warner were read only through Wikipedia's summaries of them.

**Does the evidence contradict the settled design?** No. Two refinements, both in section 6: division strength shrinks about threefold over the war, so a division cannot be a fixed-size token; and the mid-1861 armies were organised in brigades, so the 1861 start needs a synthetic division layer.

## 1. Organisation over time

### 1.1 Total strength

**Union aggregate on the rolls, Livermore's table (the only date-by-date series):**

| Date | Union aggregate | Union "present" (snippet) | Confederate aggregate on returns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jul 1861 | 186,751 | | 112,040 |
| Jan 1862 | 575,917 | 527,204 | 351,418 |
| 31 Mar / Apr 1862 | 637,126 | | 401,395 |
| 1 Jan 1863 | 918,121 | 698,802 | 446,622 |
| 1 Jan 1864 | 860,737 | 611,250 | 481,160 |
| 1 Jan 1865 | 959,460 | 657,747 on 31 Mar 1865 (aggregate then 980,086 [I]) | 445,203 |

- The aggregate columns are Livermore's p47 table, "Comparison of the foregoing numbers with the number on the Union rolls at the same dates" [LIV p47] (fetched). The OCR of that table is jumbled but the cross-checks hold: the Confederate Jan 1864 figure, 481,160, is also the sum of Livermore's own department list on p46.
- The "present" column and the 1 May 1865 total of 1,000,516 come from a search-engine summary of a reprint of the Provost Marshal General's report (snippet; the exact URL among the results was not identified). It reconciles with Livermore: 527,204 present plus 48,713 absent gives 575,917 for Jan 1862, and 611,250 plus 249,487 gives 860,737 for Jan 1864 [I].
- The share of the rolls actually present falls from about 92% (Jan 1862) to 76% (Jan 1863), 71% (Jan 1864) and 67% (31 Mar 1865) [I] from the snippet figures. Absence (sickness, furlough, desertion, hospitals) is a large and growing part of the Union rolls.
- Livermore tabulates only January and one spring date, so mid-year figures are not in his series. For single campaigns he gives effectives: Union 91,169 and Confederate 95,481 at the Seven Days [LIV p86]; 101,895 Union at the Wilderness [LIV p110].
- **Uncertainty.** Livermore says his Confederate totals are probably below the real numbers, because returns are missing and some are estimated from "present" figures [LIV p41–42]. Wikipedia quotes the War Department's own tallies on a different basis: 326,768 (end 1861), 449,439 (1862), 464,646 (1863, the peak), 400,787 (1864), 358,692 at the last reports [WP-CSA]. A Jan 1863 Union figure of 918,191 circulates against Livermore's 918,121; the difference is probably a typo in one source [I]. Confederate numbers carry a 10–20% error band.

- Dyer's department pages list about 145 commander tenures held by about 75 distinct men (OCR count of the pages, p254–259, so a floor) [DYER].
- Wikipedia's list, sourced to Eicher and Eicher, counts 9 military divisions, about 40–45 departments and about 60 districts over the whole war [WP-DEPTS] (fetched).

**Union field armies.** Wikipedia lists 17 field armies over the war [WP-UA]. The concurrent number is about 4 in mid-1861 and about 7–8 from 1863 (Potomac, Cumberland, Tennessee, Ohio, Gulf, plus the James and the Shenandoah in 1864 and the Georgia army in 1865) [I]. An army held 1 to 8 corps, averaging three [WP-UA] (fetched).

**Union corps.**
- Corps did not exist before March 1862: the President created the First to Fourth Corps on 3 March 1862, announced 13 March [DYER p280ff] (fetched). The first slice therefore starts in a war with no corps, then gains them.
- Dyer's index shows the 25 numbered corps, four separate "Cavalry Corps" entries (Potomac, Cumberland, the Mississippi division, West Mississippi), a Reserve Corps and Provisional corps, so about 30 over the war [DYER index p260–269] (fetched). Wikipedia says 26 (25 plus Cavalry) [WP-UA]. Encyclopedia Virginia says "forty-three altogether", which conflicts with both [EV-ORG]; Dyer is the more specific source.
- Concurrent corps, counted from Dyer's creation dates and the OOBs: about 0 until March 1862, then 8–11 by July 1862 (II–VI Potomac, I–III Army of Virginia, and VII, VIII and IX created 22 Jul 1862), about 18–20 in January 1863, about 22–24 in mid-1863, and about 18–20 from 1864 [I].
- Corps commands turn over fast. One reader's tally from the Quartermaster General's data and Dyer puts the average tenure of an eastern corps commander at 5.2 months (4.3 months excluding the VII and VIII Corps), and the First Corps of the Army of the Potomac had seven commanders over 10–12 tenures in two years [CWBN-1], [CWBN-2] (fetched; blog, the author's rough data).

**Confederate commands.**
- Livermore's returns name these commands: 10 (July 1861), 19 (Jan 1862), 14 (Apr 1862), 12 (Jan 1863), 12 (Jan 1864), 8 (Jan 1865) [LIV p41–46] (fetched; my counts of his lists).
- Corps were not legal until the Confederate Congress created them in September 1862 (snippet) [FANDOM-CSA]. The Army of Northern Virginia fought Antietam in two wings, and Jackson and Longstreet became lieutenant generals afterward [NPS-STRUCT] (fetched). Wikipedia lists 24 named Confederate armies [WP-CSA].
- The Confederates had about 18 lieutenant generals (21 by another count) and 7 full generals (snippet) [FANDOM-CSA], [WP-CSA].

## 2. Division size and nesting

### 2.1 Nesting

- Paper strengths: company 100, regiment 10 companies (about 1,000), brigade 2–12 regiments (4 typical), division 2–6 brigades (3 typical for infantry, 2 for cavalry), corps 2–6 divisions [WP-UA] (fetched). The NPS gives brigade 2–6 regiments (most often 4), division 3–5 brigades, corps 2–4 divisions (most often 3) [NPS-STRUCT] (fetched).
- Confederate divisions "tended to contain more brigades than their Union counterparts", with about twice the men; a Confederate division was often "nearly the same size as a Union corps" [NPS-STRUCT].
- Wikipedia's rough Confederate wartime sizes: corps 24,000–28,000; division 6,000–14,000; brigade 800–1,700; regiment 350–400; the Army of Northern Virginia's regiments averaged 409 at Chancellorsville [WP-CSA] (fetched).
- Livermore's Union average regimental strength, present for duty: about 560 at Shiloh, 650 at Fair Oaks (spring 1862), 530 at Chancellorsville, 375 at Gettysburg, 440 at Chickamauga (September 1863), 440 at the Wilderness, and 305 in Sherman's army in May 1864 [LIV p67–68] (fetched).
- Regiment totals: about 2,047 Union regiments, of them 1,696 infantry (snippet) [PHIST-SNIP]; about 1,010 Confederate regiment-equivalents [WP-CSA].
- Dyer's index to the departments and armies lists about 370 "Division" entries (159 eastern, 214 western, about 20 of them cavalry) and about 1,430 "Brigade" entries (658 and 775) across the war. These are OCR line counts, and the same formation is counted again after each reorganisation [DYER index p260–269] [I].

### 2.2 Strength over the war

| Date | Union per division (pfd or effectives) | Confederate per division |
|---|---|---|
| 31 Mar 1862 | about 11,000 (I–IV, V Corps at 11,350) [OR-XI-3-p53] | |
| Spring 1862, Shiloh | about 6,000–7,000 | |
| Jul 1862 | about 7,600 | about 9,500 infantry |
| Sep 1862 | about 4,600 | about 5,200 |
| Jul 1863 | about 3,800 infantry (2,476–5,260); about 3,500 cavalry | about 6,600 infantry |
| Sep 1863 | about 4,200 | about 4,400 |
| May 1864, East | about 6,350 infantry; about 4,300 cavalry | about 6,100 (4,960–7,915) |
| May 1864, West | about 4,700 | about 3,800–5,000 |
| Nov 1864 | about 4,400 infantry | |

- **The pattern.** Union divisions fall threefold between March 1862 and July 1863. Regiments shrank only about 1.7 times (650 to 375), so the larger part of the drop comes from how the divisions were built: the Union kept raising new regiments rather than refilling old ones, and divisions held fewer regiments [I]. The Army of the Potomac's divisions grew again in spring 1864 (the II Corps held four divisions of 7,000 or so) as heavy-artillery regiments joined as infantry [I]. The range of a Union division is therefore about 2,500–11,000, and of a Confederate division about 3,800–9,500.
- **Do not model a division as a fixed size.** Both sides' divisions are best stored as a strength with a range, with Confederate divisions typically larger in the East.

- Eicher and Eicher found that about 25% of Union army rankings and 15% of Confederate rankings fail the rank guidelines, and Warner's lists include about 25 names per side that do not qualify (snippet via search results) [WP-GENS]. Use a range, not a single number.
- **Army and department commanders.** About 75 distinct men appear in Dyer's list of department and military-division commanders (about 145 tenures) [DYER p254–259]. Union army commanders overlap with these.
- **Corps commanders.** No source read gives a total. Counting about 30 corps and an average tenure of 4–5 months, with each corps lasting two to three years, suggests about 250 tenures and about 100–120 distinct Union men [I]. For the Confederates, with about 18 lieutenant generals plus major generals commanding corps temporarily, about 25–30 [I]. This needs a tally from Eicher or the Quartermaster General's 1887 volume (gap).
- **Brigade level and above.** Dyer's alphabetical index of commanders (p46–112) yields at least 1,372 distinct "Surname, Initials" strings after OCR de-duplication (a floor), including colonels who briefly led a brigade [DYER p46–112] (fetched).

### 3.2 Politicians

- **Union cabinet:** 13 men beside the President on Wikipedia's table: Seward, Chase, Fessenden, McCulloch, Cameron, Stanton, Bates, Speed, Blair, Dennison, Welles, Smith, Usher [WP-CAB-U] (fetched). The table omits James Harlan (Interior, 1865) [gk].
- **Confederate cabinet:** 16 men in six posts beside Davis and Stephens (Secretary of War 5, State 4, Treasury 3, Attorney General 5 with Wade Keyes serving twice) [WP-CAB-C] (fetched).
- **Governors:** 59 men governed the 25 Union states (book description, snippet) [HARRIS-SNIP].
- **Congress:** the 37th Congress had 50 Senate seats and 183 House seats plus 7 delegates [WP-37C] (fetched). The Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War had 11 members over its two Congresses (6 senators including Wade, Chandler and Andrew Johnson; 5 representatives including Julian and Covode) [WP-JCCW] (fetched). The Confederate Congress had 26 Senate and about 105–109 House seats, and 267 men served [WP-CSCONG] (fetched).

### 3.3 A sensible roster size [I]

- **Whole war, named people:** about 450.
  - Union generals: about 250 (every army, department and corps commander, plus the 120 or so division commanders who matter).
  - Confederate generals: about 100 (18 lieutenant generals, 7 generals, the corps and army commanders, and the notable divisions).
  - Politicians: about 70 Union (13 cabinet, about 25 governors who matter, about 25 congressional figures including the 11 on the Joint Committee, plus the press and finance names) and about 30 Confederate (16 cabinet, Davis, Stephens, about 10 governors and congressmen).
- **First slice (Union 1861–62):** about 150–180. The existing cast in `2026-11-14-union-1861-62.md` is 18 people plus governors and Radicals; Dyer's 1861–62 department and division commanders add about 60, the Confederate opponents about 40.
- **Alive and acting at one time:** about 150 field posts (8 armies, 22 departments, 24 corps, 100 divisions) for the Union and about 80 for the Confederates, plus about 65 Union and about 25 Confederate politicians. So about 220 named people need Union-side logic on a given day, and about 330 across both sides.
- **Officer pool:** below general rank, brigade and regiment commanders are too many to name. The war had about 2,050 Union and about 1,010 Confederate regiments, and at least 1,372 brigade-level names appear in Dyer. Generate colonels lazily from a seeded stream when a report or an observer mentions one, and persist only those that surface. The pool never needs to exist whole [I].

## 4. Territory: distances, marching days, rail

**Marching.**
- A search summary of an Army Heritage Center bibliography quotes an 1862 officers' pocket companion: 15–20 miles a day on route marches, 20–25 (perhaps 30) forced, infantry columns 2.5 mph and cavalry 6 mph (snippet; the PDF could not be fetched) [MARCH-SNIP]. The same summary says the 12–15 miles a day for a division is a modern doctrinal figure, not a Civil War one.
- **Observed pace is much slower.** Sherman's March to the Sea covered about 300 miles from 15 November to 21 December 1864, about 37 days, or about 8 miles a day averaged over the whole march [WP-SHERMAN] (fetched; the daily figure is my division [I]).
- **Contested ground slower still.** The first phase of the Atlanta campaign ran 7 May–17 July 1864 over a line 138 rail miles long [WP-ATL-U], [WP-WAR] (fetched); most of that line was covered in about ten weeks, or about 2 miles a day while in contact [I].

**Distances.**
- Chattanooga to Atlanta, Western and Atlantic Railroad: 138 miles [WP-WAR] (fetched).
- Nashville to Chattanooga: about 150 miles [WP-NCRR] (fetched). Louisville to Nashville: "180-odd miles" [WP-LNRR] (fetched).
- Washington to Richmond: about 106 road miles (low-quality travel pages, snippet) [DIST-SNIP].
- Sherman's March: about 300 miles [WP-SHERMAN].

**Rail.**
- 1861 mileage: about 22,000 in the North and 9,500 in the South [BF-RAIL] (fetched); other sources give 21,300 or 22,385 for the North and 9,022 or 8,783 for the South (snippets) [RAIL-SNIP].
- Gauges ran from four to six feet. Southern locomotives averaged 25 mph in 1861 and about 10 mph by 1863, with a quarter of them needing repair [BF-RAIL].
- **The one documented long transfer.** The XI and XII Corps, about 20,000 men, moved from Virginia to Bridgeport, Alabama, 1,157–1,233 miles, on the orders given 25 September 1863. The first trains reached Bridgeport at 10:30 p.m. on 30 September, all the XI Corps's men were there by 3 October, and the movement of both corps with their artillery and baggage was complete by 12 October (snippet) [CWM-XIXII]. So a two-corps transfer took about 8 days for the men and about 17 for everything [I].

**What this means for edges and the tick [I].**
- A one-day tick matches a division's march of 12–20 miles a day. Coarse regions 40–70 miles across give road edges of 3–5 days, and contested edges more.
- A rail edge is about 150 miles a day in effect (the XI and XII Corps) rather than the theoretical 250–400, and capacity is the real limit, so model rail edges at 1 day per 150 miles with a division-per-day capacity.
- The documented long transfer fits inside the tick without sub-day steps; only battles need hour-level resolution (FOUNDATION section 9).

## 6. Design implications

**Peak counts the engine must handle.**
- **Divisions in play at once:** Union about 85–100, Confederate about 40–50. Over the whole war about 370 Union division records and about 1,430 brigade records appear in Dyer; the records are the data to be sized, the concurrent counts are what the tick must process.
- **Corps:** about 20–24 Union, about 9–10 Confederate. **Armies:** 7–8 Union. **Departments and military divisions:** 21–22 Union.
- **Named people alive at once:** about 220 on the Union side, about 330 across both. Roster about 450 for the whole war, about 150–180 for the first slice (section 3.3).
- **Largest command a player ever sees:** about 24 divisions (Sherman's group, Atlanta), about 22–26 for the Army of the Potomac. A single army's division list fits one screen.

**Is "division" the right smallest unit for the transfer verb?**

Yes, with three changes.
1. **Keep division as the unit of account, not the unit the player picks.** About 100 divisions and about 1,430 brigades make a player who picks units one by one a rebuild of *No Greater Glory*'s order screens, where reassignment took about 60–80% of play (taken from the games survey [GAMES-NGG]; second-hand here). The verb should name the two ends (army A to army B, or department to department, about 22 destinations at most), the force wanted ("two divisions", "the XI Corps", "10,000 men"), and the commander at the sending end picks which. That also satisfies hard rule 1: no verb targets a unit.
2. **Let a corps move as a bundle.** The documented long transfers were corps: the XI and XII Corps (five divisions, about 20,000 men) in September–October 1863 [CWM-XIXII]. Dyer's corps headings record others, for example the IX Corps "transferred to the Department of the Ohio March 19, 1863" [DYER p313ff]. A corps shortcut turns five picks into one.
3. **Treat strength as data with a range.** A division runs from about 11,000 (March 1862) to about 3,800 (July 1863), so "send two divisions" moves 7,600 or 22,000 men depending on the date [I]. Show men and not only division counts in the interface, and let the sending commander resist because of what he loses in men, not in divisions.

**Contested edges.** Brigade-level shuffles inside an army are the commander's business (FOUNDATION: "inside an army, the commander assigns"); the evidence supports leaving them there, since there are about 1,430 brigade records against about 370 divisions.

**The 1861 start.** The Union's August 1861 Potomac force listed about 16 brigades with no division, and the Confederates at Bull Run fielded 12 brigades in two armies with no divisions [DYER index p272–274], [WP-BR-C]. The Phase 0.3 order of battle for mid-1861 therefore needs a stated convention (for example, group the brigades into synthetic divisions by Dyer's October 1861 formation, and mark them synthetic in provenance). The Union Army of the Potomac organised divisions in October 1861 (Dyer p274) and corps in March 1862, so the first slice passes through three regimes: brigades, divisions, corps.

**Performance budget per division per day [I].**
- The tick is 40 ms. With about 150 divisions in play for both sides, allocate about 20 ms to forces (about 130 µs per division-day), about 10 ms to people and politics (about 330 people at about 30 µs), and about 10 ms to messages, indexes and overhead.
- **Plan on 100 µs per division-day, all in.** That leaves headroom for 2x error in the division count. Most divisions sit in camp on a given day; only those marching or in contact need movement, supply and combat work.
- Modelling brigades (about 500–600 concurrent) would need about 25 µs per brigade-day. It is feasible but not needed, and it would multiply the data and the interface.
- Derived indexes stay on threshold crossings only (FOUNDATION section 9), which matters more than any per-division cost.

**Edges.** Express edge lengths in whole days at 12–15 miles a day, with 1 day minimum; typical road edges 3–5 days; rail at about 150 miles a day with capacity per day; sea lanes separately (section 4).

## Findings outside my scope

1. **Division strength falls threefold while regiments fall under twofold (the economy and men lanes).** Livermore's regimental averages (650 to 375) and the OOB division strengths (11,000 to 3,800) show that the Union created new regiments rather than refilling old ones. The "men" resource should flow into new divisions at low strength, and "refill" should be a separate, costly lever. Sources: [LIV p67–68], section 2.
2. **Command turnover is high, so appointments are the main repeated verb.** Corps commanders averaged four to five months in post, and the First Corps had seven commanders in two years. At about 250 corps tenures over the war, the promotion lists and "who commands the XI Corps" decisions arrive every few weeks. The staff's default recommendation must make most of them cheap [CWBN-1], [CWBN-2].
3. **Order-of-battle licensing needs the owner's call before data entry (0014).** Dyer, Livermore, the OR and the 1887 Quartermaster General volume are public domain; the Nafziger collection has no formal licence found; Wikipedia is CC BY-SA; Eicher and Warner are in copyright. Phase 0.3 should enter data from the public-domain stack only. See section 5.
4. **The present share of the Union rolls fell from 92% to 67%.** Absence (sick, furlough, desertion) grows with the war; the economy lane should model "men on the rolls" and "men present" separately, with effectives at 89–93% of present for infantry [LIV p68].
5. **Corps-count sources conflict.** Dyer's index shows about 30 corps-level entries; Wikipedia says 26; Encyclopedia Virginia says 43. Pack data must follow Dyer.
6. **The post model needs a tier above departments, and army and department are often one post.** Dyer records the Military Division of the Mississippi from 18 Oct 1863 (Grant, then Sherman), West Mississippi from 7 May 1864 and the Middle from 6 Aug 1864, each over several departments. It also lists combined entries such as "Department and Army of the Tennessee" and "Department of Virginia and North Carolina (Army of the James)", where one man held both. Posts (FOUNDATION section 5) should allow a military-division post, and treat army and department as one post with two names where Dyer does [DYER p254–259].

## Gaps and known conflicts (to research next)

- **Corps commander tally.** No source read gives the number of distinct men who commanded a Union or Confederate corps; the 100–120 and 25–30 figures are inferences. Tally from Eicher's lists or the 1887 Quartermaster General volume.
- **Inter-army transfer frequency.** How many corps and division transfers between armies and departments happened per year (Dyer's corps headings record them) was not tallied; this sets the real load of the transfer verb.
- **Confederate order of battle by date.** No clean source found (section 5).
- **Concurrent division counts** in section 1.4 beyond the anchored battles are estimates; a Dyer-based count by date would replace them.
- **Mid-year Union and Confederate aggregates** are missing from Livermore's series; OR abstracts for 30 June returns would fill them.
- **The Provost Marshal General figures** (present and absent) were read only through a search summary; confirm against the PMG report.
