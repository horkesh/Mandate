# Union politics, 1861–64: Congress, blocs, elections, the press and national will (Phase 1 research)

Status: first pass complete (2026-11-14), for Phase 1 (blocs, mandate, national will, Congress, elections, the press; decisions 0023, 0026, 0028, 0032). Detail is heaviest for 1861–62. It does not repeat the cast and posts in `2026-11-14-union-1861-62.md` or the secession-winter and border-state material in `2026-11-14-secession-winter-1861.md`; it builds on their bloc table (section 4 of the first) and corrects it where noted.

Conventions: citation keys in [brackets] resolve in the Sources table at the end. **(snippet)** = seen only in a search-result summary, page not read. **[I]** = our interpretation, never a source's claim. **UNVERIFIED** = asked for and no source found. Where sources disagree, both values are given. Most pages are secondary (Wikipedia, essay sites, blogs); Project MUSE and senate.gov returned 403 or a verification wall, so several scholarly claims come second-hand. The one primary-style quantitative source read in full is the Willard, Guinnane and Rosen greenback paper (AER 1996). No opinion polls exist for the period; the section 2 list says what stands in for them.

### 1.3 The Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War (JCCW)

- **Created** 9 December 1861 (Chandler's resolution was introduced 5 December), after Ball's Bluff on 21 October [WIKI-JCCW], [ECWC-JCCW] (snippet for the 5 December date).
- **Members, 37th Congress:** Senate: Wade (R-OH, chair), Chandler (R-MI), Andrew Johnson (D-TN), later Joseph Wright (D-IN). House: Julian (R-IN), Covode (R-PA), Gooch (R-MA), Odell (D-NY). That is five Republicans and two Democrats [I, counted from the names] [WIKI-JCCW]. **Conflict:** the ECWC essay says Harding replaced Johnson and Loan replaced Covode in January 1864; Wikipedia says Wright replaced Johnson and Harding (D-OR) and Buckalew (D-PA) sat in the 38th [ECWC-JCCW], [WIKI-JCCW].
- **Method:** closed sessions, often without a quorum, witnesses mostly officers, subpoenas, field trips (Wade and Gooch to Fort Pillow, 1864), irregular publication, leaks to the press; no public hearings (senate.gov, snippet) [WIKI-JCCW], [ECWC-JCCW], [SEN-JCCW] (snippet).
- **Effects on generals:**
  - Charles Stone: scapegoated for Ball's Bluff; arrested 8 February 1862 (9 and 12 February in other sources); held to 14 August; never tried [ECWC-JCCW], [SEN-JCCW] (snippet for the date conflict).
  - McClellan: members pressed Lincoln at a cabinet meeting on 6 January 1862, and Wade had a stormy interview with him in early March; the committee later blamed McClellan for Pope's defeat. Stanton, who began as McClellan's ally, soon took the committee's view [ECWC-JCCW].
  - Burnside and Hooker: the committee suspected McClellan's friends of sabotaging Burnside at Fredericksburg and blamed Franklin; in 1864 it tried to rehabilitate Hooker [ECWC-JCCW].
  - Meade: after Gettysburg, Sickles's testimony led to a near two-month inquiry in early 1864 that failed to remove him; Wade edited the report against Meade [WIKI-JCCW], [ECWC-JCCW].
  - Pattern: the committee favoured anti-slavery generals and distrusted West Pointers [ECWC-JCCW]; officers who were not Republicans felt the most pressure [WIKI-JCCW].
- **Did it constrain Lincoln?** The ECWC essay calls it "a distraction more than a real constraint" (Lincoln kept Meade) but notes it helped win Frémont a new command in 1862. Tap (via Wikipedia) says its secret testimony and leaks fed hostility between West Pointers and civilians [ECWC-JCCW], [WIKI-JCCW].
- **After the war:** final testimony (Sherman) 22 May 1865. The 1864 report on Fort Pillow and the 1865 report on Sand Creek are in the sensitive-history gate (see Findings outside my scope) [WIKI-JCCW].

## 2. Public opinion: the groups that mattered and what measures them

**Measures that exist.** No polls. What a designer can anchor to: election returns (House seats, state-level results, soldier-vote tallies), newspaper alignment and circulation, the gold premium as a market view of Union fortunes, enlistment and draft numbers, army present-versus-absent figures, desertion counts, and a handful of contemporary summaries (Raymond, Weed, Welles). The earlier note found no estimate of how many Northerners would have let the South go in winter 1860–61.

### 2.2 Casualties, the draft and enlistment as pressure

- **Army size** (aggregate present and absent, from the War Department table): 575,917 on 1 Jan 1862; 918,191 on 1 Jan 1863; 860,737 on 1 Jan 1864; 959,460 on 1 Jan 1865; 1,000,516 on 1 May 1865 [STRENGTH]. The absent share rose from about 8.5 percent (Jan 1862) to 24 (Jan 1863), 29 (Jan 1864) and 35 (Jan 1865) [I, computed from the same table].
- **Calls for volunteers:** 75,000 for three months (15 Apr 1861); 42,034 more for three years (3 May 1861, snippet); 300,000 (1 Jul 1862); a second 300,000 as nine-month militia under the Militia Act (Aug 1862); 300,000 (17 Oct 1863); 500,000 (18 Jul 1864); 300,000 (19 Dec 1864) [UCSB-CALLS] (snippet; the August 1862 and February–March 1864 calls were not confirmed).
- **Credits:** 2,656,533 credited to the army, navy and marine corps, April 1861 to April 1865, with a deficiency of 102,496 (snippet from the Official Records) [OR-CREDITS] (snippet).
- **The draft in numbers:** the July 1863 draft produced 35,882 "men furnished" (which includes substitutes) and 52,288 commutation payments; February–March 1864 12,303 and 32,678; July 1864 84,291 and 1,298; December 1864 36,173 and 460; totals 168,649 furnished and 86,724 paid commutation [OR-DRAFT]. In the July 1863 draft, commutation payers outnumbered men furnished (52,288 of 88,170, 59 percent) [I, computed]. By the page's own phrasing the "furnished" column includes substitutes, so the true number actually drafted and serving is smaller; about 170,000 drafted in all, 120,000 of whom hired substitutes is the other common figure [EBSCO-DRAFT]. Either way the draft's main effect was to induce volunteering [EBSCO-DRAFT].
- **Desertion:** a War Department chart gives annual rates of 13.7 percent (1861), 16.0 (1862), 16.0 (1863), 28.0 (1864) and 45.0 (1865), with no stated method; another note puts mid-1863 at about 0.6 percent a month, 7–8 percent a year (both snippet). They conflict on base; use as a rising band only [DESERT] (snippet).
- **Overland and Atlanta casualties:** McPherson: the two main armies suffered "a combined 95,000 casualties" in front of Richmond and Atlanta, and the Army of the Potomac lost nearly two-thirds in 5 May–4 Jul 1864 of its three-year total [AHA-MCP].

## 3. Elections

### 3.1 1862

- **Dates:** Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana and Iowa voted on 14 October; New York, New Jersey, Illinois and others on 4 November; elections were rolling from October to November [GRATZ-62], [WIKI-62H].
- **House** (see 1.1): Democratic gain 22–30 seats; Republicans lost their outright majority and governed with Unionists; Senate Republicans gained three.
- **State results by seats** (one page's table): Ohio Democrats 14 (+6), Republican–Union 5 (−8); Indiana 7 (+3) and 4 (−3); Pennsylvania 12 (+6) and 12 (−7); New York 17 (+7) and 14 (−9) [WIKI-62H].
- **Governors and legislatures:** Seymour (D) beat Wadsworth (National Union) 306,649 to 295,897 (50.9 to 49.1 percent) in New York, a margin of about 10,750 of 602,500 [WIKI-NY62]. Democrats took New Jersey and flipped four lower-North legislatures including Indiana and Illinois (earlier dossier). Illinois, Lincoln's own state, was lost [PINSKER]. Speaker Grow lost his seat [PINSKER].
- **Causes, as historians and contemporaries give them:** slow war, inflation, new taxes, corruption talk, habeas suspension, the draft law, fear of freed Black labour, racist campaigning over the Proclamation, and inconclusive battles (Antietam, Corinth, Perryville) [GRATZ-62], [WIKI-62H]. Lincoln named "ill-success" and said soldiers away from the polls cost votes; Schurz blamed leniency to Democrats and disloyal officers; Lincoln's answer was that he "need[ed] success more than sympathy" [PINSKER]. A Civil War History article argues soldier withdrawals skewed the result against Republicans, since the men taken out were mostly Republicans (snippet) [CWH-62]. In New York the Times reported that about 17,000 city residents swore they were aliens to escape the draft, so Democratic turnout fell [GRATZ-62].
- **Historians' verdict:** contemporaries called it a "severe reproof", historians usually call it inconclusive, and McPherson is quoted as calling it the smallest net loss of House seats in twenty years (earlier note's second-hand citation; not re-verified here). Pinsker argues the cycle was the birth of the Union party [PINSKER]. **The verdict is contested; the game should land in a band, not on a point.**

Historians' explanation: Gettysburg and Vicksburg made the war look winnable; John Morgan's July raid through Indiana and Ohio rallied voters; Vallandigham's arrest, conviction and banishment turned him into a martyr and then a loser; Brough and Charles Anderson had been seen as underdogs weeks before. The "Ohio has saved the Union" quotation is widely repeated and the telegram cannot be found in Lincoln's correspondence [ECW-OH63]. Gideon Welles judged Vallandigham's defeat "emphatic" [CWM-63]. The pattern (Democrats at 39 to 49 percent even in a good year for the Union ticket) is the Democratic floor for the model.

### 3.3 1864

- **Result:** Lincoln and Johnson 212 electoral votes and 55.0 percent (2,218,388); McClellan and Pendleton 21 and 45.0 percent (1,812,807); McClellan carried Kentucky, Delaware and New Jersey; 22 states for Lincoln [WIKI-1864]. Totals differ by source: 2,223,035 and 1,811,754 [CWMON-SV]; 2,220,846 and 1,809,445 [ECW-SV]. Use 55.0 percent and about 4.03 million ballots.
- **Narrow states:** New York by 0.92 percent (6,749 votes), Connecticut 2.76, Pennsylvania 3.50, Delaware 3.62, New Hampshire 5.12, New Jersey 5.68, Indiana 7.19, Michigan 7.20, Oregon 7.8; Illinois (8.8) was the tipping-point state [WIKI-1864].
- **The summer low:** casualties above; Early's raid reached within five miles of the White House on 11 July and retreated on 12 July [WILLARD]; Lincoln's 18 July call for 500,000 men with a draft to cover shortfalls [AHA-MCP]; the Wade–Davis Manifesto in early August [WIKI-WD]; Thurlow Weed reported that "the people are wild for peace" [AHA-MCP], and a search summary of the Library of Congress guide says he told Lincoln in mid-August that re-election "was an impossibility" (snippet; the page as fetched does not contain it); Raymond's report of 22 August said Lincoln would lose New York, Pennsylvania and Illinois and "might lose every state," blamed military losses and a belief that no peace would come "until Slavery is abandoned," and advised offering Davis terms on the Constitution alone; Lincoln drafted an authorising memo on 24 August and rejected it within about a day, saying a commission to Richmond "would be worse than losing the Presidential contest" [HD-BLIND]. On 23 August Lincoln wrote and sealed the "Blind Memorandum" saying it was "exceedingly probable" the administration would not be re-elected and resolving to co-operate with the president-elect to save the Union before the inauguration [LOC-BLIND]. The Democratic convention platform (31 August) called for a cessation of hostilities; McPherson notes almost everyone understood an armistice request as admitting defeat; the New York World called the war "a failure" [AHA-MCP].
- **Atlanta:** Sherman's telegram of 3 September ("Atlanta is ours, and fairly won") reversed morale in North and South; Strong called it "the greatest event of the war" [AHA-MCP]. The Library of Congress guide gives three causes of Lincoln's win: the brighter military outlook after Atlanta, Lincoln's maneuvering and a reinvigorated party, and Democratic weakness (the platform and the peace-leaning running mate) [LOC-BLIND]. Frémont and Cochrane withdrew in September [WIKI-1864]. **The greenback market did not show Atlanta as a break** (see 5.2); the Tribune did not endorse Lincoln until Atlanta fell [BF-TRIB]; Greeley had backed an August plot for a new convention [WIKI-GREELEY].
- **McClellan's position:** he accepted the nomination a week and a half after the convention with a letter that implicitly disavowed the peace plank and pledged to continue the war until the Union was restored (Burlingame, snippet) [CWM-MCC] (snippet). Peace Democrats were angry; Burlingame argues a clearer repudiation might have produced a third candidate (snippet). Steve Davis argues, following Randall and Donald, that McClellan would have prosecuted the war for reunion and Davis would have rejected a reunion-based peace [ECW-MCC]. This disagrees with the popular view; the game need not settle it (0026).
- **What "losing" would have looked like.** On the record: Lincoln's own memorandum (a duty to co-operate with the president-elect to save the Union before 4 March 1865), Raymond's advice, and a Democratic platform for an armistice with a nominee who half-repudiated it. The first cannot be turned into a play-out because 0026 says the months before 4 March 1865 are not played. A defensible game ending is the verdict "the electorate has rejected the war" and an opposition ticket with a general (a sacked or popular one) as its candidate, as 0026 already provides.

### 3.4 The soldier vote

- 19 states let soldiers vote in the field in 1864 (no state with a Democratic-controlled legislature did, per ECW-SV); 12 tallied those votes separately (one source says 11) [ECW-SV], [HN-WHITE].
- In the 12 separately tallied states, 78 percent of soldier votes went to Lincoln against 53 percent of the Republican civilian vote in those same states [ECW-SV]. About 150,000 field ballots, under 6 percent of all ballots; California soldiers 92 percent Lincoln, Kentucky soldiers about 70 percent McClellan; Ohio 50,903 field ballots; Connecticut, New York, Minnesota and West Virginia mailed ballots home, so their soldier totals cannot be separated; Vermont's were delayed [CWMON-SV].
- Wikipedia's 40,247 army votes (Lincoln 30,503, 75.8 percent, McClellan 9,201, 22.9 percent) covers only seven states [WIKI-1864]; **the two do not describe the same set**.
- Many soldiers also went home to vote on furlough; no count was kept of men voting in their home states [CWMON-SV] (snippet-level statement in the search summary; one unsourced estimate of about 710,000 soldiers voting in all is too weak to use).
- Stanton's "vote as they shot" furloughs for Pennsylvania soldiers and Lincoln's furloughs and rail passes for Ohio and Pennsylvania men in 1863 show the executive using leave as an election lever [CWM-63], [ECW-OH63]. The gate does not cover it, but opponents would call it manipulation.

### 4.2 How generals and politicians used the press

- Lee read McClellan's movements in Northern papers; the Philadelphia Inquirer's report of McClellan's positions led Lee to move troops; Hardee learned from the Tribune where Sherman's supply ships were gathering [MTSU-FA].
- Sherman called correspondents enemy spies and tried to court-martial one (acquitted, expelled beyond the lines) [ECWC-PRESS].
- Lincoln rewarded friendly papers with patronage and appointments, and supporters planted pieces by officials and by Lincoln himself [ECWC-PRESS].
- The Loyal Publication Society and the Union League Club (both New York, February 1863) printed and distributed pamphlets: about 900,000 copies of 90 pamphlets by Britannica's count, 400,000 in the first year by another; counts disagree (snippet) [LPS] (snippet).
- Information flows (leaks, wire control) are covered in the information-flows note; not repeated.

### 4.3 Censorship and suppression

- **Counts:** the ECWC essay gives 92 newspapers facing some restriction and 111 wrecked by mobs, mostly in the North [ECWC-PRESS]. The Dickinson page says dozens of anti-war papers were suppressed [HD-CIVIL].
- **Telegraph censorship** began in April 1861; in December 1861 the House Judiciary Committee said the government should not interfere with telegraph transmissions except where Congress authorised it [MTSU-FA].
- **The May 1864 gold hoax:** a forged presidential proclamation calling for a day of prayer and 400,000 more men appeared in the World and the Journal of Commerce on 18 May; gold jumped about 10 percent; Lincoln ordered Dix to arrest the editors and seize the offices (offices padlocked and staff held at Fort Lafayette on Seward's and Stanton's urging); Lincoln soon revoked the arrest of Marble and Prime; the real call for 500,000 followed on 18 July. Howard was arrested on 20 or 21 May and released in August (22 or 24 Aug) (snippet) [HOAX] (snippet), [MTSU-FA].
- **Arrests of civilians:** Greenberg: at least 350 arrested in the month after Stanton's 8 August 1862 order; between 10,000 and 15,000 held without a prompt trial [HNN-GREENBERG]. Neely himself uses "15,000 or more" in a 2018 essay (snippet) and an earlier review says "well over thirteen thousand" (OCR garbled, snippet) [NEELY] (snippet). Treat 13,000–15,000 as the range.
- Lincoln defended a hard line with the question of whether he must shoot a deserting boy and leave the agitator untouched; in 1864 he called elections a necessity [HD-CIVIL].

## 5. National will

### 5.1 How historians assess it

- **Two low points:** winter 1862–63 (between Fredericksburg and Chancellorsville morale "dropped to its lowest point except, perhaps, during the late summer of 1864") and August 1864, which McPherson calls the rock-bottom of Northern morale (snippet for the first, [AHA-MCP] for the second) [MORALE] (snippet).
- **A wavering will, not a collapsing one:** McPherson's account is of a will that wavered sharply in summer 1864 and rebounded with military success, tied to a war aim Lincoln would not give up. Lincoln's December 1864 message said Northern determination "was never more firm, nor more nearly unanimous" [AHA-MCP].
- **Rival emphasis:** Gallagher is summarised (in a Horwitz opinion piece, the only text seen, snippet) as arguing that the long emphasis on slavery and liberation distorts how Americans of the 1860s thought [MORALE] (snippet). White and Masur disagree on how far soldiers came to see the war as one against slavery [HN-WHITE]. The sensitive-history gate means the pack must not treat either view as the whole story.
- **Turning points in the markets:** the market view (below) disagrees with historians in several places [WILLARD].

## Design implications

Everything below is [I] unless it carries a citation.

### Blocs and weights

Seven blocs, one change from the earlier table. **Merge the abolitionists into the Radicals** (they had a voice, not a vote, and the Radical bloc already carries emancipation pressure; keeping them separate invites a mechanic that makes enslaved people a currency). **Do not add immigrants as a bloc**: they are mostly Democratic voters whose behaviour is a driver (the draft) and a regional modifier (urban immigrant wards); a separate bloc would double count them. Keep the army as a bloc, because its voice reaches Washington through letters, desertion and the soldier vote, not only through ballots.

**Weights have two layers.** Voice (influence in Washington) sums to 100; vote share is what the election mechanic draws on. The earlier table's voice weights (Radical 15, moderate 25, War Democrat 20, Peace Democrat 10, border 15, abolitionist 5, army 10) fit the 1861 start. The proposal below changes four values by small amounts: the merged Radical and abolitionist bloc 20 to 18, Peace Democrats 10 to 12, border 15 to 13, army 10 to 12. The ranges matter more than the starts: Radicals gained influence through the JCCW and the Senate caucus, the border states lost it as emancipation advanced, and the Peace wing "nearly took control of their party in mid-1864" (Weber, via [WIKI-COPPER]), so it needs headroom up to 20.

| Bloc | Start voice [I] | Range [I] | Main drivers (sources above) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radical Republicans (with abolitionists) | 18 | 15–25 | Emancipation speed and military emancipation; general's politics (McClellan out, anti-slavery generals in); reunion terms; JCCW hearings; Seward and cabinet; Black enlistment (gate) |
| Moderate and conservative Republicans | 25 | 20–30 | Union first; party unity; cabinet balance; Raymond, Weed |
| War Democrats | 20 | 14–24 | Victories; no abolition; generalships; the draft; absorbed by the National Union label |
| Peace Democrats | 12 | 8–20 | Defeats, casualties, draft, habeas, emancipation; collapse after Atlanta |
| Border-state Unionists | 13 | 8–15 | Slavery safe; Black enlistment; arrests; emancipation by constitution later |
| The army and veterans | 12 | 8–16 | Pay, victories, commander treatment, letters home; the soldier vote (78 percent Lincoln, about 150,000 field ballots) |

Check: 18+25+20+12+13+12 = 100. The two Democratic blocs together hold 32 at the start; the **sum should stay in a band of 28–36 and the split should move**, since the Democratic share of the vote stayed between about 39 and 51 percent in every contest read (Ohio 39.4, Iowa 39.5, Pennsylvania 48.5 in 1863; New York 50.9 in 1862) even as the Peace and War wings traded places. In 1864 the Democratic ticket took 45 percent.

Voters, for the election mechanic: anchor so that historical-default play lands the Union ticket at 52–58 percent nationally in 1864, with Democratic shares in the lower North in the 39–51 percent band above.

### What Congress needs as mechanics

1. **A session calendar** (section 1.5) as pack data. While Congress is out, a bloc can only act through its other seats (JCCW is a joint committee and can sit between sessions, which the sources do not say; UNVERIFIED), the press, governors and caucuses. Actions that need Congress (confirmations, money, amendments) cannot complete; those that do not (calls, proclamations, habeas orders, appointments) can. The 1861 gap is the only one with an explicit "ratify afterwards" structure (earlier note).
2. **Vote counts as outputs with reasons.** A bill or amendment resolves from bloc support and ends with a typed breakdown (yeas, nays, abstentions by bloc); the 14 lame-duck Democrats and the eight abstentions in January 1865 show why abstention and timing need to be explicit terms.
3. **An investigation seat.** The JCCW is a post held by Radicals (five Republicans, two Democrats): it chooses a target after a defeat, takes secret testimony, leaks, and moves the target's public standing and his rivals' chances; the president can protect or sacrifice. The data fields: target, trigger defeat, chair, the standing change, and whether the target is a Republican (non-Republicans fared worse).
4. **Confirmation and rank.** The Senate's power over promotions and the 4 March 1863 lapses are one source of friction in the promotion lists decision 0026 already has.
5. **Finance bills as levers with holders.** The Legal Tender and Banking Acts carry their own bloc coalitions and the gold market reacts; the treasury seat holder can stall.

### What the press needs as mechanics

1. **Papers as typed authors of reports (0032):** alignment (Radical Republican, administration Republican, Democratic, Copperhead), reach (circulation band and rail reach), speed, bias terms, and a "first with the news" chance on battle days.
2. **A weekly front page** showing what the public believes, from the report set, not the truth; bloc support follows belief with a lag. The Tribune's pattern of attacking generals and then endorsing Lincoln late suggests editors' lines move with events and rivals' letters, not on a schedule.
3. **Suppression as a lever with a locked cost.** Both big reversals were quick (Chicago Times closed 1–2 June 1863 and reopened 5 June; World and Journal of Commerce seized 18 May 1864 and released within days) and both cost Lincoln politically with Democrats; mark suppression and arrests as "modelled with locked consequences" under the gate, never as a lever that pays [HD-CIVIL], [MTSU-FA]. Mob action stays narrated-only (previous gate table).
4. **The enemy reads it** (0032): Lee followed McClellan from the Inquirer and Hardee found Sherman's ships from the Tribune, so a leak has a military price.
5. **A manipulation event type** for market and press hoaxes (the May 1864 gold hoax) that moves the gold series and forces a suppression decision.

### National will: terms, not a single number

- **Union will** (the 0028 "two wills", Union side) as the weighted bloc support above, plus: the greenback market's view (gold series with the event sensitivity in 5.2 as a check), casualties of the last few months, the draft's burden (commutation versus men furnished), absent-versus-present army strength, the opposition's seat share, and one term for the soldier vote. Each term returns a typed reason (hard rule 6).
- **Calibration targets** are the two lows (winter 1862–63 and August 1864) and the gold break of 12 July 1864 as the war's largest market shift (+4.80 percent); the model's own gold-like indicator should show its largest one-day move after a Union relief of a threatened capital, not after Atlanta.
- **The election as a derived result:** Union ticket 52–58 percent under historical default; closer than 52 only when the model's July–August 1864 state is a stalemate with heavy losses and no fall of a major city before the vote.
- **A caution for the verdict (0026):** the sources disagree about what McClellan would have done (section 3.3); the game should report the electorate's rejection and the terms behind it, not a counterfactual.

## Findings outside my scope

- **The sensitive-history gate needs a pass on this material:** the draft riots (13–16 July 1863), the arrests and press suppressions, the JCCW's Fort Pillow (1864) and Sand Creek (1865) reports, and anything on Black enlistment. The placements above are proposals; the gate reviewer lane has not seen them.
- **A Confederate will (0028) needs its own research note.** The Union side now has a will with proxies; the second will (desertion, impressment, conscription politics, Davis versus governors such as Brown and Vance, price inflation) is unsourced.
- **Verification debt: the vote totals.** The Congressional Globe and House and Senate journals, and the Official Records tables, need a lane with working access before the pack cites votes, strengths, calls and the draft table.
- **Hidden-stat centres for the new people** (Wade, Chandler, Stevens, Sumner, Julian, Vallandigham, Seymour, Marble, Raymond, Greeley, Bennett, Weed) need dossiers with citations before the pack validator will accept them (0005, 0026); this note only names them.
- **Peace feelers as situations:** Greeley's Niagara Falls mission (July 1864), Raymond's August proposal, the Hampton Roads conference (mentioned by Randall via ECW-MCC) and Vallandigham's exile each fit the cause-predicate and historical-default format of 0023 and could be the first Phase 1 political situations.
- **State legislatures and governors are election-rule seats, not just regiment raisers.** Whether soldiers could vote in the field depended on the state legislature (no Democratic-controlled one allowed it, per ECW-SV), and a Pennsylvania court ruling cut its 1863 soldier vote. The earlier dossier's governors-as-seats model (and Morton governing around a Democratic Indiana legislature in 1863) needs a "state electoral law" field before the election mechanic is built; the kernel's seat design (FOUNDATION section 8) does not yet cover it.
- **Dakota War** (1862) is not covered here; 0026 puts it under the gate and the earlier notes did not research it. Its political effects (Minnesota, the Department of the Northwest) need a lane and a placement record before any situation is authored.
