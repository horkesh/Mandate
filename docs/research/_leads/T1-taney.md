## 2. His own words calling on the President to enforce the process

### 2a. The closing of the opinion in Ex parte Merryman (Reports pp. 269-270)

Exact print (Reports, OCR; "United States, It" is an OCR slip for a full stop):

p. 269 (end): "I shall, therefore, order all the proceedings in this case, with my opinion, to be filed and recorded in the circuit court of the United States for the district of Maryland, and direct the clerk to"

p. 270 (start): "transmit a copy, under seal, to the president of the United States. It will then remain for that high officer, in fulfilment of his constitutional obligation to "take care that the laws be faithfully executed," to determine what measures he will take to cause the civil process of the United States to be respected and enforced."

Corrections to the wording supplied in the task:
- The print has "I shall, therefore, order", not "I shall order". The task's version drops "therefore,".
- Campbell's Reports lower-case the offices ("circuit court", "district of Maryland", "clerk", "president"). The task's capitals match later reprints, not this print.
- The previous sentence (p. 269, same paragraph) is the reason: "It is possible that the officer who has incurred this grave responsibility may have misunderstood his instructions, and exceeded the authority intended to be given him;" and before that, "I have exercised all the power which the constitution and laws confer upon me, but that power has been resisted by a force too strong for me to overcome."

Cross-checks (same words, minor typography): Tyler p. 426 (capitalised, "obligation to 'take care that the laws be faithfully executed,'", ends "respected and enforced."); Pamphlet pp. 15-16 (Jackson, Miss., 1861: "obligation, to" with a comma; the passage runs over the page break between 15 and 16). So the closing was in print in 1861 in a Confederate-state reprint. Strength: STRONG (his own opinion, three printings agreeing).

### 2b. Only Congress can suspend the writ (same opinion)

- Reports p. 255 (the sentence that states the rule): "I had supposed it to be one of those points of constitutional law upon which there was no difference of opinion, and that it was admitted on all hands, that the privilege of the writ could not be suspended, except by act of congress." Also in Tyler p. 423, same words.
- Reports p. 257: "It is true, that in the cases mentioned, congress is, of necessity, the judge of whether the public safety does or does not require it; and their judgment is conclusive."
- Reports p. 266 quotes Story: "the right to judge whether the exigency had arisen must exclusively belong to that body" (Taney quoting, not his own sentence).
- Taney's own plain statement of the president's lack of power, Reports p. 260: "I can see no ground whatever for supposing that the president, in any emergency, or in any state of things, can authorize the suspension of the privileges of the writ of habeas corpus, or the arrest of a citizen, except in aid of the judicial power."
- NOT Taney's words: the reporter's headnote (Reports p. 246) "Under the constitution of the United States, congress is the only power which can authorize the suspension of the privilege of the writ." Do not quote that as Taney.

### 2c. What he said from the bench, 28 May 1861 (before the written opinion)

- Reports p. 253 (reporter's narrative, indirect): "He concluded by saying, that he should cause his opinion, when filed, and all the proceedings, to be laid before the President, in order that he might perform his constitutional duty, to enforce the laws, by securing obedience to the process of the United States." Reporter's words, not Taney's.
- Taney's written statement read in court 28 May, Reports p. 253: "I shall therefore put my opinion in writing, and file it in the office of the clerk of the circuit court, in the course of this week." (Same in RR1 Doc. 207, p. 301, from the N.Y. Times, 29 May, and Pamphlet p. 5 from the Baltimore Exchange, 29 May.)
- Pamphlet p. 5 (Baltimore Exchange, 29 May 1861, a newspaper report of his oral remarks, so the reporter's rendering): "I shall merely say to-day, that I shall reduce to writing the reasons under which I have acted, ... and shall report them with these proceedings to the President, and call upon him to perform his Constitutional duty--to enforce the laws, by compelling obedience to the civil process." Introduced by "Gentlemen, I shall feel it my duty to enforce the process of the Court." STRONG as a contemporary newspaper; note it is a reporter's account of oral words.
- RR1 Doc. 207, p. 301 (N.Y. Times, 29 May, reporter's summary): "During the week he should prepare his opinion in the premises, and forward it to the President, calling upon him to perform his constitutional duty, and see that the laws be faithfully executed, and enforce the decrees of this Court." Reporter's paraphrase.

### What the pack can safely use
- Safe: the hearing of 27-28 May 1861; Taney's statement on 28 May that he would file a written opinion "in the course of this week" and send it to the President to perform "his constitutional duty"; the opinion filed after 28 May and printed in the National Intelligencer by 4 June 1861 (Rebellion Record Diary p. 92); and in Taney's hands as a published opinion by 8 June (his own letter, Tyler p. 430).
- If the game needs one date for "written opinion issued", use "by 4 June 1861" (STRONG, printed), not 1 June. 1 June (a Saturday, the last day of the week he named) is plausible but rests on finding-aid snippets I could not read, so it is UNVERIFIED. The window supported by print is 28 May (exclusive) to 4 June 1861 (inclusive).
- Do not use Vladeck's "17 June"; the 8 June letter in Tyler shows it was out earlier.
