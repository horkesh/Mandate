# L3: Terms of the surrender of Fort Sumter, 13-14 April 1861

Source key: OR I-1 = *War of the Rebellion: Official Records*, Series I, vol. 1 (1880), archive.org `warofrebellionco0001vari` (OCR downloaded and searched; page numbers from running heads, chapter I "Operations in Charleston Harbor, S. C."). All OR cites below are STRONG (contemporary official reports and correspondence), except where marked. OCR errors are mine to flag; quotations are exact to the OCR with doubts marked [sic/OCR].

## (a) The agreed terms, in each side's own words

The terms were fixed on 11 April, refused then, and re-accepted on 13 April. Three texts matter.

1. The written offer of 11 April (Beauregard to Anderson, OR I-1 p. 13, enclosure 1 to Anderson's report of 19 April): "All proper facilities will be afforded for the removal of yourself and command, together with company arms and property, and all private property, to any post in the United States which you may select. The flag which you have upheld so long and with so much fortitude, under the most trying circumstances, may be saluted by you on taking it down." Anderson refused it the same day (p. 13, no. 2, "a demand with which I regret that my sense of honor, and of my obligations to my Government, prevent my compliance").

2. Anderson's own note of 13 April, "20 min past 2 o'clock" (p. 14-15, no. 6): "I am willing to evacuate this fort upon the terms and conditions offered by yourself on the 11th instant, at any hour you may name to-morrow, or as soon as we can arrange means of transportation. I will not replace my flag until the return of your messenger." (He refers there to the visit of "General Wigfall, who said that he came with a message from yourself".)

3. Beauregard's written terms of 13 April, "5 min. to 6 o'clock p. m." (OR I-1 p. 15, no. 7, brought by Allston and Jones): "to offer you the following terms of evacuation: All proper facilities for the removal of yourself and command, together with company arms and private property, to any point within the United States you may select. Apprised that you desire the privilege of saluting your flag on retiring, I cheerfully concede it, in consideration of the gallantry with which you have defended the place under your charge. The Catawba steamer will be at the landing of Sumter to-morrow morning at any hour you may designate for the purpose of transporting you whither you may desire."
   Anderson replied at 7.50 p.m. (p. 15, no. 8): "I have the honor to acknowledge the receipt of your communication of this evening, and to express my gratification at its contents. Should it be convenient, I would like to have the Catawba here at about nine o'clock to-morrow morning." This exchange of 13 April is the written agreement; there is no separate document headed "Terms of evacuation".

Anderson's report, telegram from the Baltic off Sandy Hook, 18 April (OR I-1 p. 12): "I accepted terms of evacuation offered by General Beauregard, being the same offered by him on the 11th instant, prior to the commencement of hostilities, and marched out of the fort Sunday afternoon, the 14th instant, with colors flying and drums beating, bringing away company and private property, and saluting my flag with fifty guns." His letter of 19 April from New York (p. 12) adds: "the withdrawal, with the honors of war, of my garrison on the 14th instant from that harbor". No detailed report by Anderson was printed in OR I-1; these two and the enclosed correspondence (pp. 13-16) are all there is.

Capt. J. G. Foster's engineer journal (OR I-1 p. 23, written 1 Oct 1861, so a retrospective of the same officer, not a day-book; label STRONG-official but later), on Wigfall's visit: "The commanding officer then accepted the conditions, saying that the terms he accepted were those proposed by General Beauregard on the 11th, namely: To evacuate the fort with his command, taking arms and all private and company property, saluting the United States flag as it was lowered, and being conveyed, if he desired it, to any northern port." Then (p. 24): the aides "returned, with the approval of all the conditions desired except the saluting of the flag as it was lowered, and this exception was subsequently removed after correspondence."

Beauregard's report, summary of 16 April (OR I-1 p. 29): "I dispatched two of my aides to Major Anderson with terms of evacuation. In recognition of the gallantry exhibited by the garrison I cheerfully agreed that on surrendering the fort the commanding officer might salute his flag." "By 8 o'clock the terms of evacuation were definitely accepted."
Detailed report of 27 April (pp. 32-33): he sent Maj. Jones "with substantially the same propositions I had submitted to Major Anderson on the 11th instant, with the exception of the privilege of saluting his flag". Anderson, through the aides, said it "would be exceedingly gratifying to him, as well as to his command, to be permitted to salute their flag ... and hoped that General Beauregard would not refuse it, as such a privilege was not unusual", but "would not urge the point". Beauregard: "I very cheerfully agreed to allow the salute, as an honorable testimony to the gallantry and fortitude with which Major Anderson and his command had defended their post, and I informed Major Anderson of my decision about 7 1/2 o'clock, through Major Jones" (p. 33).
Beauregard's telegram to Walker, 13 April (p. 309): "Anderson surrenders to the Confederate Government unconditionally, but I have granted him the same terms as on the 11th instant". And (p. 310): "We take possession of Fort Sumter to-morrow morning. I allow him the privilege of saluting his flag. No one killed on our side."

## (b) Sequence, dates and times (all STRONG, OR I-1 unless marked)

- 11 April, 2.20 p.m.: Chesnut, Chisolm, Lee leave Charleston with the demand; at Sumter 3.45; Anderson's written refusal about 4.30 (aides' report, p. 59; Beauregard p. 30; Foster p. 18 says "At 4 p. m."). Aides leave 4.40, report 5.10 p.m. (p. 59). Anderson's remark: "if you do not batter the fort to pieces about us, we shall be starved out in a few days" (p. 59).
- 11 April, about 9.10 p.m.: Walker's telegram authorising Beauregard to ask the time of evacuation (Beauregard, p. 30). Beauregard's second note, 11 April (p. 13-14, no. 3): "If you will state the time at which you will evacuate Fort Sumter, and agree that in the mean time you will not use your guns against us unless ours shall be employed against Fort Sumter, we will abstain from opening fire upon you."
- 12 April, 12.45 a.m.: second note delivered; Anderson's answer 3.15 a.m. (p. 60): he would "evacuate Fort Sumter by noon on the 15th instant" if given transport, and would not open fire "unless compelled ... by some hostile act", should he not receive "controlling instructions from my Government or additional supplies" (p. 14, no. 4). The aides judged this unacceptable and gave notice at 3.20 a.m. (p. 14, no. 5, signed Chesnut and Lee): the batteries "will open the fire ... in one hour".
- 12 April: signal shell 4.30 a.m. (Beauregard, p. 29 and p. 31: "The signal shell was fired from Fort Johnson at 4.30 a. m."; Foster p. 18 says 4.30 as well in the text "At 4 1/2 a. m."); fire general by about 5; Sumter replied about 7 a.m. (Foster p. 18; Beauregard p. 31).
- 13 April: barracks afire about 8-9 a.m. (Beauregard p. 32 "About 8 o'clock"; Foster p. 22 "at nine o'clock"; Beauregard's summary p. 29 says "about 7 1/2"). Flagstaff fell about 1 p.m. (Foster p. 23; Beauregard p. 32, "About 1.30"). Chesnut et al. record the U.S. flag "suddenly disappeared" at 1.10 p.m. (p. 61).
- 13 April, Wigfall's visit: Wigfall crossed from Cummings Point in a skiff under Gen. Simons's order (Beauregard p. 32; Chesnut et al. p. 61), entered by an embrasure with a white flag on his sword (Foster p. 23). Foster p. 23 gives his words: "Any terms that you may desire--your own terms--the precise nature of which General Beauregard will arrange with you." Anderson hauled down his flag and raised the white flag. The three aides sent by Beauregard (Miles, Pryor, Lee) arrived soon after and said Wigfall "had not seen him [Beauregard] for two days" (Foster p. 23). Anderson's note of 2.20 p.m. (above) followed.
- 13 April, about 2.45 p.m.: Maj. Jones and Col. Allston reach Sumter (report of 15 April, OR I-1 p. 64: "about a quarter to 3 o'clock p. m."), offer the terms except the salute (p. 65).
- 13 April, 5.55 p.m.: Beauregard's written terms, with salute conceded (p. 15). About 7 p.m.: Jones, Miles, Pryor, Hartstene deliver them; Anderson "much gratified"; asks for the Catawba at 9 a.m.; arranges that Lt. Snyder go to the fleet (p. 65, "It was arranged that the Catawba or some other steamer should convey the major and his command either directly to New York or put them on board the United States fleet"). Anderson's reply 7.50 p.m. (p. 15). Beauregard says "By 8 o'clock the terms ... were definitely accepted" (p. 29).
- 14 April: Snyder taken out to the fleet in the morning by Hartstene; it was arranged that "the steamers and all necessary facilities ... should be ready at 11 o'clock, and that they should be conveyed to the fleet, and have the option either of taking passage in one of their vessels or of going on the one furnished by the Confederacy" (Hartstene, OR I-1 pp. 66-67). Captain Gillis of the fleet visited the fort that morning (Foster p. 24).
- 14 April, the salute and the accident: "At 11 o'clock all facilities were at the disposal of Major Anderson, but the work of removal was delayed in consequence of the accidental explosion which killed and wounded five of his command. They were not all embarked until sundown, when it was too late to cross the bar. This, however, was effected early the following morning, and the command shortly afterwards was transferred to the steamer Baltic" (Hartstene, 16 April, p. 67). Beauregard: "the formal transfer of the fort to our possession did not take place until 4 o'clock in the afternoon of the 14th instant. At that hour ... our troops occupied it, and the Confederate flag was hoisted on the ramparts of Sumter with a salute from the various batteries" (p. 29). "The steamer Isabel having been placed at the service of Major Anderson, he and his command were transferred to the United States vessels off the harbor." Anderson says he marched out "Sunday afternoon, the 14th" (p. 12). Beauregard to Walker, 14 April (p. 314): "I have possession of Sumter. Anderson and garrison on Isabel going in morning."
- 15 April: Isabel crosses the bar early; the garrison goes to the Baltic (Hartstene p. 67). Beauregard's 17 April report (p. 28) puts it "on the 15th instant he left the harbor on the steamer Isabel" while the Cummings Point troops "lined the beach, silent, and with heads uncovered". Baltic sails for New York; Anderson's telegram is dated off Sandy Hook 18 April, letter from New York 19 April (p. 12).
   Foster's journal (p. 24) is misdated here: it has the Isabel crossing the bar on "April 14" and arrival in New York on "April 17"; Anderson's own 18 April telegram dated off Sandy Hook contradicts "17". Treat Anderson, Hartstene and Beauregard (15 April for the crossing) as controlling.

## (c) Prisoners

Nothing in OR I-1's Sumter chapter treats the garrison as prisoners of war, or mentions parole, exchange or an oath. I grepped the whole OCR of the volume: "prisoners of war" and "parole" appear only in later chapters of the volume (Pensacola/Barrancas, the Texas surrenders), not in the Sumter chapter. The terms are the removal of the command "to any post in the United States which you may select" (Beauregard, 11 and 13 April, pp. 13, 15) with "company arms and private property", "with colors flying and drums beating" and "the honors of war" (Anderson, pp. 12). That is the sourced evidence that they were not held. A flat statement that nobody was a prisoner is an inference from silence plus the terms; no official says the words "not prisoners". The wounded were treated separately: Whiting's order of 15 April (OR I-1 p. 16): "The wounded will receive the best attention, and will be placed in the State hospital." Gibbes (p. 66) records that one died, one stayed in the hospital in Charleston under Dr. Chisolm "and four were removed with the garrison". (Edward Galloway, the man who died of his wounds, is not named in OR I-1; I did not see his name in the OCR.)

## (d) The salute, the guns, the death: what the two sides say

- Fifty guns: Anderson only, "saluting my flag with fifty guns" (OR I-1 p. 12, STRONG). No official Confederate report gives a number for the U.S. salute. Foster, the Hartstene and Beauregard reports, and Gibbes all say "saluting" without a number.
- A hundred guns: the "100 guns" intended number is not in OR I-1 (OCR searched for "hundred guns", "100 guns"; the only hits are two unrelated March 1861 letters from Sumter on Confederate salvos fired from Moultrie). It appears in later retellings (see the search-snippet note at the end); the primary I can cite for "50" is Anderson alone. Whether 100 was the plan, and that the salute stopped at 50 because of the accident, is not in the Official Records. [Not verified: RETRO/NONE for "100".]
- Who proposed the salute: Beauregard himself offered it first, in the 11 April demand ("may be saluted by you on taking it down", p. 13). On 13 April the aides (Jones, Allston) came without it; Anderson asked for it; Beauregard conceded (Beauregard pp. 32-33; Jones/Allston p. 65; Foster p. 24, "this exception was subsequently removed after correspondence"). Anderson (via Foster p. 23) understood Wigfall to have offered all the 11 April terms, including the salute, so the two accounts differ on whether the salute was ever in doubt, but both end with it granted. Beauregard's telegram to Dunovant and Simons, 13 April (pp. 312-313): "He will be allowed the privilege of saluting his flag on lowering it."
- Whose flag was saluted: Anderson's own flag, the U.S. flag, was saluted "on lowering it" / "as it was lowered" (Anderson p. 12 ("whilst saluting our flag as it was lowered"); Beauregard p. 313; Foster p. 23). The Confederate flag was separately saluted: Beauregard p. 29: "the Confederate flag was hoisted on the ramparts of Sumter with a salute from the various batteries" (no number). Anderson's reports do not mention the Confederate flag.
- The accident: Anderson, 19 April (p. 12): "in consequence of some unaccountable misfortune, one man was killed, two seriously and three slightly wounded whilst saluting our flag as it was lowered." Foster (p. 24): "one man was instantly killed, one mortally and four severely wounded, by the premature discharge of a gun and explosion of a pile of cartridges." Beauregard's order (Whiting, 15 April, pp. 15-16): the garrison commander "will bury the unfortunate soldier who has been accidentally killed by explosion of misplaced powder while saluting his flag. He will be buried with all the honors of war in the parade of the fort." Gibbes (p. 66): "the explosion of some loose cartridges beneath a gun struck down seven men. One was instantly killed, and another so seriously wounded that he died soon after reaching my hospital". Hartstene (p. 67): "killed and wounded five". The casualty counts disagree among the four (Anderson 1 killed + 5 hurt, Foster 1+5, Gibbes 1 killed + 6, Hartstene 5 killed or wounded) and the official reports quoted above never name the dead man or his regiment: no "Hough" occurs in the OCR. The one name is in Capt. G. B. Cuthbert's report (OR I-1 p. 56): "the grave of the unfortunate Howe, the first victim of the sad explosion which took place while Major Anderson was engaged in saluting his flag". The volume's index (OCR line near "Howe, Daniel- Mentioned ... 56") lists "Howe, Daniel" for that page, so the official record has the Christian name Daniel but the surname Howe, which is the Confederate captain's spelling of the man later styled Daniel Hough. STRONG that a man died in the salute and was buried in the parade (with Whiting's order, p. 15-16); the name "Hough" is NOT in the OR; the identification Howe = Hough is an inference (the 1861 official record says Howe).
- Two statements by Beauregard on Wigfall: Beauregard's report (p. 32) says Wigfall crossed on Simons's order, and that Anderson "understood him as offering the same conditions ... while Colonel Wigfall's impression was that Major Anderson unconditionally surrendered". Beauregard's telegram (p. 309) calls it "unconditional" while granting the 11 April terms. Rhett's telegram (p. 309): "Fort Sumter has unconditionally surrendered ... The same terms just offered will be granted." That "unconditionally" language is on the Confederate side only; Anderson's papers never say it.
- Casualties in the Confederate telegrams: Beauregard, 14 April (p. 314): "None killed; two wounded." That cannot refer to the garrison accident of the same afternoon; the wording is ambiguous (it may refer to his own side across the whole action). Do not use it for the accident.
