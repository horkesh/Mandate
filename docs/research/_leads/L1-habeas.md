# L1: Who urged suspending the writ of habeas corpus, April-July 1861 (S18/S12)

Lane: leads-L1-habeas. Read in OCR from archive.org full text; page numbers are printed running heads (OCR doubts marked). Labels: STRONG = contemporary primary/official; WEAK = memoir or retrospective.

## Bottom line

- The order itself is sourced (a). Its text, date and page are exact.
- Nobody was found who "urged" the suspension before 27 April 1861. In the print read, the idea first appears in Lincoln's own letter to Scott of 25 April ("in the extremest necessity"). The President says the question of arresting the Maryland legislature "has been submitted to and considered by me" but does not name who submitted it.
- No recommendation from Scott, Butler, Cameron, Seward, Blair or Bates to suspend the writ was found. Bates's opinion of 5 July is explicitly NOT an urging: he says the writ did not need suspending (see below).
- The nearest contemporary calls are for martial law, arrest and force in Baltimore (Patterson 21 Apr, Thomson 23 Apr, NY Tribune 20 Apr, Butler 6 May, Judge Nelson 8 May), and for the writ itself only after the fact (Maj. Morris 14 May; Sen. Wilson 10 Jul; Sen. Lane 16 Jul).

## (a) The order

- Lincoln to "the Commanding General, Army of the United States", Washington, 27 April 1861. STRONG (official; Nicolay & Hay, Complete Works 1894, vol. 2, p. 39; archive.org id `completeworksofa02linc_0`).
- Exact text (OCR): "You are engaged in suppressing an insurrection against the laws of the United States. If at any point on or in the vicinity of any military line which is now or which shall be used between the city of Philadelphia and the city of Washington you find resistance which renders it necessary to suspend the writ of habeas corpus for the public safety, you personally, or through the officer in command at the point at which resistance occurs, are authorized to suspend that writ." Signed "Abraham Lincoln".
- The brief's quotation says "repressing"; the print says "suppressing". The words "a military line ... between Philadelphia and Washington" (not "from Annapolis") are the print.
- Not in OR Series I vol. 2 (searched `warofrebellionco0002vari`: no hit for "military line", "authorized to suspend", "Annapolis City"). The "p. 630" in the brief is Judge Nelson's telegram (below), not the order. OR Series II vol. 1 (where the order is probably reprinted) could not be found as a readable scan; HathiTrust is blocked. Citation of the order to OR is therefore unverified; cite Nicolay & Hay p. 39 and the Globe (below).
- Companion orders in the same volume: 10 May 1861 proclamation authorizing the commander on the Florida coast to suspend the writ (p. 45); 2 July 1861 order, same wording for the "military line ... between the city of New York and the city of Washington", countersigned "William H. Seward, Secretary of State" (p. 54). STRONG.
- The 27 April and 10 May orders were read into the Senate record by an opponent, 11 July: Globe, 37th Cong. 1st sess., Senate 10 July 1861 (issue of 11 July, `sim_united-states-congress-congressional-globe_1861-07-11_3`).

## (b) By person

### Lincoln himself, 25 April (the first appearance)
- Lincoln to Lt.-Gen. Scott, Washington, 25 April 1861, N&H vol. 2 p. 38. STRONG. "I therefore conclude that it is only left to the commanding general to watch and await their action, which, if it shall be to arm their people against the United States, he is to adopt the most prompt and efficient means to counteract, even, if necessary, to the bombardment of their cities, and, in the extremest necessity, the suspension of the writ of habeas corpus."
- The same letter: "The question has been submitted to and considered by me, whether it would not be justifiable ... for you ... to arrest or disperse the members of that body. I think it would not be justifiable nor efficient." Who submitted the question: not named. Nobody found.
- Scott transmitted this to Butler on 26 April as "instructions respecting the legislature of Maryland" received from the President. OR I-2 pp. 601-602 (printed head 602): "...in the extremest necessity suspension of the writ of habeas corpus." STRONG. This is Scott relaying, not recommending.

### General Scott
- No letter from Scott recommending suspension, and no "if necessary" request, found in OR I vol. 2 (all "habeas"/"writ" hits read: 7) or in N&H vol. 2. Nobody found.
- Scott as the source of the arrest power (not of a request): Townsend (by direction of the General-in-Chief) to Cadwalader, 16 May: "Herewith you will receive a power to arrest persons under certain circumstances, and to hold them prisoners though they should be demanded by writs of habeas corpus. This is a high and delicate trust ... errors, if any, should be on the side of safety to the country. This is the language of the General-in-Chief himself." OR I-2 p. 639. STRONG.
- Townsend for Scott to Butler, 7 May (reply to Butler's 6 May): "in regard to the arresting of persons who commit acts of hostility to the Government you are clothed with the same authority which has been conferred upon him." OR I-2 p. 627. STRONG.
- Scott to Banks, 24 June (arrest of the Baltimore police board, not the writ): "It is the opinion of the Secretary of War, and I need not add my own, that the blow should be early struck." OR I-2 p. 139 (the letter begins on p. 138). STRONG, adjacent. Shows Scott and Cameron urging arrests in Baltimore in June.

### General Butler (Annapolis, Relay House)
- Butler to Scott, Relay House, 6 May, OR I-2 p. 624 (printed head 624): of Dorsey, Gaither and Carroll's cavalry: "Can anything be done with them? Might they not be arrested and at least restrained until we are certain what will be the disposition of Maryland? But this is a matter for your better judgment." STRONG. He asks for arrest leave; he does not mention the writ. No letter from Butler urging suspension found.
- Butler 18 May to Cameron (p. 641): "Is it because I have caused Winans to be arrested?" STRONG; defends the arrest, not the writ.

### General Cadwalader
- Reply to Townsend, Baltimore, 16 May, OR I-2 p. 640: "The power to arrest persons under certain circumstances, and to hold them prisoners though they should be demanded by writs of habeas corpus, is certainly a high and delicate trust. I will use every effort to execute it, if necessary, with prudence and discretion". STRONG. He receives it; he did not ask for it. In the same letter (p. 644 head, 16 May) he acknowledges "the power to suspend the writ of habeas corpus had been previously received with your letter of the 16th."
- Return to Taney, Fort McHenry, 26 May: "he is duly authorized by the president of the United States, in such cases, to suspend the writ of habeas corpus, for the public safety ... he, therefore, respectfully requests that you will postpone further action upon this case, until he can receive instructions from the president". Ex parte Merryman, 17 F. Cas. 144 (Case No. 9,487), Wikisource transcription of Federal Cases vol. 17 (primary text). STRONG as to his refusal; not an urging.

### Secretary Cameron
- No letter or telegram from Cameron recommending suspension found (OR I-2; Rebellion Record vol. 1; N&H vol. 2). Nobody found.
- Adjacent: Scott's 24 June letter quotes Cameron's opinion on striking the Baltimore police board (above).

### Attorney General Bates
- Opinion to the President, Attorney General's Office, 5 July 1861, House Ex. Doc. No. 5, 37th Cong. 1st sess., transmitted to the House 13 July (archive.org id `suspensionofwrit00unit`; later Op. Atty. Gen. vol. 10 p. 74). STRONG.
- Questions put to him (pp. 1-2): (1) whether the President has "the discretionary power to cause to be arrested and held in custody persons known to have criminal intercourse with the insurgents"; (2) whether he may refuse to obey a writ of habeas corpus in such cases.
- Gist (p. 5): "I am clearly of opinion that, in a time like the present ... the President has the lawful discretionary power to arrest and hold in custody persons known to have criminal intercourse with the insurgents". On the writ (p. 11): "I deny that he is under any obligation to obey such a writ, issued under such circumstances."
- Not an urging of suspension (pp. 11-12): "Whatever I have said about the suspension of the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, has been said in deference to the opinions of others, and not because I myself thought it necessary ... I never thought of first suspending the writ of habeas corpus any more than I thought of first suspending the writ of replevin before seizing arms and munitions destined for the enemy."
- Transmittal, 13 July (p. 1): asked for "a copy of the order suspending the writ", he answers "there is no such order in the records or the files of my office" and refers the House to the Secretary of State. So Bates neither drafted nor kept the order.
- Lincoln's 4 July message says an opinion "will probably be presented by the attorney-general" (N&H vol. 2 p. 60), i.e. it was commissioned after the fact.

### Seward
- Countersigned the 2 July order (N&H vol. 2 p. 54). No letter or speech by Seward urging suspension in April-July found in the volumes searched. Nobody found. (His later remarks, e.g. the Lyons "bell" story, are retrospective and not read.)

### The Maryland arrests (Merryman)
- Merryman was arrested "at two o'clock on the morning of the 25th of May 1861" at his house by an armed force "professing to act under military orders"; the return says by Col. Samuel Yohe under the orders of Maj.-Gen. William H. Keim (Pennsylvania), brought to Fort McHenry (Cadwalader's return gives a date of "the 20th inst." in the Wikisource transcription, which conflicts with the 25 May arrest in the petition and Taney's opinion: treat 25 May as the arrest date). Taney issued the writ 26 May; attachment 27 May; the marshal was refused at the gate 28 May; opinion filed later and printed in the Intelligencer (Rebellion Record vol. 1 diary notes it as "simply a protest against the suspension of the writ by the President"). Ex parte Merryman, 17 F. Cas. 144; Rebellion Record vol. 1 (`rebellionrecord14evergoog`). STRONG.
- Who urged the arrest of Merryman: Keim's and Yohe's authority only; no urging found.
- Earlier refusal of a writ at Fort McHenry, before Merryman: Maj. W. W. Morris, Fourth U.S. Artillery, commanding, to Judge Giles, 14 May (printed in NY Evening Post, 14 May): "I certainly regard it as sufficient legal cause for suspending the writ of habeas corpus." He gives reasons of expediency: the writ "in the hands of an unfriendly power might depopulate this fortification". Rebellion Record vol. 1, Documents, Doc. 160, pp. 239-240. STRONG (printed letter), WEAK as an "urging": he asserts it for himself and cites no order.

### Postmaster Blair (Montgomery Blair) and Francis P. Blair
- Nothing found in OR I-2, N&H vol. 2 or Rebellion Record vol. 1. Nobody found.

### Others on record in the window
- Maj.-Gen. Patterson to Col. L. Thomas, Philadelphia, 21 April: "it is expedient to declare or put the entire line, and ten, twenty, or thirty miles on each side, or the entire department under my command, under martial law; and if the General concurs, I ask his approval, or rather that he will give the order." OR I-2 p. 586. STRONG; martial law, not the writ. This is the first officer's call for the military-line power Lincoln granted on 27 April.
- J. Edgar Thomson (Pennsylvania Railroad) to Cameron, Philadelphia, 23 April: "As soon as the capital is safe from attack, it seems to me that the Government should at once turn on Baltimore and place it under martial law, and require that it should pay all damages". OR I-2 p. 596. STRONG (letter); a civilian railroad president; martial law, not the writ.
- NY Tribune, 20 April ("The Rattlesnake's Fangs"), Rebellion Record vol. 1 (about p. 80): "What can martial law in Baltimore be worth if the traitors who instigated this assassination be not dealt with according to law? ... This is no time for half measures." STRONG (contemporary newspaper); martial law, not the writ.
- Judge M. Nelson, Frederick County Court, telegram to the commander at Relay House, 8 May: "Send us five hundred men by first train, with power to arrest and disarm." OR I-2 p. 630. STRONG; a Maryland civil judge asking for arrest power, not for suspension.
- Congress, after the fact, in favour: Sen. Henry Wilson (Mass.), Senate 10 July 1861: "a full, complete justification of the President in authorizing General Scott to suspend the writ of habeas corpus in and about that city ... If there ever was in any portion of the Republic any spot of earth, or any time, where or when the writ of habeas corpus ought to be suspended, the city of Baltimore was the spot, and the last few weeks the time, for its suspension." Congressional Globe daily issue of 11 July (`..._1861-07-11_3`), Senate, about p. 44 (page inferred from running heads; verify). STRONG.
- Sen. Henry S. Lane (Ind.), Senate 16 July: "The next objection is to the declaration of martial law, by which the writ of habeas corpus was suspended. I only regret that when the writ was suspended, the corpus of Baltimore treason was not 'suspended' too ... I approve, then, the suspension of the writ of habeas corpus." Globe issue of 17 July (`..._1861-07-17_9`); page not fixed (running heads unreadable). STRONG.
- Joseph Holt (Irving Hall, 10 Sept) and Daniel S. Dickinson (Tunkhannock, 19 Aug) endorsed suspension in printed speeches, but both are after the window (Rebellion Record vol. 1 Documents). Not used.
- Opposition, for context: Sen. Kennedy (Md.) on 10 July "there was no immediate necessity for it"; Sen. Polk (Mo.), same day, no vote "that he has done right in suspending the writ".

## Findings outside my scope

- OR I-2 also prints (page not recorded) the Baltimore police board's memorial to Congress (July 1861) calling the suspension "arbitrary" and saying the usurpation was "the advised and deliberate act of the War Department itself": useful as the opposition's words (Chap. IX, July 1 arrest section).
- Scott's 24 June order to Banks (OR I-2 p. 139) shows Scott and Cameron driving the next step after the writ (arrest of the Baltimore police board and Marshal Kane); relevant to a later Baltimore situation.
