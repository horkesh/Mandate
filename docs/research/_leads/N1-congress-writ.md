# N1: Did anyone on record urge Lincoln to put the writ / the Merryman question to Congress, 26 May - 4 July 1861? (S18 `merryman_writ`)

Agent: leads-N1-congress-writ. Read in OCR text (archive.org `_djvu.txt`, read from my own downloads); OCR doubts are marked. Page numbers are printed running-head pages unless marked "page not read" (the archive.org `sim_` newspaper OCR has no page numbers).

## Verdict

NONE FOUND of the specific thing asked (a named person, between 26 May and 4 July 1861, in his own recorded words, urging the President to submit the suspension of the writ, or the Merryman question, to Congress).

What does exist, and is useful for S18 options:
1. Taney's own opinion says only Congress can suspend, and holds up Jefferson's 1807 course (send it to Congress) as the model. This is the nearest thing to "put it to Congress", but it is a judicial statement, not advice addressed to Lincoln.
2. Lincoln himself, in the 4 July message, does the very thing (submits it to Congress), and says he had "trusted" Congress would "readily ratify".
3. Two named people expected Congress to legalise the President's acts in general (Secretary Cameron, c. 18-21 June; Senator Wilson's planned bills, reported 29 June / 1 July). Neither text names the writ.
4. The loudest contemporary voices in the window argue the opposite: no need to wait for Congress (The Independent, 6 June; Reverdy Johnson's article, 29 June; an anonymous NYT piece, 8 June). The NYT editorial of 2 June concedes the point only "for the sake of the argument".

## A. Lincoln: how he describes asking Congress (STRONG, official text)

Nicolay & Hay, *Complete Works* (1894), vol. 2, "Message to Congress in Special Session", 4 July 1861 (archive.org `abelinccompwks02lincrich`); cross-checked in the 12-vol. 1905 edition vol. 6 pp. 308-310 (`compworksabe06lincrich`).

- 1894 vol. 2 p. 59 (1905 vol. 6 p. 308): of the calls for volunteers and army/navy additions: "These measures, whether strictly legal or not, were ventured upon, under what appeared to be a popular demand and a public necessity; trusting then, as now, that Congress would readily ratify them."
- p. 59-60 (1905 p. 308-310): on the writ: "Soon after the first call for militia, it was considered a duty to authorize the commanding general in proper cases, according to his discretion, to suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus ..." and "Now it is insisted that Congress, and not the executive, is vested with this power. But the Constitution itself is silent as to which or who is to exercise the power".
- p. 60 (1905 p. 310), the passage that answers your question about the message: "No more extended argument is now offered, as an opinion at some length will probably be presented by the attorney-general. Whether there shall be any legislation upon the subject, and if any, what, is submitted entirely to the better judgment of Congress."

So the message does NOT ask Congress to act on the writ. It leaves it open ("any legislation ... and if any, what"), and says the Attorney General's opinion will follow (Bates, 5 July; not read, see "Not reached"). Separately (p. 59) it does expect Congress to ratify the other measures.

RETRO: Nicolay & Hay, *Abraham Lincoln: A History*, vol. 4 (1890; `abrahamlincolnhi04nicol`) ch. 8, p. 176: "No attention was of course paid to the transmitted papers; but the President at the time of their receipt was engaged in preparing his message to the coming special session of Congress, and in that document he presented the justification of his act." The same pages (176-177) print the autograph first draft ("I have been reminded from a high quarter that one who is sworn ... should not himself be one to violate them"). WEAK alone; consistent with the message.

No Lincoln letter of 28 May - 3 July in the 1905 vol. 6 mentions Congress and the writ (letters of 13 June, 19 June, the 2 July order to Scott are about Harney, Scott, Mansfield; the 2 July order extends the 27 April suspension authority, vol. 6 pp. 295-296).

## B. Taney (judge): "Congress alone", and the Jefferson precedent (STRONG, judicial)

*Reports of Cases ... Circuit Court, District of Maryland* (Cornell copy `cu31924068531247`), Ex parte Merryman, pp. 246-270. Oral statement 28 May (NYT 29 May 1861, Baltimore dispatch, page not read; OCR: "The President, uncer the Constitution and laws of the United States connot euspenrt the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, nor authorize any wilitary officer to co so"); written opinion filed 1 June (N&H *History* vol. 4 p. 175, RETRO), printed in full NYT 4 June 1861.
- p. 255: "I had supposed it to be one of those points of constitutional law upon which there was no difference of opinion, and that it was admitted on all hands, that the privilege of the writ could not be suspended, except by act of congress."
- p. 256: Jefferson in Burr's case "claimed, on his part, no power to suspend it, but communicated his opinion to congress, with all the proofs in his possession, in order that congress might exercise its discretion upon the subject".
- p. 257: "congress is, of necessity, the judge of whether the public safety does or does not require it; and their judgment is conclusive."
- p. 267 (quoting Marshall, Bollman): "it is for the legislature to say so."
- pp. 269-270 (ending; he sends it to the President, not to Congress): "direct the clerk to transmit a copy, under seal, to the president of the United States. It will then remain for that high officer, in fulfilment of his constitutional obligation to 'take care that the laws be faithfully executed,' to determine what measures he will take to cause the civil process of the United States to be respected and enforced."
Same text in OR II vol. 1, beginning p. 577 (`warofrebellion0201rootrich`, readable; the passages above are in its OCR).

## Findings outside my scope
- The writ order of 27 April was addressed to Scott for the Philadelphia-Washington line; 2 July order (N&H 1905 vol. 6 pp. 295-296) re-grants it for the New York-Washington line. S18 may want both dates.
- NYT 29 May 1861 prints a Washington dispatch saying Taney "volunteered" to go to Baltimore and wished "the Virginians would wade to their waists in Northern blood" (partisan hearsay, press only; flag as WEAK).
- Cameron's speech (NYT 22 June) gives the Army at "250,000 men" in the field and half a million in six months: possible numbers cross-check for the strengths pack.
