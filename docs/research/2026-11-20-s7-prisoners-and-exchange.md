## 1. What the July cartel agreed

### Earlier local exchanges and recognition

Grant's **14 October 1861 reply to Polk** says he cannot exchange on his own authority, does not recognize a Southern Confederacy and will consult higher authority. McClernand's **22–23 October correspondence** describes unconditional release of three prisoners and instructs Buford to avoid discussion of belligerent rights and ground the mission in humanity. Polk's reply accepts that method despite its waiver of his belligerency claim and offers sixteen United States prisoners; Brayman's list and report record their return. Thus the record supplies a local Union statement of the recognition/authority problem **and** a practical reciprocal release, before the general cartel. It does not alone establish every reason for the administration's national policy in 1861. [II1, pp. 511–513.]

### The general agreement

The agreement signed by John A. Dix and D. H. Hill at Haxall's Landing on **22 July 1862** established an exchange scale, delivery arrangements and parole obligations. A general commanding an army or a lieutenant-general counted as sixty privates; a major-general as forty; a brigadier-general as twenty; a colonel as fifteen. Its ratios are accounting equivalents, not evidence of the military effectiveness or worth of any person. [II4, pp. 266–267, articles 1–2.]

Article 3 treated captured civilians differently according to status: private citizens were to be released unconditionally, while civilians employed in military service, such as teamsters and sutlers, entered the exchange arrangements as privates. This is the agreement's text. It does not establish that every political detainee was released or that either government accepted the other's classification. [II4, p. 267.]

Articles 4 and 6 required discharge on parole within ten days of capture, with article 6 allowing the practicability qualification. A paroled person could not perform military duty until exchanged; article 6 expressly included field, garrison and police duty, guarding prisoners or stores, and other duties usually performed by soldiers. Actual delivery of the equivalent across the lines was required before return to service. **Capture, parole, delivery and completed exchange are separate events.** [II4, pp. 267–268.]

Article 7 designated Aiken's Landing on the James River and Vicksburg as principal delivery points, allowed local arrangements between commanders, and required an exchange agent on each side. Article 9 said misunderstandings were not to interrupt release on parole. These clauses establish the intended machinery, not uninterrupted compliance. [II4, p. 268.]

The cartel belongs inside the 1862 research window. It cannot by itself supply a rule that the whole prisoner topic begins later, or that all captives were exchanged promptly. It supplies no national annual total of distinct people exchanged. [II4, pp. 266–268.]

## 2. Exchange was already qualified and contested

Confederate General Orders No. 54, **1 August 1862**, alleged violations by Pope and Steinwehr, excepted their commissioned officers from the cartel, ordered close confinement, and threatened hanging selected officers if a specified account of civilian executions were substantiated. It distinguished officers from enlisted men. The order proves a conditional retaliatory policy; it does not prove that the threatened executions occurred. Davis's covering allegations must remain attributed to him. [II4, pp. 835–838.]

On **22 September**, Randolph told Lee that delivered commissioned officers had been exchanged and described **10,368** noncommissioned officers and privates in exchange accounting, with each noncommissioned officer counted as two privates. This is not a count of 10,368 distinct people, and it is not a total for the year. His expectation of another Saturday delivery is an expectation, not its completion. [II4, p. 894.]

Political exchange could mean banishment from home. On **18 September**, Randolph instructed that East Tennessee political prisoners should be exchanged on condition that they went beyond Confederate lines and did not return. His **19 September** case instructions separately considered release on oath, hospital confinement, parole and detention. Neither document makes political detention interchangeable with the cartel's ordinary military categories. [II4, pp. 892–893.]

John Baxter's **13 November** report described his mission with Thomas A. R. Nelson and Thomas W. Humes to recover East Tennessee prisoners. Baxter reported 145 received at Knoxville, 62 sent home and 83 remaining, with discrepancies between the number expected from Salisbury and those actually brought. He also printed draft agreements concerning civilians. He expressly said those agreements had **not been ratified and were invalid**. They cannot be cited as an implemented general settlement of political detention. The attached October instructions and November report are separate dates. [II4, pp. 943–945.]

For captured Black men, Mercer's November report and Seddon's **30 November** answer deny an assumption of equal prisoner status. They are examined separately in the [Black service note](2026-11-20-s7-freedpeople-and-black-service.md), section 6. Their policy and threatened punishment cannot establish the individual captives' eventual fate. [II4, pp. 945–946, 954.]

## 3. Camp Douglas: conditions, disputed assessments and defective records

The earliest local administrative problem in this selection predates the cartel. Chicago mayor Julian S. Rumsey reported approximately **7,000** prisoners on **24 February 1862**, complained about an insufficient guard and an incomplete fence, and asked for reinforcement. Halleck answered that an escort should be retained and that he had no troops to spare. These are contemporary security and capacity claims, not a completed census or a health return. [II3, pp. 315–316.]

Henry W. Bellows's **30 June** letter to Hoffman described standing water, foul sinks, crowding and vermin and urged removal from the camp. Bellows was appealing for intervention; his account should be identified as his inspection and judgment, not converted into a national prison description. On the same date, McVickar reported **326 in hospital**, with additional sick people in barracks; Hoffman described the shortage following the release of Confederate medical officers. [II4, pp. 106–107.]

Hoffman's **1 July** correspondence acknowledged drainage and accommodation problems and sought substantial expenditure. Another letter described the absence of reliable descriptive and accounting records across successive commands. The War Department's later endorsement directed corrective measures. Requests and endorsements establish administrative action, not that a particular drain, hospital or register had already been completed. [II4, pp. 110–111.]

Tucker's **8 July** report is unusually useful because it exposes the limits of its own figures:

| Figure in the report | What it describes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| 7,807 | Prisoners then present, according to the return | All people ever confined there |
| 260 | People in hospital at the reporting point | All sick people; the report also mentions sick in quarters |
| 650 | Deaths reported through 5 July | A rate with a known, stable population at risk |
| Incomplete admissions and escape records | The command's acknowledged information problem | Permission to reconstruct missing totals by subtraction |

[II4, pp. 154–155.]

**Do not divide 650 by 7,807 and call the result a camp mortality rate.** One is a cumulative count over time, the other a later stock; the underlying admission records are explicitly deficient. The table preserves the report's statements without repairing them by invention. [II4, p. 155.]

Hoffman's **10 July** answer refused major expenditure, allowed essential roof repair, and disputed Bellows's overall assessment. He also directed cleaning by prisoner labor. This disagreement is part of the evidence. It does not cancel the recorded sick and dead, and Bellows's criticism does not make every remedy he wanted an order that was carried out. [II4, p. 166.]

On **8 July**, Hoffman declared martial law within the specified camp perimeter and a hundred feet beyond the sentinel chain. Civilian intrusion and camp discipline therefore belong in the local history alongside sanitation. The order is not a general suspension throughout Chicago. [II4, p. 154.]

## 4. Other Union camps: local change, not a single national condition

J. Cooper McKee's **21 July** Camp Butler report described unsuitable accommodation and sanitation in May, followed by changes in hospital arrangements. He reported **123 deaths in May** and **30 in June**, among a prisoner population described as exceeding 2,000. His account attributes improvement to the measures taken. It is evidence of an official's observed sequence and explanation; it is not a controlled estimate of the effect of a building or a stable monthly death rate. No average daily population is provided here. [II4, pp. 254–256.]

H. W. Freedley's **27 October** report on his Camp Chase inspection, following arrival at Columbus on the evening of **16 October**, described **723 prisoners**, of whom only **12 were prisoners of war**, and the remainder political or other categories in his account. He reported 24 patients and four deaths during September and gave a favorable assessment of much of the accommodation. His continuation discussed cooking equipment, compelled use of boilers, ration accounting and the command's arrangements. This later, differently composed population cannot be compared directly with Camp Douglas's summer military prisoners as if the two were matched institutions. [II4, pp. 659–660.]

The same correspondence includes Meigs's **27 October** authorization of contracted nursing for smallpox cases at Camp Chase. That is evidence of a medical problem and an authorized response, not a measured success or a campwide smallpox death count. [II4, p. 658.]

## 5. Confederate custody: prisoner testimony and Confederate records

Lieutenant and quartermaster **J. B. Dorr, Twelfth Iowa Infantry**, reported to Hackleman on **16 July** about imprisonment at Tuscaloosa and Montgomery. He described inadequate quarters, vermin, ration changes and shootings by guards, including the killing of Lieutenant W. S. Bliss at the beginning of May. His account combines personal observation with information about other prisoners; preserve those distinctions rather than treating every incident as independently verified. Oglesby forwarded the report on **19 July**. [II4, pp. 230–232.]

Dorr's statement that 1,600 of 2,300–2,400 captured had been released through death, parole or escape **combines three different outcomes**. It supplies no defensible death total of 1,600. Nor can his local account establish that conditions were identical in all Confederate prisons. [II4, p. 232.]

Surgeon **John Swinburne's 24 July** letter to Winder from Savage Station protested inadequate nourishment for wounded prisoners and restrictions on attending surgeons, and asked either for adequate food or return to Union lines. Its location is a field hospital after the Peninsula fighting, not a standing Salisbury camp. Its urgency is evidence of Swinburne's reported circumstances and appeal; it is not a laboratory assessment of caloric intake. [II4, pp. 277–278.]

Confederate administrative records give a different kind of evidence. On **31 August**, Henry Wirz warned Belle Isle's commander of an expected additional **about 2,000** prisoners, asked whether guards and tents were sufficient, and said he would send 100 guns. The additional number is a forecast, not the island's total, and the question about tents is not proof of their eventual issue. [II4, p. 868.]

The Lynchburg ration abstract for **1–17 August**, and the monthly receipt-and-issue return, record food and supplies accounted for by named Confederate officers. Ration issues are not unique people, nutritional adequacy or proof that each intended recipient received the stated issue. The printed monthly table's heading/OCR needs particular care: the entries are dated 1862 within the 1862 correspondence. Use a scan, not the OCR's stray year, if transcribing it. This note derives no population or diet calculation from that table. [II4, pp. 837–838, 868.]

These sources establish different problems through different observers. They support researching conditions **on both sides in 1862**, without assigning a common national mortality band or treating one side's suffering as a credit against the other's.

### Union parole camps: soldiers' own petition

A **15 November petition**, reproduced with a statement that it was signed by **forty-eight Wisconsin paroled men at Annapolis**, complains of filth, assaults, robbery, killing, drunkenness and gambling and asks to return to Wisconsin until exchange. Salomon forwarded it on **24 November**, endorsing the need to address longstanding complaints and asking which Wisconsin men had been exchanged. The published text does not reproduce all forty-eight signatures. Its allegations and request are the petitioners', not an independently established campwide crime count. [II4, p. 749.]

Hoffman's **26 November answer** says furloughs were not granted to paroled prisoners of any state, acknowledges many absent without leave, and states that those captured and reported before 11 November had been exchanged except part of the Harpers Ferry prisoners. His administrative statement does not prove that every individual had received notice or rejoined a unit. The petition concerns **Union men after Confederate captivity**, distinct from Confederate prisoners held by the Union. Conditions in both kinds of camp deserve separate dates and denominators. [II4, p. 758.]

## 6. Counts and corrections for the reviewer

Hoffman's **24 July** approximate list totals **20,500 Union-held prisoners of war** across eleven stations. Camp Douglas accounts for 7,800 in that rounded list. This is one dated estimate of holdings, not captures during 1862, total capacity, cumulative admissions or a Confederate-held figure. The actual printed location is **page 278**, not page 277 as cited in the earlier gate facts. Page 277 begins Swinburne's letter. This note records the citation correction without editing the older research or the register. [II4, pp. 277–278.]

| Question | Evidence reached | Remaining limit |
|---|---|---|
| Did a general exchange agreement exist in July 1862? | Signed cartel, its nine articles | Implementation has to be established separately |
| Did parole mean immediate return to combat? | Explicit prohibition until exchange | A person's actual return requires a service record |
| Were conditions a later-war issue only? | February–October 1862 reports and returns | No national comparative mortality series |
| Did all political prisoners follow the cartel? | Conditional banishment and failed draft agreements | No complete political detainee census |
| Were Black captives assured ordinary soldier status? | November Confederate rejection in a specific capture case | No established final fate for Manuel and the other captives |
| Why did Union authorities refuse a general cartel in 1861? | Grant's local recognition/authority statement and reciprocal releases are now read | No complete national policy explanation; do not make this local statement the sole federal reason |
