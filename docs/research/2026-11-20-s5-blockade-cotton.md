# S5: blockade, cotton and neutral trade through November 1862

Research, 2026-11-20; not canon or a gate signature. Sources: [S5 register](2026-11-20-s5-sources.md). Printed-page citations support historical observations; **[I]** marks interpretation, arithmetic and proposed model inputs. This note covers economic and diplomatic effects; it does not author S4's naval operations or map data.

## A blockade can restrict trade without sealing every inlet

Adams distinguishes Lincoln's announcement of a blockade from a mere legislative closure of ports. His account dates the proclamation to **19 April 1861**, the extension to Virginia to the 27th, initial implementation at Virginia ports to the 30th, and a small presence off the more important ports by the end of May. British objections to a legislative closure concerned a paper prohibition rather than a maintained force. [A1 pp. 244-249]

By **15 February 1862**, Russell's instruction to Lyons accepted the blockade's effectiveness; the subsequent parliamentary disputes did not reverse that policy. A Commons challenge on 7 March ended in withdrawal, and Russell defended the position in the Lords on the 10th. [A1 pp. 267-272] [I] The historically observed legal outcome is compatible with successful running and with criticism of particular captures. A success counter alone cannot decide legal effectiveness.

The evidence also distinguishes regular bulk commerce from selected high-value cargo. Schwab describes fast, shallow-draught vessels and expensive pilots trading through Nassau, Bermuda and Havana; Nassau transshipment connected local running with larger ocean-going ships. He records both scarce necessities and highly profitable luxury imports. [SCH pp. 237-239] [I] Numerous successful runs would not demonstrate restoration of prewar carrying volume, low freight costs, or delivery to the armies after landing.

## What the Navy's 1862 numbers count

Welles's report of **1 December 1862** describes four maritime blockading squadrons and a coast of nearly 3,000 miles with many inlets. It praises the blockade's effectiveness in emphatic terms. That praise is the Secretary's assessment, not an independently measured rate of interception. [N62 pp. 3-4]

| Figure | Definition in the report | Research use |
|---|---|---|
| 42 vessels in commission in March 1861; 76 available old-navy vessels in his account | Starting naval resources, not 76 ships already present at Confederate ports. Most commissioned ships had been abroad. [N62 p. 24] | Initial mobilization constraint. |
| 264 vessels in the previous annual report | Includes old navy, purchases and new vessels completed **or under construction**. [N62 p. 26] | An administrative inventory; no direct on-station coverage percentage. |
| 427 vessels in the late-1862 account | Includes ships afloat or progressing toward completion, purchases and departmental transfers. [N62 pp. 25-28] | Aggregate expansion, with readiness qualifier. |
| 3,268 guns and 340,036 tons | Accompany that 427-vessel inventory, including construction. [N62 pp. 26-28] | Not a deployed blockade-only total. |
| 153 seizures reported by the previous annual communication | Cumulative count at that earlier reporting point. [N62 p. 42] | Retain report date/perimeter. |
| 390 additional reported seizures; 543 cumulative | Vessels of all descriptions; some destroyed, most sent for adjudication. [N62 pp. 42-43] | Do not label them all ocean steamers or successfully condemned prizes. |
| 45 prize cases concluded; $554,176.51 ordered distributed | Judicial/distribution results, not gross cargo value of all captures. [N62 p. 43] | A distinct lag between seizure, adjudication and payment. |
| About 28,000 afloat/receiving-ship personnel and recruits; at least 12,000 yard/station workers | Rounded departmental totals. [N62 p. 43] | Labour and support establishment; not the number of blockade crews alone. |

[I] There is no compatible denominator here for **543 divided by total attempts**. Neither successful voyages, unique ships, attempted passages, captured tonnage nor cargo volume can be recovered from the seizure total alone. The proposed capture-rate checkpoint is therefore **unavailable**, rather than zero or a rate borrowed from later-war statistics.

Welles also distinguishes blockading an enemy-held port from regulating commerce in a port occupied by Union forces. His discussion proposes substituting domestic trade administration in occupied ports and releasing naval effort for other work. [N62 pp. 6-7] [I] Port capture changes access, customs administration and naval requirements; it should not be merely an additive increase in one universal blockade percentage.

[I] FY1862's recorded cotton weight is about **0.287% of FY1860**; this is a ratio within the United States official series, not a claim that precisely 99.713% of every possible Confederate export was stopped by ships. Changing jurisdiction, deliberate withholding, occupation, routing and reporting all matter. The sources' different bale totals must not be combined using an assumed fixed bale weight: retain pounds where reported and each source's stated bale convention.

The same Treasury appendix reports breadstuffs and provisions exported at **$45,271,850 in FY1860**, **$94,866,735 in FY1861**, and **$119,338,786 in FY1862**. These are dollar values, not wheat tonnage or shipments exclusively to Britain. [T62 p. 243; scan checked; column-flow OCR misaligns these rows] [I] This supplies an adjacent trade series against which to test assertions that European economic interests consisted only of Confederate cotton.

## Cotton shortage: inventory and unequal exposure

Adams estimates that America supplied **78-84%** of English raw-cotton imports before the war, while emphasizing that estimates vary. He describes a large prewar crop, stocks accumulated before the interruption, and overproduction of manufactured goods in 1858-60. The immediate effects on raw-cotton speculators, owners of accumulated goods, small mills and workers consequently differed. [A2 pp. 6-11]

[I] The dependence range is a range among the historical estimates Adams discusses, not a statistical confidence interval. A stock carried in from 1860-61 can delay the effects of import interruption; a fall in new arrivals should not empty every mill on the same day. No exact opening inventory in weeks by district is established by this note.

Adams's published poor-relief series provides several dated observations for the distressed Lancashire districts and adjacent areas:

| Observation window | People receiving public poor relief | Scope |
|---|---:|---|
| Normal reference | About 48,000 | Population of the defined districts about 2.3m. |
| First week November 1861 | 61,207 | Public relief, not total unemployed. |
| First week December 1861 | 71,593 | Same series. |
| March 1862 temporary high | About 113,000 | Later spring reduction precedes renewed increase in June. |
| December 1862 high | 284,418 | After the midterms, not a 1 November observation. |

Source: [A2 p. 12]. Adams discusses inconsistencies between the underlying official returns, especially Liverpool's inclusion and weather-sensitive port employment. His separate figure of approximately 236,000 on private relief in early December may overlap the public series. His printed phrase suggesting a combined total over 550,000 does not follow from those two numbers: [I] their arithmetical sum is 520,418 even **before** addressing duplication. No unduplicated combined total is asserted here. [A2 p. 12 and note]

He also reports an estimate of 500,000-600,000 manufacturing operatives in the district, with about 412,000 receiving some aid at the greatest distress, including people partly employed. Those figures concern a different population and measurement from public relief for all district residents. [A2 p. 13]

[I] A candidate foreign-pressure report should describe lost employment, household hardship and relief as distinct observations. It should not transform every person on relief into an unemployed factory operative, or confuse the distress maximum after the election with conditions before it.

Adams argues that serious distress did not produce a Lancashire demand compelling Cabinet intervention. He also rejects a simple thesis that Northern wheat mechanically kept Britain neutral, noting the lack of wheat references in the cabinet materials he studied. [A2 pp. 13-17] [I] His argument is valuable caution against one-variable explanations, not proof that food imports had no influence on any person, firm or government calculation.

## Confederate policy also restricted trade

Schwab records a **21 May 1861** act restricting cotton exports to Confederate seaports, an extension to other leading exports on 2 August, and later restrictions on supplying occupied areas. This is **not a statutory ban on every cotton shipment to Europe**: the seaport restriction and wider voluntary/state pressure had different forms. Cotton factors urged withholding; Louisiana prohibited further shipments to New Orleans, while South Carolina's attempted restriction drew Memminger's objection. [SCH pp. 250-251]

Opponents of withholding argued that cotton exports were needed to buy supplies. Schwab presents the competing arguments and the conflict between reducing foreign cotton supply and obtaining arms, food and remittances. [SCH pp. 251-252] [I] Do not make all export loss a Navy output or assume a unified Confederate trade policy without internal opposition.

State ventures are an adjacent institutional channel. Schwab describes North Carolina sending an agent abroad in 1862 and buying cotton for exchange abroad; his account of the subsequent trade and disputes is retrospective. [SCH pp. 256-258] [I] State purchases, private cargo, Confederate Treasury cotton and armament purchases require separate ownership and delivery evidence. A bale subscribed to a Richmond loan cannot simultaneously be treated as an independently available state cargo.

## The route after a successful passage

### Union licensed commerce was an administrative channel of its own

The Treasury report reproduces Chase's **28 August 1862** regulations and the accompanying War and Navy orders. They provide more specific evidence than an assumption that occupation immediately restored unrestricted trade. The regulations required permits for the enumerated destinations, allowed local Treasury agents to impose temporary restrictions subject to the Secretary's sanction, and required applications identifying merchandise, consignee, route and packages. They prohibited coin/bullion transport to the designated insurrectionary areas except for military purposes/orders or the Secretary's special licence, and prohibited buying cotton or other merchandise there in gold or silver. [T62 pp. 268-269, regulations I-V]

For ports reopened by presidential proclamation, the regulations provided consular licensing for vessels departing foreign ports and custom-house clearance at the reopened port. The War and Navy orders directed cooperation with Treasury-regulated trade while retaining exceptions for the safety or operations of the commands. They also limited property seizures to the specified circumstances and required reporting. Those are written instructions, not a finding that officers and traders universally complied. [T62 pp. 270-272, regulation XVIII and accompanying orders]

The appendix also reproduces the **20 May 1862** supplementary act. It authorized the Secretary to refuse a United States clearance when there was satisfactory reason to believe goods were intended for insurgent-controlled places, even when their ostensible destination was a foreign or domestic port. It allowed security for delivery to the permitted destination and measures against inland transport intended for insurgents. [T62 pp. 274-275, §§1-3]

[I] These are distinct questions: whether a port is under blockade; whether it is occupied and reopened; whether a particular trader/cargo has a permit; whether military operations permit movement; and whether a transaction provides coin to an opponent. A Treasury restriction on a United States clearance is not, by itself, a universal statement about the international legality of every neutral ship's capture. The same distinction prevents a cotton trade agreement from automatically restoring customs receipts or hard-currency finance to either government.

[I] This supplies a narrow holder-based implementation question for later work: when licensed commerce matters to a reported shortage or remittance, which officer authorized it, what was permitted, and what reached its consignee? It does not require a new player verb or a detailed merchant economy, and it does not establish a measured numerical benefit from granting permits.

[I] The smallest useful causal chain supported by these sources is:

1. Cotton or other export becomes available to a particular seller or government, rather than merely pledged. [M62 p. 3; SCH pp. 26-29]
2. It reaches a usable port and is loaded; restrictions and inland transport can prevent this. [SCH pp. 250-252, 273-274]
3. A runner passes the coast and reaches a transshipment/consignee market. [SCH pp. 237-239]
4. Proceeds or secured advances become spendable abroad; agents procure a cargo. [SCH pp. 28-29; M62 p. 10]
5. The return cargo passes the coast, is unloaded and moves inland. [SCH pp. 237-239, 273-274]

No stage is guaranteed by completion of the previous one. This is a proposed explanatory chain, not new engine state or a full merchant simulation. It can inform the existing holder-based money, munitions and rail mechanisms. A blockade's financial and munitions effects should not be counted twice as unrelated generic penalties.

The unresolved quantities are month-by-month cargo tonnage and composition, port-specific attempts and losses, costs of insurance and crews, actual public/private cargo division in 1862, and delivery delays from particular ports. The reported fleet, trade and price series constrain outcomes but do not identify all those mechanisms.
